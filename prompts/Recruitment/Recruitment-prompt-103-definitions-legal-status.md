# Prompt 103 — Does the Equality Act reasonable-adjustment duty apply at recruitment, before any job offer, including to an unsuccessful candidate?

**What this prompt is for.** Establish the legal starting point for the rest of the batch: whether the Equality Act 2010 reasonable-adjustment duty bites *before* a contract or offer exists; whether it still bites if the candidate is not appointed; whether a recruitment agency sits under a different or concurrent duty; whether application-stage format adjustments and interview-stage interpreter booking are two statutes or two examples of one duty; and whether DeafBlind access (tactile BSL, deafblind manual, braille/large print) is documented as a distinct legal object. ## Headline

The reasonable-adjustment duty **does apply at recruitment, before any offer or contract**. Equality Act 2010 s.39(1) covers “the arrangements A makes for deciding to whom to offer employment” and “by not offering B employment.” s.39(5) applies the s.20 duty to an employer. Schedule 8 para 5 names the interested disabled person, for the relevant matter “deciding to whom to offer employment,” as “a person who is, or has notified A that the person may be, an applicant for the employment.” That is the statute, not guidance.

The process duty is **not conditional on the candidate winning the job**. EHRC’s employer recruitment guidance (aligned with the Employment Code) says: if an applicant needs adjustments to take part in an interview or assessment, “you must make them” (if reasonable); separately, if after those adjustments they would not be the best person, “you do not have to offer it to them.” A failure to make a reasonable adjustment is discrimination under s.21 and **cannot be justified**. That is why an unsuccessful candidate can still have a live claim about the *process*, even though the employer is not required to appoint them. There is **no GB tribunal or EAT judgment found this pass** whose ratio is “employer refused a BSL interpreter for interview, candidate lost the job, claim succeeded.” Absence of a named GB interview-interpreter judgment is a gap, not a finding that the duty does not exist.

The duty **does not vanish because the role was advertised through an agency**. The end employer remains under s.39. A recruitment agency is an “employment service-provider” under s.55; s.56(2)(d)–(e) include finding employment for persons and supplying employers with persons to do work. s.55(6) applies a reasonable-adjustment duty to the service-provider (with a vocational-service carve-out that does not swallow ordinary agency placement). The Act’s own explanatory notes give the agency-website-accessibility example. EHRC recruitment guidance adds that an employer can be liable for an agent acting within authority. Concurrent duties are therefore the statutory structure. Who actually books and pays the interview interpreter is a fulfilment question for prompt 104, not a hole in the duty.

Application-stage and interview-stage adjustments are **one duty, two worked examples**. There is no separate “application SLA” and “interview SLA” in the Act or the Employment Code. ACAS splits the examples across pages (alternative application format on the recruitment-discrimination page, last updated 30 June 2026; “providing a sign language interpreter for someone who is deaf” on the interviewing page, same date) but both sit under “any stage of the recruitment process.”

DeafBlind-specific recruitment law is **thin**. The same s.20 auxiliary-aid requirement covers a deafblind communicator, tactile/hands-on signing, or deafblind manual in principle. The Services Code lists “sign language interpreter, lip-speaker or deaf-blind communicator” as an auxiliary-aid example — that Code is Part 3 services, not Part 5 work, so it is adjacent not controlling. BDF’s hearing-loss briefing (May 2024) names tactile / hands-on sign and deafblind manual as interpreter methods. EHRC recruitment guidance names Braille and large print as application-format examples. Home Office careers names braille, audio or BSL at application stage as something that employer has provided. **No named GB tribunal** was found that treats DeafBlind interview access as a distinct ratio. Deafblind UK’s interview page (2026) is candidate advice, not a legal standard.

**Hard-don't for anything public.** “Employers must provide a BSL interpreter for every Deaf interview” is not what the statute says. The statute says take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take, once the employer knows or could reasonably be expected to know of the disability and the likely disadvantage. Cost, practicability and effectiveness are reasonableness factors. AtW interview-support funding does not move the legal duty onto DWP.

## 103.1 Duty before offer, including an unsuccessful candidate

The gate finding is the statute, not a campaign slogan.

s.39 is titled “Employees and applicants.” The first prohibited act is discrimination “in the arrangements A makes for deciding to whom to offer employment.” That is the application form, the portal, the test, the interview panel, the agency longlist. The third is “by not offering B employment.” s.39(5) then applies the s.20 duty to that employer. Schedule 8 does not wait for a start date: the interested disabled person, for the matter “deciding to whom to offer employment,” is already “a person who is, or has notified A that the person may be, an applicant.”

Two consequences follow, and they must not be collapsed.

1. **Process.** Once the employer knows, or could reasonably be expected to know, that this applicant is disabled and that the arrangements (spoken interview, written timed test, inaccessible PDF form) put them at a more-than-minor-or-trivial disadvantage, the employer must take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take. A BSL interpreter, extra time, an alternative format, a different room, or a deafblind communicator are auxiliary-aid or PCP examples. ACAS puts the sign-language-interpreter example on the interviewing page. EHRC puts “provide an interpreter for a deaf job applicant during the interview” in the employer recruitment guidance. Neither document makes the step automatic regardless of reasonableness. Both treat it as the central worked example for a Deaf applicant.

2. **Appointment.** The same EHRC guidance is explicit that the employer does not have to offer the job to a disabled applicant who, *after* the process adjustments and after considering adjustments needed to do the work, would not be the best person. The process duty and the appointment decision are sequential. That is why an unsuccessful candidate is not locked out of a s.21 claim about the interview they never had a fair chance to take. It is also why “they didn’t get the job, so there was no duty” is not a reading the statute supports.

No GB employment tribunal or EAT judgment was found this pass that has that sequential split as its ratio on a BSL interview. The closest named story on the public web is **Wynn v Multipulse Electronics Ltd (Northern Ireland, 2007)** under the then NI Disability Discrimination Act: interview cancelled when no interpreter was arranged, application later rejected on health-and-safety grounds. Equality Commission NI still teaches it. It is **not** an Equality Act 2010 GB precedent and must not be filed as one.

Knowledge is the practical limit. Schedule 8 para 20 is more specific for applicants than the general “knew or ought to have known” slogan: the employer must know, or be reasonably expected to know, *both* that an interested disabled person is or may be an applicant *and* that they have a disability likely to put them at the relevant disadvantage. A standard “do you need an adjustment for this process?” box is how most employers generate that knowledge without falling into the s.60 trap (prompt 105). If the candidate never asks and the disadvantage is not obvious, the duty may not arise. If they ask for an interpreter, knowledge is no longer the issue.

## 103.2 Agency versus direct advertisement

Two duty-holders can sit on the same application.

- The **end employer** is under s.39 whether they advertised on their own site or through an agency.
- The **agency** is an employment service-provider under s.55. Finding candidates and supplying them to a hirer are the s.56(2)(d)–(e) services. The reasonable-adjustment duty in s.55(6) applies to those services. The vocational-service exception in s.55(6)–(7) is a different object (mainly training/guidance) and does not read as a general agency opt-out.

The Act’s own notes use an accessible agency website as the example. EHRC recruitment guidance adds the agency-as-agent point: the employer can be liable for what the agency does inside its authority, so the contract and the briefing matter. BDF’s hearing-loss briefing tells employers who use agencies to ask for evidence that the agency itself makes adjustments.

What the statute does **not** do is publish a booking protocol. It does not say “the agency always books,” “the employer always pays,” or “AtW always pays.” Those are prompt 104 and prompt 108 cells. Treating the agency as a legal vacuum is the error this gate is there to stop.

s.41 (contract workers / principal) is the *in-post* split already covered in prompt 97. It is the wrong section for a candidate who has not yet been supplied.

## 103.3 Application-stage formats versus interview-stage interpreters

EHRC and ACAS document both stages. They do not enact two duties.

Typical application-stage examples in the official stack: form or pack in large print, Braille, audio or electronic format; not requiring a voice-only telephone request for a pack (RNID 2017 factsheet still uses that as its “likely unlawful advert” illustration); extra time or an alternative method for an online test.

Typical interview-stage examples: BSL interpreter; lipspeaker; speech-to-text; hearing loop; accessible room; extra time; questions in advance; lighting and sightlines for signing or lipreading.

The legal test at both stages is the same: substantial disadvantage from a PCP, physical feature or missing auxiliary aid, knowledge, reasonableness. A recruitment portal that only offers a single “reasonable adjustments” tick-box is a design choice, not a statutory category. Whether that box is capable of capturing a DeafBlind request for tactile interpretation plus braille materials is a fulfilment/practice question, not a second statute.

## 103.4 DeafBlind candidates

The Act does not say “DeafBlind.” It says disabled person, substantial disadvantage, auxiliary aid.

What exists:

- Services Code auxiliary-aid list includes a “deaf-blind communicator.” Wrong Part of the Act for an employment claim, useful as Commission vocabulary.
- BDF names the methods: tactile / hands-on sign; deafblind manual.
- EHRC recruitment guidance already treats Braille and large print as likely-reasonable application formats.
- Home Office careers publishes braille / audio / BSL at application and BSL or lipspeaker at interview as things that employer has actually provided.
- Deafblind UK and Sense publish candidate- or employer-facing advice. Neither is a Code. Neither cites a named GB recruitment judgment.

What does not exist, this pass:

- A statutory or Employment-Code chapter on DeafBlind recruitment.
- A named GB tribunal on tactile BSL, deafblind manual, or braille application packs.
- A published national protocol that a standard adjustments checkbox must include those options.

Do not upgrade charity advice into a legal requirement. Do not collapse DeafBlind access into “just book a BSL interpreter.” The methods are different; the duty-holder and the test are the same.

## 103.5 What is unpublished or contested

- **No GB interview-interpreter judgment found.** The legal structure is clear; the case-law illustration for this exact fact pattern is not. Wynn is NI 2007. Rimmer is JCP. Iqbal is in-post. Do not plug the gap with any of them.
- **Reasonableness of a same-week interpreter booking** is fact-specific. BDF notes language-support professionals are often booked two to four weeks ahead. That is a market fact, not a statutory notice period.
- **AtW interview support versus employer cost** is a live operational split, not a legal transfer of the s.20 duty. Scope and the Oxford careers page both state that AtW does not replace the employer duty. Prompt 104 takes the process.
- **Who books when an agency sits in the middle** is not written down in a national REC or EHRC protocol found this pass. Prompt 108.
- **DeafBlind recruitment practice** is practitioner text only.

International material that must not fill a UK cell: the EEOC ADA example of an employer cancelling a deaf applicant’s interview rather than booking an interpreter. Same fact pattern, different statute.
