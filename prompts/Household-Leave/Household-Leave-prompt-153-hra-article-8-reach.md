## Headline

**The originating framing overstates private-sector reach.** Article 8 is a Convention right given further effect by the Human Rights Act 1998. It does **not** create a free-standing employment-tribunal claim against an ordinary private employer. Employment tribunals have **no jurisdiction** to hear a bare HRA claim. What a private-sector employee can get is this, and no more: if they already have another statutory claim (almost always unfair dismissal under ERA 1996 s.98, sometimes an Equality Act claim), the tribunal must, so far as it is possible to do so, read that legislation compatibly with Article 8 (HRA s.3), and the tribunal itself must not act incompatibly because it is a public authority (HRA s.6(3)(a)). *X v Y* [2004] EWCA Civ 662, [2004] ICR 1634 is the named Court of Appeal authority for that structure. Mummery LJ said, in terms, that s.6 “does not… give the applicant any cause of action under the HRA against a respondent which is not a public authority.”

A **core public-authority employer** (NHS trust, government department, local authority acting as employer) is bound directly by s.6. Even then, Article 8 is a qualified right. Interference is permitted under Article 8(2) if it is in accordance with the law, pursues a listed legitimate aim, and is necessary and proportionate. *Turner v East Midlands Trains Ltd* [2012] EWCA Civ 1470 held that the ordinary unfair-dismissal “band of reasonable responses” test is itself compatible with Article 8; the band flexes where the consequences of dismissal are particularly serious. That is not a family-leave case, and it is not a finding that Article 8 adds a new leave entitlement.

**No named GB tribunal, EAT or appellate decision was found this pass whose ratio is that Article 8 required an employer to grant flexible working, carer’s leave, parental leave, or time off for dependants, or that refusal of those rights in order to meet family caring responsibilities breached Article 8.** The cases that look closest on the facts — *Dobson v North Cumbria Integrated Care NHS FT* (community nurse, three children, two disabled; weekend-rostering PCP) — were decided as **Equality Act indirect sex discrimination**, not as Article 8. *Ghebrehiwt v Wilson James* is a s.57A scope case. *Thompson v Manors*, *British Airways v Starmer*, *Hedger v British Deaf Association* are flexible-working / sex-discrimination cases. Recoding them as Article 8 authorities would be a citation error.

Hybrid status (s.6(3)(b)) is narrow. *YL v Birmingham City Council* [2007] UKHL 27 held that a private care home providing contracted residential care was **not** exercising a function of a public nature. Care Act 2014 s.73 later deemed specified care providers to be public authorities for HRA purposes when providing local-authority-arranged or -funded care — that is a *resident’s* Article 8 home/care argument, not an *employee’s* family-leave argument against a private employer. Do not transfer it.

ACAS’s flexible-working Code and leave pages do **not** mention Article 8. The live toolkit in 150–151 is the four statutory rights plus the Equality Act. Article 8 is not printed on those pages as a fifth form to fill in.

**Hard-don’t for anything public.** Do not print that Article 8 “protects private-sector workers” as if it were a direct duty. Do not print that a named refusal of flexible working or leave “breaches Article 8.” Do not treat *X v Y* as a family-caring case — it is a private-life / public-sex-act unfair-dismissal case that happens to contain the horizontal-effect framework.

## 153.1 The three machines, kept apart

**Machine A — s.6 direct duty.** Binds a core public authority (and a hybrid body only while it is doing a public function, and not when the act is private). An NHS trust refusing a nurse’s hours request is inside Machine A. A high-street retailer is not. A private care-home *employer* is not made a public authority for employment purposes by Care Act s.73, which is about the provision of arranged care to a service user.

**Machine B — s.3 interpretive duty.** Binds the tribunal in every case, public or private. It can only operate on existing legislation. It cannot invent a household-stacking rule that Parliament did not write (prompt 150). *Ghaidan* allows reading-in only where that does not go against the grain. The grain of s.80G is eight business grounds and a right to *request*. The grain of s.57A is a reasonable amount of emergency time off, not ongoing personal care (*Qua*; *Ghebrehiwt*).

**Machine C — the tribunal’s own s.6 duty.** Reinforces Machine B. It does not convert the employer into a public authority.

The originating table treated Machines A–C as one sentence. They are not.

## 153.2 What the named cases are actually about

*X v Y* is the horizontal-effect case. The facts are a caution for a public sexual offence and a charity working with young offenders. Family caring is not in the ratio. Using the case for its framework is required; using it as a caring-leave precedent is a miss.

*Turner* is a ticket-fraud dismissal and a reputation argument. It matters because it closes the door on “read a stricter test into s.98.” It does not open a door onto leave.

*Dobson* is the fact-pattern closest to this batch (disabled children; hours; public employer) and the courts still used the Equality Act. That is the live hook for a public- or private-sector carer, not Article 8.

## 153.3 What this means for 154–156

Prompt 154 (AtW bridge) should not lean on Article 8 as a funding duty. Prompt 156 (lived experience) should not treat “Article 8 protects family life at work” as a campaign fact. The accurate public sentence is: public-authority employers are directly bound by s.6; private-sector workers cannot sue on Article 8 alone; the four statutory rights plus the Equality Act are the operable toolkit; household stacking is still unpublished (150).
