# Grok research prompt batch — Prompts 78–80: Video Relay Service & Telecoms Equity (Home/Office/Travel)

Numbered per the project's master prompt index (`prompts/SuperGrok_Research_Prompt_Pack.md` in the DeafCommunityMapping repo — confirmed next free number was **78** at time of writing, per the index's own "⚠️ NEXT PROMPT NUMBER" line; **update the index and that line in the same edit that adds this batch**).

**Why this batch exists.** VRS & Telecoms Equity is the single highest-figure domain on the dossier dashboard (64 figures currently logged) but, unlike AIS, HMRC, ATW/PIP, BSL Act, CODA, and Deaf-older, it has never had a dedicated Grok sourcing/gap-check pass — every figure on that domain page so far has gone in without the same "cite it, then verify the citation actually says that" discipline applied everywhere else in this project. That is a coverage-to-scrutiny mismatch worth closing before the number of figures grows further. The core question this batch exists to pin down: why a Deaf BSL user cannot pick up a phone and dial any UK number the way a hearing person can, and what specifically breaks down across the per-organisation VRS contract model, third-party refusal, and the home/office/travel access gap.

**Checked against existing prompts first.** No existing batch in this project's index runs a dedicated pass on VRS or telecoms relay provision. Access to Work (prompts 52–54, and B-series in `SUPERGROK_PROMPTS.md`) touches interpreter funding caps for employment generally but does not address telecoms/VRS specifically. Jobcentre Plus (27–33) covers on-site interpreter booking at Jobcentre appointments, not remote VRS calling capability. This is the first dedicated batch on VRS/telecoms as its own domain. Flag clearly anywhere the evidence is thin, unpublished, or anecdotal only — do not fill a gap with a plausible-sounding generalisation, and do not import the US FCC TRS model's numbers or structure as if they describe the UK system.

**78. The per-organisation VRS contract model and third-party refusal**

As a UK telecoms and disability-access policy researcher, using Ofcom's relay service guidance and annual relay reports, the Equality Act 2010 reasonable-adjustment duty, the Communications Act 2003, and any published contracts, tenders, or terms of service from UK VRS providers (e.g. SignVideo, InterpreterNow, Sign Solutions, Sign Live), establish exactly how UK VRS provision is currently structured and why it does not work like a phone number tied to the Deaf user. Cover: (1) confirm whether VRS access is typically purchased per-organisation (an NHS trust, a bank, an employer buys a contract or seat licence) rather than provided as a portable service that travels with the Deaf user and lets them reach any number — cite the contract/procurement model directly rather than assuming it; (2) any documented Ofcom finding, tribunal case, or reported incident of a third party (a business, public body, or call recipient) refusing to accept or engage with a VRS-relayed call, and what legal exposure, if any, that refusal creates under the Equality Act reasonable-adjustment duty — flag clearly if no such case has actually been adjudicated rather than assuming one exists; (3) how the always-available, centrally funded 999 BSL emergency relay service (999 BSL) is funded and governed compared with non-emergency VRS provision, and whether any Ofcom document explains why that funding model has not been extended to non-emergency calling; (4) any Ofcom consultation, industry body (e.g. UK Council on Deafness), or government review that has explicitly examined the per-organisation contract model as a structural barrier, with what it recommended, if anything. For each finding, give the source title, publishing body, date, and direct URL. Do not present a plausible-sounding structural explanation as fact unless a named regulator, provider, or contract source actually says so — flag as "no reliable figure found" if the mechanics are inferred rather than documented.

**79. The home/office/travel access gap — where VRS breaks down by location and context**

As a researcher specialising in Deaf accessibility and assistive technology, using RNID, SignHealth, British Deaf Association, and Action on Hearing Loss publications, Access to Work's own published guidance and statistics, Ofcom's Digital Exclusion / accessibility research, and any UK academic or grey-literature research on Deaf telecoms use, establish precisely where and why VRS provision breaks down across three contexts: home, workplace, and travel/mobile use. Cover: (1) home use — what proportion of Deaf BSL users have working VRS access from a home broadband/mobile connection, what it costs (app subscription, data/bandwidth requirement), and whether any UK universal service obligation (broadband USO under the Digital Economy Act 2017, or Ofcom's General Conditions) currently covers or excludes VRS-grade connectivity; (2) workplace use — how Access to Work funds VRS or interpreter provision for employed Deaf people, any documented funding cap, approval delay, or employer non-compliance that leaves a Deaf employee unable to make or receive calls colleagues make freely, and whether VRS access funded through Access to Work is portable if the person changes employer (cross-reference but do not duplicate prompts 52–54's Access to Work funding-cap findings — this prompt is specifically about the telecoms/calling consequence, not the funding mechanism itself); (3) travel/mobile use — what documented options (if any) exist for a Deaf BSL user needing to make an unplanned call while away from a pre-arranged VRS contract (contacting a GP, reporting to police, a delayed-train or taxi/delivery query), and whether any mobile network operator, app, or Ofcom-monitored service offers something approximating universal reachability; (4) any study or survey quantifying the practical gap between the number of organisations/numbers a hearing person can reach by phone versus what a Deaf BSL user can reach via VRS. For each source, give title, publishing body/organisation, date, and URL, and flag clearly wherever a figure is anecdotal, a single case, or unquantified rather than a measured statistic.

**80. International comparators — universal or portable relay service models**

As a comparative telecoms-policy researcher, using the US FCC's Telecommunications Relay Service (TRS) rules and Interstate TRS Fund structure, EU accessibility law (including the European Accessibility Act), and telecoms-regulator relay frameworks in Canada (CRTC), Australia (ACMA's National Relay Service), and New Zealand, identify systems where video relay or an equivalent sign-language relay service is provided as a universal, user-tied service rather than a per-organisation contract, and assess honestly what each model actually delivers rather than what it states as policy. Cover: (1) how the US VRS model works in practice — centrally funded via the Interstate TRS Fund, free at point of use to the registered Deaf user, any registered user able to call any US phone number and be called back via a dedicated ten-digit or toll-free VRS number — and what oversight/cost-control structure (FCC certification of providers, per-minute compensation rates) makes this financially sustainable; (2) any documented complaint, fraud problem (VRS has a documented history of billing-fraud scandals in the US), or cost-control failure in the US model, so the comparison is balanced rather than an endorsement; (3) how Australia's National Relay Service or the EU accessibility framework compare, and whether either is closer to a "dial any number, be reached at any number" model than the UK's current per-organisation-contract system, or whether on closer inspection they turn out to be narrower than they first appear; (4) any UK-specific Ofcom consultation, policy paper, or Parliamentary question that has explicitly considered adopting a universal or portable VRS funding model for the UK, and what became of it — flag clearly if no such consideration has been documented rather than assuming one must exist given the scale of the US precedent. For each source, give title, author/institution, country, publication date, and URL, and note which specific features, if any, would most directly close the UK's home/office/travel gap identified in prompt 79 if adopted, without asserting that adoption is straightforward or already under way.

### Notes for this batch specifically

- Prompt 78 is the gate for the whole batch — do not let Grok assert the per-organisation contract model, the 999 BSL funding contrast, or a third-party-refusal legal exposure as settled fact unless a named contract, regulator document, or adjudicated case actually says so. This project's DAGM decision record already establishes the rule that "not found at URL on DATE, sourced, dated" is the safe unit — not a confident structural claim about how an industry works.
- Prompt 79 should keep the three contexts (home/office/travel) clearly separated in the findings table rather than blended into one "access gap" figure — the funding mechanism, the cost driver, and the fix differ by context, and collapsing them would repeat the CODA batch's warning about collapsing distinct groups into one number.
- Prompt 80 is the most likely to return US-only, aspirational-sounding claims — apply the same discipline as prompt 76 (CODA international comparative) and the BSL Act batch's non-ministerial provision-mapping prompt: report what a jurisdiction's regulator or fund actually pays out and enforces, not what its policy documents say it aspires to.
- As with every prior batch, flag confidence per finding rather than presenting anything as settled — this project's standard is "sourced and dated," not "asserted."
- A fourth prompt (lived experience / advocacy case for reform, matching the pattern closed out in every other batch — AIS 37, CODA 77, Deaf-older 71, EOL prompt 4) was deliberately held back from this batch given current usage constraints. If usage allows a follow-up, that would be prompt 81, run and reviewed as its own pass rather than folded into this file after the fact.
