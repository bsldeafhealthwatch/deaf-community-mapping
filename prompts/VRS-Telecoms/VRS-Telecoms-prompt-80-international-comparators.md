# Prompt 80 — International comparators: universal or portable relay models

**Master index prompt 80.** Third file in the VRS & Telecoms Equity batch (prompts 78–80). Compiled 10 September 2026. Read prompts 78 and 79 first. This file tests what other jurisdictions actually pay for and enforce — not what their policy documents aspire to.

**Dossier rule.** Stay unbiased. Do not import a US FCC number as if it described the UK. Do not treat “functionally equivalent” language as evidence that a Deaf user in that country can in fact dial any number at midnight from a train. Prompt 76 (CODA international) and prompt 64 (BSL Act non-ministerial map) are the method cousins: report the fund, the hours, the reachable set, and the documented failures.

**What would close the UK gaps in prompt 79,** if a foreign model actually delivered them:

- a user-tied credential that works from any broadband or mobile connection (home);
- funding that is not reset when the employer changes (workplace);
- the ability to place and receive a call to an unpublished number while away from a partner venue (travel).

---

## Headline

Two jurisdictions operate a **user-tied, any-number video relay** paid from an industry fund: the **United States** (FCC TRS Fund / VRS) and **Canada** (CRTC / CAV / National Contribution Fund). A registered user receives a ten-digit number, can dial ordinary telephone numbers, and can be called back. The service is free at the point of use. That is the model that would close the UK home / office / travel gaps identified in prompt 79. It is also the model with a documented billing-fraud history in the US, and it is expensive: the FCC’s FY 2025–26 Internet-based TRS contribution factor is 2.086 percent of contributors’ end-user revenues; VRS alone is budgeted at about **$661 million** for that fund year.

**Australia’s National Relay Service** and **New Zealand Relay** are national, levy-funded, and user-tied — closer to “dial a number” than the UK commercial directory — but on inspection they are **not 24/7 video**. Australia’s published Video Relay hours have been weekday daytime plus a Saturday morning window; Deaf Australia was still campaigning for 24/7 in 2026, and the May 2026 federal budget added A$5 million a year for three years “in preparation for 24/7 VRS,” which is an admission that 24/7 is not yet the delivered service. NZ’s Video Interpreting Service is 08:00–20:00 weekdays, shorter at weekends.

The **European Accessibility Act** (Directive 2019/882) requires electronic-communications providers to offer real-time text and, where they offer video, total conversation, and to deliver synchronised total conversation into 112. It does **not** create a US-style VRS compensation fund. EENA’s 2024 relay paper records “no unified legal and technical requirements” for relay across the EU and “very limited or non-existent services in many countries.” Ofcom’s 2019–21 emergency-video-relay work was the UK implementation of the EECC emergency limb, not an EAA VRS fund.

No UK Ofcom consultation, DSIT paper or Parliamentary answer was found that adopts, costs, or rejects a TRS-Fund-style universal VRS for non-emergency calling. The 2012 relay review promised to “examine the case”; the 2019 EECC consultation explicitly declined to mandate non-emergency video relay; *Locked Out* (27 November 2025) recommends a national 24/7 VRS for England and Wales without specifying an industry-fund design. Contact Scotland BSL remains the only UK everyday approximation of the North American model.

## 80.1 United States — what the TRS Fund actually is

Section 225 of the Communications Act requires functionally equivalent telephone service for people with hearing or speech disabilities. VRS is one TRS product. Providers that the FCC has certified bill the Interstate TRS Fund per compensated minute. Carriers and other contributors pay into the Fund on a percentage of end-user revenues. The user does not buy a seat from the bank they are calling.

That is the structural opposite of SignVideo’s SignDirectory.

It is also not cheap and not ungoverned. FY 2025–26 VRS is a **$661 million** line inside a **$1.48 billion** net Fund requirement. Rates are tiered so that scale is cheaper per minute. The Bureau this year marked small-provider demand projections down by about 29 percent. The Code of Federal Regulations now reads like a fraud manual — idle-call cut-offs, written third-party contracts, call-centre inventories, international-IP limits — because VRS billing fraud is a documented US problem. This pass does not reprint an unverified historic dollar total; the existence of the control apparatus is the sourced fact.

Features that would close UK gaps: the 10-digit number (home inbound + travel), the industry fund (workplace portability — the payer is not the employer), 24/7. Features that would not travel cleanly: US numbering, US fraud economics, and a contribution factor of two percent of end-user revenues.

## 80.2 Canada — the same architecture at a smaller envelope

Canada copied the user-tied number and the industry fund, then put a single administrator (CAV) in charge rather than a multi-provider compensation market. Approved spend is in the mid-C$30 millions, not the mid-US$600 millions. Users can keep a second account so work and home traffic do not share a number — an implicit admission that workplace calling is part of the design, not an Access-to-Work afterthought.

The dedicated-number list for banks and the CRA is the interesting overlay. Even in a system where any number is dialable, large organisations still publish a VRS-specific incoming line to stop IVR hell and hang-ups. That is evidence that “dial any number” and “the called party knows what a relay call is” are different problems. The UK commercial directory only solves the second problem, and only for organisations that have paid.

## 80.3 Australia and New Zealand — national, but not the US hours

Do not write that Australia “has universal VRS” without the hours. The NRS is a genuine national, levy-funded, user-tied service. Video Relay inside it has been a daytime product. Deaf Australia’s May 2026 budget note — extra A$5 million a year “in preparation for 24/7 VRS” — is the sourced statement that 24/7 is planned, not delivered.

New Zealand is the same shape at a smaller scale: TDL plus Crown money, Concentrix as provider, Video Interpreting on Teams, **closed overnight**. MBIE’s live page is explicit about the weekday 08:00–20:00 window.

Both countries are closer to Contact Scotland than to SignDirectory. Neither is closer to FCC VRS on hours. For the UK travel gap (unplanned evening/weekend call), an Australian-style national service with weekday hours would not be enough.

## 80.4 The EU framework is not a VRS fund

The European Accessibility Act’s electronic-communications schedule is about **how a voice/video call is carried** (real-time text, total conversation) and about **112**. It is not a compensation mechanism that pays interpreters to bridge BSL and a high-street bank. EECC Article 85(4) leaves relay services to national conditions. EENA records the result: a patchwork, some 24/7 national services, many thin or resident-only.

The UK already used the EECC emergency limb to build 999 BSL. Treating the EAA as an impending UK universal VRS would be a citation error. Ireland’s 2025 ComReg measures are the nearest English-language transposition on this pass: RTT, total conversation, 112 — still not a TRS Fund.

## 80.5 Has the UK looked at adopting this?

Sourced trail, in order:

1. Ofcom, October 2012 — NGTR mandated; video-relay review promised; DCMS voluntary-provision initiative named.
2. DCMS voluntary initiative — no closing statement found. The live residue is the commercial directory.
3. Ofcom, December 2019 — emergency video relay proposed; non-emergency video relay explicitly not proposed.
4. Ofcom, June 2021 / June 2022 — 999 BSL becomes a General Condition.
5. Ofcom, May 2025 — emergency service evaluated; non-emergency question not reopened.
6. BSL Advisory Board, *Locked Out*, 27 November 2025 — recommends national 24/7 VRS for England and Wales. Does not specify an industry-fund design or a 10-digit numbering plan.
7. Contact Scotland retender, 2025 — Scotland keeps the user-tied everyday model, now with Sign Solutions.

No sourced HMT costing, Ofcom CBA, or ministerial decision was found that says “we have considered a TRS Fund and rejected it because X.” The documented UK position is narrower: emergency equivalence was judged proportionate; everyday equivalence was left to organisations, AtW, and (in Scotland) a government contract.

## Do-not-print list

- Do not print the US model as “free” without the carrier contribution factor and the $661 million VRS line.
- Do not print a US fraud-dollar total that was not opened from an FCC enforcement order on this pass.
- Do not print Australia or New Zealand as 24/7 VRS.
- Do not print the EAA as a VRS-fund statute.
- Do not print “the UK has considered and rejected the US model.” The sourced trail is: emergency mandated, everyday left to the market plus Scotland.
- Do not assert that adopting a TRS Fund in the UK would be straightforward. Numbering, fraud controls, interpreter supply and the contribution base are all open design questions. This file maps features; it does not run a CBA.
