## Headline

National VRS systems mapped in prompt 80 are **resident products**. A UK BSL tourist is an outsider to all four.

- **United States.** FCC VRS compensation and the User Registration Database are built for eligible **US** users. International-travel rules (pre-departure notice, calls only to/from the United States, one-year cap) are for *US residents going out*, not for a UK visitor coming in. Identity rules want an SSN or alternative ID **plus documentation that the user is residing in the United States**. Do not tell a Croydon BSL user to “just open Sorenson in New York.”
- **Canada.** CAV user agreement, 27 June 2018, still live in the published PDF: “**Only Canadian citizens or residents of Canada** who (a) are Deaf, hard of hearing, or speech impaired, and (b) use American Sign Language and/or Langue des signes québécoise are eligible to register.” Language is ASL/LSQ, not BSL.
- **Australia.** NRS Video Relay is **Auslan**, weekday-heavy hours, not 24/7. Registration wants a name-and-**address** support document (driver licence or utility bill). Overseas-call pages describe what *registered NRS users temporarily abroad* can do to call **Australia** — the mirror of the FCC outbound rule, not a tourist on-ramp.
- **New Zealand.** Video Interpreting Service is **NZSL**. The “can I use NZ Relay overseas?” FAQ assumes a user who downloaded the NZ app **before leaving New Zealand**. No visitor-registration page found.

**999 BSL from foreign soil still only reaches a UK 999 room** (Ofcom, prompt 84). The destination emergency number is a different product. US 911 via VRS is for registered VRS users. Australia 000 via NRS is for NRS users. EU 112 sign-language access is a patchwork: EENA’s 2026 accessibility report card counts sign-language emergency paths in **13 of 29** surveyed countries. No source found that those paths accept **BSL** or International Sign as of right for a roaming UK visitor.

AIS does not travel. GHIC/EHIC buys state healthcare “on the same basis as a resident.” That is a charging sentence, not a BSL interpreter. FCDO hospital guidance offers lists of **English-speaking** interpreters.

No sourced foreign-airport BSL or International Sign QR comparable to Heathrow Convo / Manchester SignLive was found on this pass. Regulation (EC) 1107/2006 in the EU is the same PRM floor as the UK-retained text — assistance through the terminal, not a language roster.

Lived-experience harvest is thin. RNID’s March 2026 Matt blog is sudden hearing loss and special-assistance tick-boxes, not a BSL emergency abroad. No named UK BSL first-person of a failed 112/911/000 VRS registration was found. Do not generalise from that absence to a slogan.

## 164.1 Registration barriers

The four user-tied systems in prompt 80 all have a **who-is-this-for** sentence. Read those sentences before anyone drafts a “use the local VRS” tip.

Canada is the cleanest exclusion: citizen or resident, **and** ASL or LSQ. A UK BSL user fails both tests on the published agreement.

The United States is the most often misunderstood. FCC material about “using VRS abroad” is written for Americans who already have a ten-digit VRS number and who must tell Sorenson or their default provider that they are leaving. The SSN waiver exists precisely because some people (including visitors) have no SSN — and the same order then requires **US residence documentation**. That is not a tourist kiosk.

Australia and New Zealand publish generous “use us while you are overseas” pages. In both cases the subject of the sentence is **their** registered user. NRS registration wants an address document that matches. NZ’s overseas FAQ tells the user to download the app *before leaving New Zealand* and then talks about **text** relay. Video in both countries is the national sign language, on limited hours.

## 164.2 Emergency calling abroad

Two different 999s get collapsed in casual advice. Do not collapse them.

1. **999 BSL from foreign soil** → UK control room. Keep the app for a UK-side emergency. emergencySMS does not roam.
2. **Local emergency number** → local PSAP. US 911-via-VRS wants a registered VRS user. Australia 000-via-NRS is built for NRS users; video is Auslan and not 24/7. NZ 111 video is NZSL on VIS hours. EU 112 sign-language access exists in a minority of countries and is described by EENA as unequal; no source opened here says a roaming UK BSL user is in scope.

Hearing companion, local text-to-emergency where it exists, and (in the EU) whatever RTT the visited member state has actually switched on are the residual paths. International Sign as an accepted emergency language: **no reliable figure found.**

## 164.3 Healthcare abroad

AIS is an NHS / English adult-social-care information standard. It does not follow the passport.

GHIC/EHIC is “same basis as a resident” for **state** healthcare charging. A resident of France who is Deaf is owed whatever France’s own disability and healthcare rules owe that resident — usually LSF, not BSL. The UK official stack (FCDO hospital page, Travel Aware BSL film, travel checklist) points at English-speaking interpreter lists and travel insurance. Check the policy for medical interpreting. Do not print “GHIC includes a BSL interpreter.”

## 164.4 Airports and transit abroad

Heathrow Convo QR and Manchester SignLive are UK-operator products. This pass did not find a named foreign peer. EU 1107 will get a Deaf passenger to the gate if they asked for assistance; it will not put an International Sign interpreter on the desk. UK airline/tour-operator VRS listings (BA, easyJet, Virgin, Jet2, Aer Lingus, Ryanair, TUI) still work only as **calls back into that company’s UK or listed contact centre**, and only inside published hours, and only in BSL (Ryanair also lists ISL). They do not become a Paris hospital line.
