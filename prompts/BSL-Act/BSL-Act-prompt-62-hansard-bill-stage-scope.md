## Headline

Nobody in either House tabled, moved, or debated an amendment that would have put non-ministerial departments on the Schedule. HMRC, the Charity Commission, HM Land Registry, the CPS, Ofsted and the Food Standards Agency are not named in the Second Reading, Committee, Report, Third Reading or Lords debates located for this pass.

That is not the same as “the limit was hidden.” Three official documents state the limit in plain words:

1. **Section 2(8)** of the Act confines “relevant government department” to a “United Kingdom ministerial government department listed in the Schedule.”
2. The **Explanatory Notes** say the section 2(9) regulation power “is intended to only be used where there are ‘machinery of government’ changes.”
3. The **Delegated Powers Memorandum** (22 March 2022) goes further and is the closest thing to an on-the-record explanation of the non-ministerial exclusion: the power “has been designed so that it could not be exercised to add a non-ministerial department or other public body to the list.”

The only amendment made at Committee was Rosie Cooper’s **Amendment 1 to the long title**, which *aligned the title with the already-rewritten Bill* by inserting the words “ministerial government departments.” The original long title had spoken of a BSL Council and of “public bodies” having regard to principles. That wider framing was dropped before Committee, as a Private Member’s Bill constraint (no new public expenditure; no statutory council), not after a floor fight about HMRC.

Post-enactment annual reports then restated the same cut in operational language: only “core” ministerial departments report; agencies, NDPBs and other ALBs are “not in scope” and are “encouraged” to use BSL. That is implementation commentary, not a 2021–22 amendment debate.

## 62.1 What the enacted text actually does

**Long title (as enacted).** “An Act to recognise British Sign Language as a language of England, Wales and Scotland; to require the Secretary of State to report on the promotion and facilitation of the use of British Sign Language by ministerial government departments; and to require guidance to be issued in relation to British Sign Language.”

**Section 2(2).** A British Sign Language report describes what each *relevant government department* has done to promote or facilitate BSL in its communications with the public.

**Section 2(8).** “Relevant government department” means a United Kingdom *ministerial* government department listed in the Schedule.

**Section 2(9)–(10).** The Secretary of State may by negative-procedure regulations amend the Schedule so as to add, remove or vary an entry *in consequence of* a change to the name of a United Kingdom ministerial government department, or a change in the way ministerial government departments are organised. That is the machinery-of-government power identified in the HMRC brief (prompt 55 / batch 55–61).

**Section 2(3)–(4) and the Notes.** The report is about public announcements, plans, strategies, consultations, press conferences, social media and websites. It is **not** intended to capture individual front-line BSL interpreter requests as Equality Act reasonable adjustments (Jobcentres, courts, ports, hospitals, etc.), nor personal ministerial correspondence.

**Schedule (current, after SI 2023/424 machinery-of-government edits).** Attorney General’s Office; Cabinet Office; Department for Business and Trade; Department for Culture, Media and Sport; Department for Education; Department for Energy Security and Net Zero; Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs; Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government; Department for Science, Innovation and Technology; Department for Transport; Department for Work and Pensions; Department of Health and Social Care; Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office; His Majesty’s Treasury; Home Office; Ministry of Defence; Ministry of Justice; Office of the Leader of the House of Commons; Office of the Leader of the House of Lords; Office of the Secretary of State for Scotland; Office of the Secretary of State for Wales; Office of the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland.

HMRC is **not** on that list. His Majesty’s Treasury **is**. The reporting duty therefore attaches to Treasury *departmental communications*, not to HMRC’s operational tax channels.

## 62.3 Question (1) — did anyone raise which departments would be covered, and were NMDs named?

**Finding: the Schedule was mentioned; named non-ministerial departments were not.**

### What *was* said

**Chloe Smith, Minister for Disabled People, Commons Second Reading, 28 January 2022 (vol. 707 col. 1257):**

> “Through the Bill, the Secretary of State for Work and Pensions will regularly report on what each relevant Department—those detailed in the schedule of the Bill—has done to promote or facilitate the use of BSL in their communications with the public. Part 2 sets that out in more detail. Such communications could include public announcements, the publication of any plan, strategy or consultation document, or any activities promoting the work of that Department, for example, press conferences.”

Same speech: the Bill “will also require the DWP to report on information supplied by ministerial Departments regarding their use of BSL.”

**Rosie Cooper, Commons Second Reading (vol. 707 col. 1235):**

> “I hope that the required BSL report set out in clause 2 spurs on all Departments to meet the basic need to provide accessible information to the deaf community.”

**Rosie Cooper, Commons Third Reading, 18 March 2022 (vol. 710):** clause 2 would mean that “any public announcement made without an interpreter or any White Paper released without BSL translation will be reported to the Secretary of State, and that failure will be published for everyone to see.” Departments would be “held to account.” She did not list the Schedule bodies, and she did not name HMRC or any other NMD.

**CBP-9446 on Committee.** Rosie Cooper described the reporting duty as a “valuable tool” for the public to hold the government, “and public bodies such as schools and hospitals,” to account. That sentence is looser than the statute. Schools and hospitals are not Schedule bodies. The Library is recording her political hope for the *effect* of published reports, not an amendment to extend the legal duty. Treat it as sponsor rhetoric, not as a change to s. 2(8).

**Chloe Smith at Committee (as recorded by CBP-9446).** Communications could include public announcements, publication of strategies, plans and consultations, and press conferences promoting a department’s work. No NMD list.

**Lords Second Reading, 25 March 2022.** Baroness Barker’s contribution (via Parallel Parliament extract) restates clause 2 as a duty to report “across all the departments of state listed in the Schedule.” No NMD is named in the extracts located for this pass. Lord Holmes of Richmond sponsored the Bill in the Lords; the government’s request, recorded by CBP-9446, was that the Lords pass the Bill without amendment. They did.

### What was *not* said

A targeted search of TheyWorkForYou debate pages for the Bill stages plus the two Commons Library briefings found **no hit** for: HMRC; His Majesty’s Revenue and Customs; Charity Commission; Land Registry; Crown Prosecution Service; Ofsted; Food Standards Agency; “non-ministerial department”; or “non ministerial.”

“Non-statutory board” *does* appear (Chloe Smith, Second Reading col. 1257) — that is the BSL Advisory Board, not non-ministerial departments. Do not conflate the two strings.

**Confidence on the negative finding.** High that the Library briefings and the published Bill papers do not record an NMD debate. Medium-high on the floor itself: Hansard.com could not be fetched directly (403), so a stray unindexed intervention is not *formally* impossible. Nothing in the secondary record suggests one exists.

## 62.4 Question (2) — was any amendment tabled that would have extended the Schedule?

**Finding: no. The only Committee amendment went the other way — it wrote “ministerial government departments” into the long title.**

Public Bill Committee papers for 23 February 2022:

- **Amendment 1** (Rosie Cooper). Long title: leave out from beginning to end of line 5 and insert “Recognise British Sign Language as a language of England, Wales and Scotland; to require the Secretary of State to report on the promotion and facilitation of the use of British Sign Language by **ministerial government departments**; and to require guidance to be issued in relation to British Sign Language.” Member’s explanatory statement: “This amendment brings the Bill’s long title in line with the contents of the Bill.”
- Clauses 1 to 4 agreed.
- Schedule agreed (no amendment to add or remove a body).
- Bill, as amended, reported.

Notices of Amendments dated 17 and 22 February 2022 contain the same single amendment. CBP-9446: “Only one amendment was proposed, to change the Bill’s long title.” Report stage (18 March 2022): “No amendments were proposed.” Lords: passed without amendment.

### Why the long title changed

The long title *as introduced* (Bill 33, 26 January 2022), recorded by CBP-9446, still read:

> “Declare British Sign Language (BSL) an official language of the United Kingdom; to provide for a British Sign Language Council to promote and advise on the use of BSL; to establish principles for the use of BSL in public services; to require public bodies to have regard to those principles and to guidance issued by the Council; and for connected purposes.”

That is the original ballot-Bill framing: a statutory Council and duties on “public bodies.” By the time the print of Bill 33 appeared, the *clauses* had already been rewritten with government support into recognition + ministerial reporting + guidance. Amendment 1 caught the title up. Rosie Cooper’s own Committee explanation, as recorded by CBP-9446: the change reflected (a) there is no single “official language” of the UK, so “declare… official” was the wrong verb, and (b) “the limited scope of Private Members Bills.” The original statutory Council became Chloe Smith’s non-statutory advisory board precisely because a PMB “cannot create new public expenditure.”

So the movement in the papers is **narrowing from “public bodies / BSL Council” to “ministerial government departments / non-statutory board,”** not a failed attempt to *add* HMRC. No MP is recorded as objecting to that narrowing on the ground that NMDs would fall out.

## 62.5 Question (3) — did a minister or the sponsor explain *why* the Schedule stops at ministerial departments?

**Finding: yes, but in the accompanying papers, not in a floor exchange about HMRC.** Three layered explanations exist. None is a resourcing argument about interpreter budgets. None names HMRC.

### A. Drafting / PMB-scope rationale (sponsor, on the record)

Rosie Cooper at Committee, via CBP-9446: the rewritten long title and the drop from a statutory Council to a non-statutory board were driven by PMB limits and by clause 1(2) (recognition does not affect any other enactment). That is why the Bill reports on *ministerial departments* rather than creating enforceable duties on “public bodies.”

### B. Machinery-of-government rationale (Explanatory Notes)

Explanatory Notes on s. 2(8)–(9):

> “Subsection (8) defines what is meant by a ‘relevant government department’. It is one of the ministerial government departments listed in the Schedule to the Act. Subsection (9) gives the Secretary of State power to amend this list by regulations. This power is intended to only be used where there are ‘machinery of government’ changes that may merge government departments or if, for example, a specific part of a ministerial department’s portfolio were decoupled from its current department and formed a new ministerial department in its own right.”

That is a *how-the-list-is-kept-current* rationale, not a *why-NMDs-are-out* rationale. It assumes the list is ministerial.

### C. Express non-ministerial exclusion (Delegated Powers Memorandum, 22 March 2022)

This is the document that answers the question this prompt asked. DWP’s memorandum to the Lords Delegated Powers and Regulatory Reform Committee, published with the Lords print:

> Clause 2(9) “gives the Secretary of State power to make regulations to amend the list of ministerial departments in the Schedule. As this is a power to amend primary legislation, it is a Henry VIII power. However, the power is only exercisable in tightly defined circumstances — where there has been a change in a department’s name or in the way in which ministerial government departments are organised.”

> “The purpose of the delegation is to ensure that the list of relevant ministerial departments can be kept up to date in the event of a change in the way in which the ministerial government departments are organised. For example, if a ministerial department is abolished then it would no longer be appropriate for the Secretary of State to publish a report about its BSL communications. Conversely, if a new department is created or one that is not currently included in the Schedule were merged with another department that is listed in the Schedule, then the power could be used to rectify the position.”

> **“The power has been designed so that it could not be exercised to add a non-ministerial department or other public body to the list. Moreover, the power could not be used to remove a department from the list purely for policy reasons.”**

> “At the time of the preparation of the Bill, the list of relevant ministerial departments is considered to be appropriate, in order to ensure certainty regarding the departments on whom the duty falls.”

Negative procedure was justified as matching “the uncontroversial nature, and relatively narrow, nature of the power.”

**What this is, and what it is not.** It is a contemporaneous government legal explanation that (i) the Schedule is ministerial by design, (ii) the Henry VIII power is boxed so it cannot be used later to bolt HMRC or an NDPB onto the Act, and (iii) adding an NMD would therefore need **primary legislation**, not s. 2(9). It is **not** a speech in which a minister was challenged about tax, prosecution, land registration or charity regulation. No such challenge appears in the debate record located here.

**Source.** British Sign Language Bill: Delegated Powers Memorandum, 22 March 2022, bills.parliament.uk publications list (publication 45893 / document 1725). <https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/2915/publications>

## 62.6 Question (4) — did the impact assessment or a government fact sheet address the NMD gap?

**Finding: no dedicated impact assessment for this Bill was located, and the published fact sheets do not mention the NMD cut.**

The bills.parliament.uk publications list for Bill 2915 includes the Bill prints, Explanatory Notes (Commons and Lords), amendment papers, Delegated Powers Memorandum, legislative consent motions, and ministerial “will write” letters. It does **not** list an impact assessment.

The 28 January 2022 DWP press notice “Government backs vital British Sign Language Bill” describes a duty on “ministerial departments to report their BSL usage” and guidance to “ministerial departments.” It does not discuss bodies outside that set. <https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-backs-vital-british-sign-language-bill>

Absence of an IA is normal for a short, government-supported PMB with no new spending. It is still a gap: nobody was required to cost, or even list, the operational bodies (HMRC, CPS, Land Registry, Ofsted) that sit next to the Schedule departments and handle the high-stakes contact the campaign actually cares about.

Post-enactment, the gap is acknowledged in a different register. The first BSL report (31 July 2023) and the later annual reports state:

> “For the purposes of the act, only core departments need to report on how they promote and use BSL in their communications. Agencies, non-departmental public bodies and other arm’s-length bodies are not in scope of the act, but are encouraged to use BSL translation and interpreting where it meets the needs of their audiences.”

CDP-2023-0191 (12 October 2023) repeats the same cut for the Library’s audience: “The list includes only the main central government departments; arms-length and non-departmental public bodies are not included.”

Those sentences post-date Royal Assent. They confirm how government reads the Act. They are not 2021–22 Bill-stage debate.

## 62.7 Has the Schedule-amendment power been used since 2022?

In scope for prompt 65 in more detail. For this file, one fact is needed so the s. 2(9) discussion is not left hanging.

The Schedule **has** been edited, but only for machinery-of-government renames and splits. The principal instrument cited on legislation.gov.uk is **S.I. 2023/424** (the Secretaries of State for Energy Security and Net Zero, for Science, Innovation and Technology, for Business and Trade, and for Culture, Media and Sport and the Transfer of Functions Order 2023), in force 3 May 2023. It substituted “Business and Trade” and “Culture, Media and Sport,” omitted the old Department for International Trade entry, and inserted DESNZ and DSIT. Later edits rename DLUHC as the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government.

No instrument located for this pass adds an NMD. That is consistent with the Delegated Powers Memorandum’s own box: s. 2(9) was written so that it *cannot* do that job.

## 62.8 How to read this without over-claiming

The honest shape of the 2021–22 record:

- The original campaign Bill pointed at “public bodies” and a statutory Council.
- Government support was bought by rewriting the operative clauses down to recognition + a reporting duty on a closed ministerial Schedule + non-statutory guidance and a non-statutory board.
- Amendment 1 then made the long title tell the truth about that rewrite.
- DWP’s Delegated Powers Memorandum told the Lords, in terms, that the Henry VIII power could not later be used to add a non-ministerial department.
- Nobody on the floor is recorded as saying “what about HMRC?”
- The bigger live argument at the time — and the one Jeff McWhinney and others ran (prompt 63) — was “no enforceable individual right,” not “the Schedule missed the tax office.”

That is a scrutiny gap, not evidence of concealment. The limit is in the Act, the Notes, and the Memorandum. It was simply not the argument anyone chose to have in the chamber.
