# Prompt 137 — AI notetakers and transcripts at work: data protection, monitoring, and colleague consent

**Master index prompt 137.** Fourth file in the Devices & Technology workplace batch (prompts 134–140). Compiled 11 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier.

**What this prompt is for.** The general workplace data-protection / consent question when an employer (or the employee) puts an AI notetaker, live transcript or meeting-summary tool into a meeting as a reasonable adjustment — cognitive-processing aid for a neurodivergent employee, or captioning / notetaking aid for a Deaf / BSL-using employee. Ask whether colleagues can object to being recorded, how published guidance resolves that clash with s.20, and whether any ICO decision or named employer policy has tried the facts. Cross-reference prompt 129 (Chinese Walls / deal-team barriers) and do not repeat it. Prompt 135 holds the Teams caption *enablement* default. This file is the recording / transcript / summary layer.

**Hard-don’t.** Do not print that using, refusing or objecting to an AI notetaker is lawful or unlawful without a named statute, regulation, ICO decision or judgment.

---

## Headline

Two duties sit on the same meeting. **Neither published source decides which one wins.**

- A voice recording and the transcript it generates are **personal data** of everyone who speaks (UK GDPR; ICO *Monitoring workers*, October 2023). The employer (or the employee’s vendor, if the bot leaves the tenant) is a controller or processor and needs a **lawful basis**, transparency, minimisation, a retention rule, and — where the processing is likely high-risk — a **DPIA**.
- **Consent is usually the wrong basis in employment.** ICO: consent must be freely given; the employer–worker imbalance means workers “are likely to feel that they have no choice.” Legitimate interests plus a documented three-part test is the basis ICO treats as the usual flexible option. A tick-box at the start of the call does not convert that into valid consent.
- Workers (and other people on the call) **can object** under Article 21 where the basis is legitimate interests or public task. The right is **not absolute**. The controller can continue if it demonstrates **compelling legitimate grounds** that override the objector’s interests, rights and freedoms, or if the processing is for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims. The controller must tell the objector if it refuses, and point them to the ICO / a court.
- Equality Act s.20 still applies to the disabled employee who needs a transcript or live text. ACAS (30 January 2025) has a generic “conflicting needs” page: talk it through; consider different options; if unresolved, look at whether the adjustment is still reasonable; take advice before refusing. The worked example is document length (ADHD vs autism), **not** a recording objection.
- **No ICO decision notice, EHRC employment paragraph, ACAS sentence or named tribunal was found that resolves “colleague objects to the AI notetaker / colleague’s Art. 21 right versus the disabled employee’s s.20 right.”** That join is the unpublished cell.

Teams can require **explicit participant agreement** before recording or transcription. Microsoft’s default for that policy is **Off**. An admin can turn it on. That is a product control, not an ICO ruling.

US class actions against Otter (N.D. Cal., claims surviving in part August 2026) are **international**. Do not fill a UK cell with them. Prompt 129’s MAR / SYSC wall stays in that file.

## 137.1 What the two statutes actually say, without joining them

**Data protection.** Recording or transcribing a meeting processes personal data of every identifiable speaker. The employer needs a lawful basis, a purpose that cannot reasonably be met by a less intrusive step (ICO: minutes may suffice), transparency before the recording starts, minimisation, security, a retention clock, and a processor contract if a vendor sits in the call. Consent is rarely valid between employer and worker. Article 21 lets a person object to legitimate-interests processing; the controller then either stops or documents why compelling grounds override.

**Equality Act.** s.20 is fact-specific. A transcript or live text can be an auxiliary aid. ACAS tells the employer to discuss conflicting needs and not to refuse an adjustment lightly. That is not a ranking rule that puts s.20 above Art. 21 or the other way around.

**The unpublished join.** No official source ranks those two duties when the objector is a colleague and the purpose is a disabled employee’s adjustment. Firm blogs that say “if one person objects, stop” or “get everyone’s consent” are practice notes, not ICO decisions. Do not promote them to law.

## 137.2 Product facts that change the risk shape (not the legal test)

- Tenant transcription (Teams / Meet / Zoom inside the existing DPA) is a different processor map from a consumer bot that joins as “OtterPilot” and may train on the audio.
- Teams can force a consent gate. The shipped default does not.
- Live captions that are **not saved** (prompt 135: Teams live captions “aren’t saved alongside the video file”) are a less persistent processing operation than a stored transcript or Copilot recap. Persistence is a minimisation fact, not a free pass.

Disciplinary, grievance, health and redundancy meetings concentrate special-category and confidential content. ICO monitoring guidance plus ACAS record-keeping advice both bite harder there. That still does not decide the RA conflict.
