# Prompt 168 — Self-directed AI writing/comprehension tools as a personal accommodation

**Master index prompt 168.** Addendum to Devices-Technology 134–140. Compiled 14 September 2026. Gate for 169.

**What this prompt is for.** An employee, on their own initiative, pastes *their own* draft or *a document they must read* into Claude / Copilot / ChatGPT / Grammarly / a local model — proofread, bullets, plain English. Not an avatar (136). Not a meeting bot (137). Not an NHS patient letter (51). Do not re-derive the cap, the 3,210 BSL-interpreter count, or the “BSL is not written English” fact.

**Hard-don’t.** Do not print that an employer is obliged to fund a named AI product.

---

## Headline

**No UK employment-regulator text names a general-purpose AI writing assistant as a reasonable adjustment for a BSL-first employee.**

That absence is the finding. It sits next to a fact already on file (prompt 51 / Locked Out): BSL has no ordinary written form, so written English is not automatically accessible. That fact does **not** convert ChatGPT into an EHRC-listed auxiliary aid. EA s.20 remains fact-specific. Software *can* be an auxiliary aid. EHRC and ACAS still name readers, interpreters, and specialist equipment — not a consumer chatbot used on the employee’s own prose.

Closest documented objects:

- **Specialist writing software** (Read&Write, Texthelp, Microsoft Editor, Dragon, screen readers) is the AtW / DSA / HE catalogue. York St John (staff and students) lists Grammarly in an “AI and reasonable adjustments” table *inside higher education*, and mentions AtW for staff. That is one university page, not EHRC.
- **Nicholas v Rabart Decorators Merchants Ltd** 6001763/2023. Marketing coordinator, **dyslexia**, used free Grammarly and ChatGPT on social posts. Tribunal accepted other RA / s.15 claims. On Grammarly **Pro** as an auxiliary aid it said it was **not satisfied** the product would remove the disadvantage — the claimant had not evidenced what Pro did. So the one named ET that *mentions* ChatGPT/Grammarly as writing support **refused to treat the paid upgrade as the adjustment**. Not a Deaf/BSL claimant.
- **Access to Work SAE** funds specialist software for employment. The live employer factsheet and staff-guide SAE chapter do not name ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot or Grok as a product line. No official table of “AI writing-assistant licences awarded.” MoleWorks 2025 pack: AtW/DSA sometimes *recommend* AI-powered writing or transcription tools that employer IT then **blocks**. Recommendation-plus-block is the grey zone, not a funding series.
- CIPD Autumn 2025 Labour Market Outlook: employees use AI in **76%** of organisations; **54%** free tools; **42%** paid (including employee-paid). That is general productivity use, not a BSL-adjustment census.

Employer policy on *this* use is usually silent or sits inside a generic “approved tools only” AI policy (prompt 169). No BDA / RNID / ASLI page found that frames self-directed ChatGPT-for-emails as the BSL-first adjustment product.

## 168.1 How to read the absence

s.20 does not run a product catalogue. An employee *can* argue that a writing tool removes a substantial disadvantage if written English is the PCP and BSL is their first language. No published EHRC, ACAS, DWP or BDA sentence performs that argument for ChatGPT. The one tribunal that was asked about a paid writing-AI upgrade said the evidence was not there.

Do not print “employers must buy Claude.” Do not print “self-directed use is never an adjustment.” Both over-claim.

## 168.2 What stays unpublished

- Official AtW count of genAI writing-assistant licences.
- A Deaf/BSL-first named first-person of this exact use.
- An EHRC or ACAS worked example.

169 takes the confidentiality collision when the pasted text is the employer’s.
