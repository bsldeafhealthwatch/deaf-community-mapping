# Prompt 138 — International comparative: workplace AI avatars, notetakers, refuse-the-machine, and consent vs accommodation

**Master index prompt 138.** Fifth file in the Devices & Technology workplace batch (prompts 134–140). Compiled 11 September 2026. **International context only.** None of this fills a UK cell in 134–137 or 139–140. Do not paste an EEOC, ADA, EU AI Act, CAN-ASC or Australian EAF sentence into those files as if it were UK law.

**What this prompt is for.** Ask whether any of USA, Canada, Australia or the EU has published *regulatory* guidance that (1) treats AI signing avatars or AI notetakers as workplace reasonable accommodations and states a right to refuse the machine in favour of a human, and (2) reconciles colleague / co-worker data-protection objections with the disabled employee’s accommodation right. Log absence. Flag instruments that look close but sit in a different legal system (federal accessibility standards, parliamentary recommendations, hiring-algorithm guidance).

---

## Headline

**No asked jurisdiction has published the UK-shaped join.**

Closest texts, all non-transferable:

- **Canada — CAN-ASC-6.2:2025** (*Accessible and Equitable Artificial Intelligence Systems*), Accessibility Standards Canada. Note under the accommodation clause: replacing human interpreters with AI or using AI sign-language recognition “can cause harm to ASL and LSQ signers, particularly in critical settings such as justice and healthcare.” “Any use of AI for sign language communication should be agreed upon with the intended beneficiary, with the option to opt for a human alternative at any time.” A later note: when AI sign-language systems are deployed, deaf end-users need autonomy to accept or decline and a real option to request a human interpreter without penalty. This is a **national accessibility standard** under the Accessible Canada Act architecture, aimed at federally regulated entities. It is not a UK EHRC Code and it is not an ICO decision.
- **EU — European Parliament report A10-0244/2025** (digitalisation / AI / algorithmic management at work), paragraph 20: calls on the Commission and Member States to guarantee effective reasonable accommodation and to “respect their ultimate authority to determine whether AI-based assistive technologies in the workplace is adapted to their individual needs.” That is a **Parliament recommendation to the Commission**, not an enacted Article of the AI Act or of Directive 2000/78. The AI Act itself classifies certain employment AI (recruitment, task allocation, monitoring, performance) as Annex III high-risk, requires worker notice before a high-risk system is put in place (Art. 26(7)), and requires transparency when a person interacts with an AI system (Art. 50). It does **not** name signing avatars as a workplace accommodation product, and it does **not** create a right to refuse an avatar in favour of a human interpreter.
- **USA — EEOC *Hearing Disabilities in the Workplace and the ADA* (2023, page live 2026).** Lists a sign-language interpreter (in person or VRI) **and**, separately, “assistive software or applications (for example, for automated captioning, voice recognition…)” and “utilizing accessibility features of mainstream technology (for example, using the captioning feature on virtual meeting platforms).” The interactive process decides effectiveness. EEOC’s 2022 AI technical assistance is about **algorithmic hiring / assessment tools**, not avatars as interpreter substitutes. No EEOC sentence was found that an employee may refuse an AI avatar and insist on a human interpreter as of right. ADA Title I remains fact-specific undue-hardship analysis.
- **Australia — Employment Assistance Fund.** Auslan Level 1 reimburses work-related interpreting, remote interpreting, **and** captioning / real-time captioning / note-taking. Those are human or human-supervised products on the live JobAccess page (caps updated 2026). NAATI (March 2025) and AUSIT (November 2025) have position statements on AI in translation and interpreting as a **profession**, not as an employer reasonable-adjustment rule. No Fair Work Ombudsman guide was found that ranks an AI avatar against an Auslan interpreter at work.

**Colleague-consent vs accommodation.** No asked regulator published a worked example that ranks a co-worker’s recording objection against a disabled employee’s notetaker. EEOC, CHRC’s 2026 federally-regulated accommodation guide, JobAccess conversation guide, and the AI Act transparency / worker-notice rules do not perform that ranking. US all-party consent statutes (California etc.) and the Otter N.D. Cal. litigation are wiretap / state-privacy facts, not ADA-accommodation ranking. Do not import them.

**Do not transfer.** Different duty-holders (ADA Title I employer; Accessible Canada Act federally regulated organisations; AI Act providers and deployers; Australian DDA + EAF grant). Different “undue hardship / unjustifiable hardship / disproportionate burden” tests. Different data-protection statutes (US sectoral; PIPEDA / provincial; GDPR already closer to UK GDPR but the AI Act is additional and not copied into UK law).

## 138.1 How to read the close texts without importing them

CAN-ASC-6.2 is the only official instrument found that puts “AI for sign language” and “option to opt for a human alternative at any time” in the same paragraph. It is written as an accessibility **standard** for organisations in the Accessible Canada Act world. Treating that sentence as UK Equality Act s.20 would be the exact transfer this prompt forbids.

The EU Parliament line about “ultimate authority” over whether workplace AI-AT is adapted to the person is a political ask sitting on top of Directive 2000/78. The AI Act’s actual operative hooks for work are high-risk classification, worker notice, human oversight and interaction-transparency — none of which decide an avatar-versus-interpreter booking.

EEOC lists human interpreters and automated captioning as *different items on the same menu*. That is consistent with the interactive process. It is not a ranking rule, and it is not about BSL avatars.

Australia pays for human Auslan and for captioning through EAF. That is a funding menu, not a refuse-the-avatar right.

## 138.2 Consent / monitoring

No asked jurisdiction published the prompt-137 join (colleague objects to the bot; disabled employee needs the transcript). US multi-party consent statutes and the Otter litigation are recording-consent regimes. They do not rank against ADA Title I. GDPR-family rules in the EU are already closer to UK GDPR; the additional AI Act worker-notice duty still does not perform the ranking.

---

## 138.3 What stays unpublished internationally

- A named employment-tribunal or EEOC/CHRC/FWC decision that an employer discharged the duty by offering an AI signing avatar instead of the requested human interpreter.
- A named employment-regulator code chapter titled for workplace AI avatars.
- A published employer policy, in any asked country, that writes down the colleague-objection vs RA-notetaker ranking.

WFD/WASLI 2018 remains the international *public-authority* avatar statement already logged in Domain 14 and prompt 136. It is not an employment code.
