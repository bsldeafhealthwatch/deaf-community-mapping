# Prompt 136 — Has any UK employer used an AI sign-language avatar instead of a human interpreter at work, and is there published guidance on refusing it?

**Master index prompt 136.** Third file in the Devices & Technology workplace batch (prompts 134–140). Load-bearing prompt. Compiled 11 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier.

**What this prompt is for.** Test Domain 14’s fault line — who controls the tool, can the employee refuse it, is it interpreter-equivalent — inside **employment**, not NHS patient-facing use. Ask whether a named UK employer has piloted, procured or proposed an AI signing avatar as a partial or full substitute for booked human BSL interpreters at work. Ask whether EHRC, ACAS, BDF or a Deaf-sector body has published guidance on whether that offer would satisfy Equality Act s.20, or whether the employee can insist on a human interpreter. Log absence. Do not print lawful or unlawful without a named statute, regulation or judgment.

**Index check.** This file is the research *answer* to prompt 136. Domain 14 already holds SignGPT, WFD/WASLI April 2018, De Meulder, RNID Sciencewise, and commercial avatar vendors as *research / public-service* material. Prompt 135 holds built-in English captions. Prompt 129 is wall-crossed AI notes. Do not import those cells as an employment-law conclusion unless the source itself is writing about employers.

## Headline

**No named UK employer case was found.** No published EHRC, ACAS or Business Disability Forum sentence decides the Equality Act question. That double absence is the finding.

What exists instead:

- **Vendor and transport pilots that are not workplace interpreter-substitution.** Silence Speaks (UK startup, WIRED 7 May 2025) markets a text-to-BSL avatar and names “the workplace” as an application; the only named deployment track in that coverage is Transport for London train-announcement screens — passenger information, not an employee’s booked interpreter. Signapse’s documented UK live use with South Western Railway / Network Rail / Whoosh Media is QR-code rail announcements. InSignLanguage’s Innovate UK BridgeAI case (posted January 2026) is public-transport proof-of-concept. SignWow is human VRS sold into work via Access to Work, not an avatar.
- **Statutory codes that name a human interpreter and are silent on avatars.** EHRC *Employment Statutory Code of Practice* (2011) lists “providing a reader or interpreter” among example adjustments. ACAS (updated 30 January 2025) gives “a sign language interpreter” as an example of specialist support. Neither document mentions signing avatars, AI interpreters, or a right to refuse a machine. The draft ACAS disciplinary/grievance Code (consultation 2026) adds “an interpreter if there is a language barrier” as a process adjustment and asks a separate question about AI *in grievance drafting* — not avatar-as-RA.
- **Deaf-sector positions that are policy asks, not judgments.** ASLI’s June 2025 Pathways to Work response: AI “remain[s] incapable of adequately meeting the communication needs of deaf BSL users in workplace settings” and must be tested with BSL users before government introduces it. BDA *AI and BSL* discussion paper (page live August 2026): risk that government purchasers, including Access to Work, are “mis-sold AI solutions that sound cheap.” RNID 2026 survey records AI *captions* offered instead of STTR, and a Sciencewise dialogue on AI BSL translation — not a named employer avatar award. WFD/WASLI statement on signing avatars (14 March 2018, updated 14 April 2018) cautions public authorities against avatars as a replacement for human signers for live, complex or high-stakes information. It is written at public-authority access, not UK Part 5 employment.
- **De Meulder** (BDA conference July 2025; *Deaf in AI*, arXiv 5 May 2025 / rev. 5 July 2026) asks who decides if AI is “good enough for interpreting” and critiques AI framed as an interpreter substitute. That is research governance. It is not an EHRC employment opinion.

No published employment tribunal or EAT decision was found that turns on an employer offering or imposing an AI signing avatar instead of a requested human interpreter.

**Hard-don’t.** Do not print that offering an avatar instead of a human interpreter is lawful or unlawful. Do not print that the EHRC Code “requires” a human interpreter to the exclusion of AI — the Code gives an example, it does not decide 2026 products. Do not treat Interpreting Matters’ December 2025 conference blog (“organisers relying solely on AI-generated BSL risk failing to provide a reasonable and effective adjustment”) as EHRC guidance; it is an agency’s professional view. Do not treat Silence Speaks, Signapse or TfL as a workplace interpreter replacement. Do not paste WFD/WASLI or De Meulder into an Equality Act conclusion.

## 136.1 The workplace object is still empty

Domain 14 already mapped the research and public-service field. This prompt asked a narrower question: has a UK *employer* used an avatar to stand in for the interpreter that employee would otherwise book for meetings.

The named UK deployments that exist are one-way, pre-scripted or announcement-style BSL on public screens and QR codes. That is closer to Domain 14’s patient-facing / public-information strand than to a two-way workplace meeting. Silence Speaks’ WIRED copy *wants* a workplace market. Wanting a market is not a named employer case.

SignWow and AtW VRS remain human interpreters on a video platform (prompt 134 staff-guide bundles). Do not recode them as avatars.

---

## 136.2 Guidance stops at “for example, a sign language interpreter”

The live employment tools give a human interpreter as an *example* of an auxiliary aid or specialist support. They do not:

- accredit or ban signing avatars;
- say an employer may or may not meet s.20 by offering one;
- give the employee an express right to refuse a machine and insist on a human.

Reasonableness under s.20 stays fact-specific (does this step avoid the substantial disadvantage; is it practicable and proportionate). That general test is verified. Its application to a 2026 avatar product is unpublished. Interpreting Matters’ “risk failing” sentence is how one agency reads the 2011 Code. It is not an EHRC update.

ASLI and BDA are the clearest *workplace-adjacent* policy asks: do not treat current AI as meeting BSL workplace need; do not let AtW buyers take a cheap-AI pitch at face value. Those are consultation and discussion-paper positions.
