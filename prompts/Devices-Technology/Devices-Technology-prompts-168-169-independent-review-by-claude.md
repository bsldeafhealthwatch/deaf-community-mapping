## Verdict for any public page

**Curate first. Draft almost nothing — more so here than in 134–140, because this ground is thinner and the one named case cuts against over-claiming in both directions.**

The one sentence a page is allowed to lead with, if any:

> No UK employment-regulator text names a general-purpose AI writing tool (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Grammarly, or a local model) as a reasonable adjustment for a BSL-first or dyslexic employee's own writing. The one employment tribunal that has considered a paid AI writing upgrade as part of a disability discrimination case did not accept it as the adjustment — for lack of evidence about what it does, not because AI tools can never count. Separately, published employer and NHS policy treats pasting confidential work text into an unapproved public AI tool the same way it treats an unapproved website — a policy and governance question, not a settled data-protection breach.

## Don't list (print near any workplace-AI / AT page)

- Do not print that *Nicholas v Rabart* held AI writing tools cannot be reasonable adjustments. It held the opposite failure mode: the claimant didn't put in evidence of what the specific paid product did differently, so that specific claim failed on evidence, not on principle. Other adjustment and s.15 claims in the same case succeeded.
- Do not print that *Nicholas v Rabart* involved a Deaf or BSL-using claimant. It was a dyslexia case.
- Do not print that an employer is obliged to fund ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, or any named AI product as a reasonable adjustment. No EHRC, ACAS, or AtW text says so.
- Do not print that self-directed AI writing-tool use can *never* be a reasonable adjustment. The absence found is an absence of a named regulator or tribunal endorsement, not a prohibition — s.20 remains fact-specific and could in principle be argued on better evidence than the claimant supplied in the one case found.
- Do not print that pasting confidential work material into a public AI tool is automatically a UK GDPR breach. Published texts treat it as a governance/policy matter (approved-tool lists) and a controller/processor question if personal data is involved — neither is a blanket "breach" finding, and no ICO Decision Notice on this exact fact pattern was found.
- Do not print that a local or self-hosted model is a documented, funded, or approved fix for the confidentiality question. No AtW award and no employer policy naming this as the accepted mitigation was found — the documented middle path in the sources opened is a *tenant* product (e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot) with a data-processing agreement, not a personal local model.
- Do not treat the York St John page (168) as a national HE or DWP position — it is one university's practice note.
- Do not treat MoleWorks' "recommended-then-blocked" pattern as a measured national rate — it is one advisory pack's framing of a documented grey zone.
- Do not conflate this addendum's object with 136 (AI avatar offered instead of a human interpreter) or 137 (AI notetaker recording a meeting, colleague consent, ICO Art. 21). This is the employee's own writing/comprehension task, done on their own initiative.
- Do not treat the CIPD 76%/54%/42% uptake figures as split by disability or by BSL-user status — they are all-staff figures.
- Do not print the New Zealand ERA 2025 material (169, confidentiality line drawn at uploading employer material to OpenAI) as UK law — it is labelled international in the source file and should stay that way.
