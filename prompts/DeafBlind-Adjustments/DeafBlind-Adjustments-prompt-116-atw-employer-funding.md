# Prompt 116 — Does Access to Work treat DeafBlind-qualified support differently from BSL-only provision?

**Master index prompt 116.** Second file in the DeafBlind-specific in-post workplace adjustments batch (prompts 115–120). Compiled 11 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment; do not wear a DEI or anti-DEI banner. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier. Gaps are logged as gaps, not filled guesses.

**What this prompt is for.** Test whether the AtW cap, staff-guide bands, cost-share rules and approval process treat deafblind manual / RIDB / guide-communicator support as a different product from a standard BSL interpreter. Name any staff-guide category that would cover a guide-communicator. Log any *DeafBlind-named* award reduction, delay or refusal that turns on cost or rarity — not the general backlog already logged in prompts 20 and 52–54. Ask whether any employer-funded (non-AtW) workplace CG or DeafBlind-specific kit is documented as standard practice. Do not paste NAO 109-day or RNID survey percentages into this cell unless the source itself names a DeafBlind claimant.

## Headline

Access to Work’s **ceiling is one number for every customer**: **£69,260** a year from 8 April 2024, frozen through **31 March 2027**. There is no published DeafBlind uplift, no RIDB exemption, and no extra cap for a scarcer profession. Official statistics count a **“BSL Interpreter”** support-worker type (3,210 customers paid in 2024/25, average **£18,200**) and a primary-condition bucket **“Deaf or hard of hearing”** (4,980 customers; **£90.3 million**, 28% of element spend). They do **not** publish a “Deafblind Interpreter,” “RIDB,” or “guide-communicator” customer count. Dual-sensory customers, if present, are inside those larger buckets. That absence is the official-data finding.

The staff guide **does name the role**. It defines “interpreter with deafblind people” as someone with specific training who is **registered with NRCPD in this category**, and it states that because there are “very few” such registrants, “many deafblind people will work with BSL interpreters who have additional skills, for example, in Close Frame interpreting.” It defines a Communication Support Worker as someone who uses “a variety of methods to support deaf and deafblind people access communication.” Those sentences recognise scarcity. They do **not** create a separate hourly band.

The published rate card that advisers use is still shaped around BSL and generic communication support:

- British Sign Language (must be NRCPD registered or trainee): National **£26–£47** / London **£30–£52** an hour
- Communication Support (not a registered SLI): National **£22–£30** / London **£27.50–£35**
- Remote BSL VRS/VRI bundles inc. VAT: 60 min **£105** … 1,125 min **£1,480**
- STTR: **£78–£120**
- Generic Support Worker / Job Aide / Travel Buddy / Carer sit on lower bands (national minimum wage up to the mid-£20s)

No published staff-guide row is headed “RIDB,” “deafblind manual interpreter,” or “guide-communicator.” A guide-communicator that AtW agreed to fund would have to be argued into **Support Worker**, **Communication Support**, **Travel Buddy**, or **Job Aide** — products priced below a registered interpreter and below NUBSLI’s April 2026 RSLI medians (London full day **£375**). NUBSLI’s published fee table has **no RIDB column**.

Cost share, on the live employer factsheet (updated 7 October 2025), does **not** apply to support workers, travel, or communication support at interview. AtW “will consider paying grants of up to 100%” for those. Cost share is for special aids and equipment and premises adaptations, on an employer-size threshold (0–49 nil; 50–249 £500; 250+ £1,000, then 20% up to £10,000). A DeafBlind-specific *person* and a DeafBlind-specific *device* therefore sit on different contribution rules. That is not a DeafBlind exception; it is the general support-worker rule.

**Named DeafBlind-specific friction exists, but it is old.** Sense’s written evidence ATW0075 to the Work and Pensions Committee, **19 June 2014**, is the only clustered, named UK documentary account found on this pass of awards being challenged *because the support was DeafBlind-qualified rather than BSL*:

- a Sense employee using deafblind manual had to **re-justify RIDB over BSL at every review**; her manager was phoned to challenge the hourly rate because BSL interpreters could be sourced more cheaply
- a Sense employee, deafblind and a wheelchair user, waited **14 weeks** for a renewal that had been requested six weeks before expiry; Sense paid the interpreter and support worker in the gap
- a hands-on signing user had hours cut **15 to 10 a week** with no change in circumstances, and an agreed hourly rate “approximately a fifth of the going rate,” with the customer making up the difference
- a Deaf + restricted-visual-field employee needing >30 hours a week was told funding would be capped at **£30,000** and she should employ a salaried interpreter

Those facts are **2014**. The £30,000 “30-hour rule” salary cap in that evidence is not the live 2026 ceiling. No 2025–26 tribunal, ombudsman or named WPC submission was found that repeats the “cheaper BSL instead of RIDB” challenge as a fresh, named DeafBlind case. The RNID/DeafATW survey (Dec 2025–Jan 2026, n=358 “deaf, deafblind or hearing loss”) and AWS0095 / AWS0121 (March 2026) include “deafblind” in the sample label; published splits and the named case study (AB) are **BSL-user**, not RIDB-versus-BSL.

Employer-funded (non-AtW) workplace guide-communicator support or DeafBlind-specific kit as **standard practice**: **no reliable figure found**. Sense communicator-guides and Deafblind Scotland Guide Communicators are charity/NHS community or social-care roles, not a published employer workplace product. Deafblind UK’s Employability Programme (page live 8 September 2026) is for people **aged 18–60 not in full-time work**, and mentions Access to Work advice — it is a jobseeker course, not in-post employer provision. Vibrating pagers and braille displays can sit under AtW Special Aids and Equipment in principle; no official SAE table that itemises them as a DeafBlind workplace standard was found on this pass.

**Hard-don’t for anything public.** Do not print the frozen cap, the BSL rate card, or the absence of an RIDB band as unlawful. Do not treat 2014 Sense evidence as a 2026 judgment. Do not file AB, Rimmer, or generic RNID cap quotes as DeafBlind-rarity cases.

## 116.1 Same cap, BSL-shaped rate card, named role without a tariff

Three official facts sit on top of each other.

1. One cap. £69,260. Frozen. No RIDB rider.
2. One published interpreter tariff. The BSL band, with NRCPD verification baked in. RIDB is also an NRCPD category (prompt 115), so a registered RIDB *can* be argued into that band. The guide never says so in terms, and never prices the scarcity.
3. One scarcity sentence. “Very few registered interpreters with deafblind people,” so many customers will use BSL interpreters with extra skills (Close Frame). That is AtW describing substitution toward the larger RSLI bench (1,664) and away from the RIDB bench (19). It is not a finding that substitution is lawful or unlawful. It is how the guide tells advisers the market works.

A guide-communicator is in a worse position on the same card. The role is not an NRCPD category (115). The closest official labels are CSW (“deaf and deafblind people”) and generic Support Worker / Travel Buddy. Those bands top out below the BSL band and well below a NUBSLI RSLI day rate. AtW can still award a higher quoted rate — Disability Arts Online tells employers the published pounds are guidance and quotes above them should be considered — but that is discretion, not a published RIDB or CG tariff.

## 116.2 Cost share is about kit, not the person

The live employer factsheet puts support workers, travel and CSI on the 100%-consider list. Special aids and equipment and premises adaptations take the employer-size threshold. So a DeafBlind employee’s *interpreter or guide* and their *braille display or vibrating pager* are not on the same contribution rule. Do not print “employers must always pay for RIDB” or “AtW always pays 100% of RIDB.” The factsheet is “will consider paying up to 100%.” CSI is the only line that says AtW *pays* 100%.

BDF’s recruitment-toolkit wording still lists “those who need support workers such as sight guides or sign language interpreters” among groups AtW covers, which is consistent with the 100% support-worker rule, not a separate DeafBlind scheme.

## 116.3 The 2014 Sense file is the named precedent — and it is twelve years old

ATW0075 is usable as documentary evidence that, in 2014, AtW staff treated deafblind manual / hands-on as a *more expensive substitute for BSL* and pushed hours, rates and a salaried cap accordingly. It is not usable as a description of 2026 decision-making unless a fresh source says the same thing is still happening. This pass did not find that fresh named source.

What 2025–26 sources *do* show is the general cap-and-delay story already logged in 52–54 and 101: RNID survey (n=358, title includes deafblind), AWS0095, AWS0121 case AB (deaf BSL, seven-month renewal, award expired). Those stay in the BSL/AtW cells. They do not become RIDB-rarity findings by the word “deafblind” appearing in a sample sentence.

## 116.4 Employer-funded standard practice is unpublished

No UK employer policy, framework lot, or sector agreement was found that treats a workplace guide-communicator or a DeafBlind-specific alarm/braille package as a default, non-AtW adjustment. Charity provision (Sense CG, Deafblind Scotland GC, Deafblind UK Employability) is real and live-checked, and it is the wrong institutional actor for an *in-post employer* cell. Action Deafness employment support names Deafblind clients and AtW advice; it is a jobseeker/Connect-to-Work offer in Hertfordshire, not a national employer-funded CG contract.
