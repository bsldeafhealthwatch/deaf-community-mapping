# Prompt 46 — Legal and procedural basis for an AIS communication-need request or complaint

**Master index prompt 46.** Compiled 9 September 2026. First item in the Templates-section batch (prompts 46–51). Companion to the AIS legal brief (prompt 34). Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment; do not wear a DEI or anti-DEI banner.

**What this prompt is for.** Ground any future bracket-fill letter before a word of template is drafted. There is **no official, nationally prescribed form of words** a patient must use. A plausible-sounding legal phrase is not a verified one. Do not paste this brief into a live template.

**Scope.** England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run different complaints systems and (in Scotland) a tighter national interpreting policy — see prompt 36. This brief does not cover those.

**Anchor case (same as prompts 34–37).** A BSL Deaf patient attends a planned, non-emergency hospital follow-up. The trust system does not hold (or does not act on) a BSL flag. The patient is telephoned instead of texted, emailed or relayed. No in-person BSL interpreter is booked. A video relay link is offered instead.

---

## Headline

Three different legal layers can be cited in the same letter, and they do not all say the same thing. AIS (DAPB1605 v4.0) is a “must comply” information standard that requires the recorded need to be identified, flagged and **met promptly** by a qualified communication professional; it does **not** hard-default in-person BSL over VRI for every planned appointment. The Equality Act 2010 s.20 duty to provide an auxiliary aid is the stronger lever when the patient has already said they need in-person BSL and VRI would leave them at a substantial disadvantage. The NHS complaints regulations govern how a **past** failure is investigated; they are the wrong tool for an appointment that is still in the diary — that is a PALS / booking-desk operational request.

There is no published national template letter from NHS England that a patient sends the other way. NHS England’s only published example letter is a GP-surgery letter **to** patients asking them to declare needs.

## 46.2 The legal layers a letter can cite — what each one actually does

Cite the layer that matches the ask. Stacking all of them in a first request is usually unnecessary.

### Layer A — Accessible Information Standard (DAPB1605 v4.0)

Published under Health and Social Care Act 2012 s.250. Health and Care Act 2022 s.95 rewrote s.250 so a person to whom an information standard applies “must comply … except in so far as the requirement to comply is waived.” Commenced 7 July 2025 (SI 2025/807). Operational enforcement machinery has not been used in public against named NHS bodies (prompt 34).

**The six steps the organisation must run:** identify, record, flag, share, meet, review.

**Musts that matter for a letter:**

- Staff **must** identify and record needs at first or next interaction, using SNOMED CT (or the human-readable category) — requirement 1.14 / 1.16.
- Organisations **must** put flags or alerts on the record so staff are prompted to act — 1.24 / 1.25.
- Online records, where they exist, **must** let the person review the recorded need and request changes — 1.20. People **should** be able to verify accuracy — 1.19.
- Organisations **must** provide accessible contact methods — 1.29.
- Communication support **must** be provided promptly, “for example arranging interpreter support … without unreasonable delay” — 1.31. No number of days is published.
- BSL / deafblind interpreters **must** have appropriate qualifications, DBS, a professional code, and valid registration (NRCPD or RBSLI cited) — 1.32.
- Organisations **must** publish an accessible communication policy that includes a complaints / feedback process — 1.34 / 1.37.

**What AIS does not do:** it does not prescribe patient letter wording; it does not give a statutory booking lead-time; it does not say VRI is forbidden for planned care.

Commissioners (ICBs, NHS England) “must have regard” in the older page language and must write the Standard into contracts (requirement 2.1). NHS Standard Contract 2026/27 **SC12.3** is the contractual “Provider must comply with the Accessible Information Standard” clause — trusts and other Standard-Contract providers, not GMS/PMS GP practices.

### Layer B — Equality Act 2010

Service-providers (NHS trusts, GP practices, dentists, pharmacies, ambulance services, private providers of NHS care) must make reasonable adjustments (s.20) so a disabled person is not put at a substantial disadvantage. Three requirements: change a practice; change a physical feature; provide an **auxiliary aid or service** (s.20(5)). Where the requirement relates to information, reasonable steps include providing it in an accessible format (s.20(6)). Cost must not be passed to the disabled person (s.20(7)). The duty is anticipatory for services to the public (Part 3 / s.29).

A registered BSL interpreter is the classic auxiliary aid in the EHRC statutory code example. Whether *in-person* rather than *remote* is the reasonable adjustment is fact-specific: effectiveness, practicality, cost, and the person’s already-recorded need. CQC’s dental mythbuster is explicit that a dentist must not ask a Deaf patient to pay, and that refusal should rest on evidence that options were considered.

This layer is the one that can support “I need an in-person interpreter for this appointment, not VRI,” where AIS only says “offer the option.”

### Layer C — NHS Constitution + Health Act 2009

NHS bodies, ICBs, NHS England, primary-care providers and independent providers of NHS care must **have regard** to the NHS Constitution (Health Act 2009 s.2). NHS England and ICBs must also **promote** it (NHS Act 2006).

Relevant Constitution rights (current GOV.UK text):

- to be involved in planning and decisions and “to be given information and support to enable you to do this”
- of access to your own health records “and to have any factual inaccuracies corrected”
- to have any complaint acknowledged within three working days and properly investigated
- to discuss how the complaint will be handled and to know the likely period
- to take an unresolved complaint to the Health Service Commissioner (PHSO)

The Constitution does **not** itself create a free-standing right titled “in-person BSL interpreter.” Some ICB plain-English pages (e.g. Mid and South Essex) paraphrase “request accessible information under the Accessible Information Standard” — that is a local paraphrase, not the Constitution’s own wording.

### Layer D — UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018 (record correction only)

A request to correct a missing or wrong communication-need flag is also a request to rectify inaccurate personal data. Useful when the organisation already holds a record that says the wrong contact method. Not a substitute for AIS or the Equality Act when the issue is booking an interpreter.

## 46.3 Scenario 1 — Request that a communication need be flagged or corrected on the NHS record

This is a **records request**, not a complaint. Do not open with complaints-regulation language unless a previous request has already been ignored.

### Who to write to

| Setting | First addressee | Why |
|---|---|---|
| GP / primary care | Practice manager (copy reception / AIS lead if named on the practice website) | The GP record is the usual home of the coded need and the alert that outbound letters and referrals should pick up. EMIS / SystmOne local alert + SNOMED. |
| Hospital / community trust / mental-health trust | PALS **or** health-records / patient-experience team; copy the clinic that holds the next appointment | Trust PAS/EPR flag is local. Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag (DAPB4019) on Spine is a related but separate standard (health conformance 31 Mar 2027 / social care 31 Oct 2027; NHS England checklist page still says 30 Sep 2026 — hold both dates, prompt 34). |
| Cross-organisation | Ask the holder of the record you have just used (the last clinic, the GP) to record **and** to include the need on the next referral / discharge. e-RS guidance tells the referrer to put accessibility needs in a prominent position. | AIS “share” step is process guidance, not a guaranteed sticky flag across every PAS. |

A third party writing on the patient’s behalf should enclose written consent. NHS.uk is explicit that consent speeds the process; without it the body will usually pause.

### Suggested citation stack for scenario 1 (not template wording)

1. Accessible Information Standard DAPB1605 v4.0, requirements 1.14–1.16 and 1.24–1.26 (identify, record, flag) and 1.19–1.20 (review / request change).
2. Health and Social Care Act 2012 s.250 as amended (information standard; “must comply” from 7 July 2025).
3. NHS Constitution right to have factual inaccuracies on the health record corrected.
4. Equality Act 2010 s.20 only if the missing flag is already causing a substantial disadvantage (wrong contact method, no interpreter booked). Not required on a first clean request.

### Escalation if the first request is ignored

PALS (hospital) or a second letter to the practice manager (GP) → formal complaint to the **responsible body** (see 46.5) → PHSO after local resolution is exhausted or has run six months. Parallel: ICB as commissioner (you cannot run provider and commissioner formal complaints on the same matter at the same time — NHS England / NHS.uk). CQC is not an AIS assurer and is not the complaints body.

## 46.4 Scenario 2 — Request an in-person BSL interpreter for a specific upcoming appointment rather than VRI

This is an **operational booking request**. Speed matters more than legal citation. A formal complaint will not be investigated before next Tuesday’s clinic.

### Who to write to

- The department / clinic that holds the appointment (booking team, medical secretary, outpatient coordinator).
- Copy PALS at a trust if the appointment is close or a previous booking failed.
- GP: practice manager / reception, with the date and time of the appointment in the first line.
- Do not address a named consultant as the “AIS defendant.” Name the service.

Give enough notice. There is no published national minimum booking window for NHS BSL. AIS only says “promptly” / “without unreasonable delay.” Local agency lead-times vary; that is a fulfilment fact (see the SWL cost / agency work), not a statutory figure.

### What the body is obliged to do

### Correct legal framing — and the framing that would be wrong

**Defensible:** “My recorded need is an in-person BSL interpreter. AIS DAPB1605 requires you to meet that need promptly with a registered communication professional. Your 2025 implementation guidance says remote interpretation is not a total replacement and that for routine care I should be given the option of face-to-face. Under Equality Act 2010 s.20 you must take reasonable steps to provide an auxiliary aid. VRI would put me at a substantial disadvantage for this appointment because [one short factual reason — e.g. length, consent discussion, visual examination, previous VRI failure]. Please confirm in writing, by [date that is before the appointment], that a registered in-person BSL interpreter has been booked.”

**Not defensible as a statement of law:** “AIS legally requires an in-person interpreter and forbids VRI.” That sentence is not in the Standard.

Scotland’s national interpreting policy is the tighter F2F-default comparator (prompt 36). Do not cite Scottish policy as if it bound an English trust.

### Escalation if ignored before the appointment

1. PALS / practice manager same day — this is the route NHS.uk itself flags for urgent operational problems.
2. Ask the clinic to postpone rather than proceed without support (consent / Equality Act risk sits with the provider if they proceed).
3. After the appointment, if it went ahead without support → scenario 3.
4. Formal complaint and PHSO are backward-looking. They do not book next week’s interpreter.

## 46.5 Scenario 3 — Complaint that a past appointment proceeded without correct communication support

This is a **formal NHS complaint** (or a PALS concern first). The Complaints Regulations, not AIS, set the process.

### Informal first, then formal

| Step | What it is | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Front-line / clinic manager | Conversation or email to the service that ran the appointment | Same day / next day if the problem is still live |
| PALS | Informal, confidential, not the statutory complaints procedure. NHS.uk: useful when the issue is urgent. Available in most hospitals; patchy in general practice. | Want a practical fix or an explanation without opening a formal file |
| Formal complaint | Local Authority Social Services and National Health Service Complaints (England) Regulations 2009 | Want a written investigation, apology, and recorded finding |
| ICB as commissioner | Alternative addressee for the *same* matter — you cannot run provider and commissioner investigations simultaneously | Use if you do not want to complain to the provider, or the provider is the problem |
| PHSO | Second and last NHS stage. Health Service Commissioners Act 1993 s.4: local procedure should normally be invoked and exhausted first. PHSO will also look if local resolution has taken more than six months. From 2023 PHSO triages out “relatively minor” impact (annoyance, one-off inconvenience). | After the local final response, or after six months with no response |

NHS complaints advocacy is free and independent (local provider; VoiceAbility is the national brand often cited). An advocate can help write the letter and attend meetings; they cannot give legal advice or make the complaint in their own name.

### Who the “responsible body” is

| What went wrong | First formal addressee | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital / community / mental-health trust appointment | Trust complaints team (often via PALS). Many trusts ask that formal letters go to the chief executive’s office; the complaints team still investigates. | Standard Contract SC12.3 + AIS + Equality Act all bite here |
| GP appointment | Practice manager / practice complaints lead | GMS/PMS practice, not SC12.3. Equality Act and AIS still apply. ICB is the alternative addressee |
| Dentist / pharmacy / optician | The practice, or the ICB | Same dual-route rule |
| More than one organisation | Write once; duty to co-operate produces a single coordinated response | Complaints Regulations reg.9 |
| Specialised / military / prison | NHS England, not the local ICB | NHS.uk / NHS England complaints page |

### What a scenario-3 letter needs to cite

Enough to identify the duty that was not met, not a barrister’s pleading:

- What happened (date, service, appointment type).
- What had already been recorded or requested (flag, previous email, “I use BSL”).
- What support was / was not in the room (no interpreter / VRI only / family used).
- The duty: AIS DAPB1605 requirements 1.29–1.32 (contact method, prompt support, registered professional) and/or Equality Act 2010 s.20 auxiliary-aid duty.
- What the person wants now: apology, the flag correcting, confirmation that the next appointment will have an in-person registered interpreter, staff briefing — be specific.
- That this is a formal complaint under the 2009 Regulations, and that the response should explain the right to the PHSO.

Do not name an individual nurse or receptionist as the wrongdoer in the opening line. Name the trust or the practice. Individual-naming risk is the subject of prompt 49.

### Parallel routes that are *not* the NHS complaints ladder

- CQC: can take action if communication needs are not met, but “the AIS is not assured by CQC, that is the role of the commissioner” (CQC, page updated 10 Mar 2026). A CQC report is not a substitute for local resolution if the person wants an apology or a corrected flag.
- County court / sheriff court Equality Act claim. Pre-action language belongs after local resolution has failed, not in a first request (prompt 48).
- PHSO named findings already on the dossier (prompt 37): Alan Graham (UHB, Feb 2026), “Samantha” GP vaccine mix-up (Aug 2026), Mrs E (2014, pre-AIS), UHB Mr X CQC FPNs (2022, consent not AIS). Useful as “this pattern has been upheld,” not as a citation of law.

## 46.6 Correct escalation path in one line

**Request (flag or upcoming interpreter):** the service that holds the record or the appointment → PALS / practice manager if ignored or the date is close → only then a formal complaint.

**Complaint about a past appointment:** optional PALS → formal complaint to the provider *or* the ICB (not both) → written local response → PHSO. Time limits: 12 months to complain locally; 3 working days to be acknowledged; 6-month statutory backstop; PHSO after the local final response (or after six months with none), normally within a year of becoming aware.

## 46.8 What a later template may safely *point at* — and what it must not invent

Safe to point at, once prompt 47 has checked that a stronger existing letter does not already exist:

- The numbered AIS requirements above, with the Standard’s name and year.
- Equality Act 2010 s.20 as the reason an in-person interpreter can be requested where VRI would not remove the disadvantage.
- The 2009 Regulations and the NHS.uk complaints page for process and timescales.
- PALS as the urgent operational route.
- Consent when writing on someone else’s behalf.

Must not invent:

- A statutory “25 working day” reply deadline.
- “AIS makes VRI unlawful for planned appointments.”
- A named individual as the non-compliant officer.
- A national booking-lead-time in days.
- “CQC will enforce this letter.”
- Scottish F2F-default policy as if it bound an English body.

Prompt 47 is the next gate: catalogue what BDA, SignHealth, RNID, Citizens Advice and NHS-published patient letters already do, before any new letter is drafted. Prompt 49 is the hard “do not put this in a public template” constraint list.
