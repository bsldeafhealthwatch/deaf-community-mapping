# Prompt 36 — Comparative and international good practice on flagging and interpreter-booking defaults

**Master index prompt 36.** Compiled 8 September 2026 from the AIS working brief (prompts 34–37). Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment; do not wear a DEI or anti-DEI banner. Cream / MODELLED labels are estimates, not measured series.

**Anchor case.** A BSL Deaf patient attends a planned, non-emergency hospital follow-up. The trust system does not hold (or does not act on) a BSL flag. The patient is telephoned instead of texted, emailed or relayed. No in-person BSL interpreter is booked. A video relay link is offered instead.

This prompt asks which systems elsewhere solve persistent communication-need flagging and default-to-in-person interpreter booking for planned / non-emergency appointments.

---

## Headline

England already has the **design** for a sticky flag (AIS coded fields plus the Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag on Spine). The gap is conformance and workflow, not the absence of a standard. The closest transferable *rule* for planned-care interpreting is **Scotland** (face-to-face as preferred and main method; video bounded). The closest transferable *enforcement path* is the **US complaint-led OCR model**, not a national EHR flag. No peer-reviewed before/after DNA-rate paper for a face-to-face default was found in this pass — treat any such claim as MODELLED until a named study is attached.

---

## 36.1 Health systems with a legally mandated, cross-provider “sticky” flag

### England (already specified — not delivered)

AIS requires SNOMED-coded needs, a visible flag, and sharing on referral “where possible”. DAPB4019 (Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag) puts a disability-adjustment flag on Spine / National Care Records Service so it can be seen wherever the person is treated. AIS and RADF are **related, not interchangeable**. Both can be live and the patient can still be telephoned.

### Australian Capital Territory — patient-held flag (February 2026)

Canberra Health Services’ Digital Health Record / **MyDHR** lets people record and edit their own disability and access needs, including communication, visible to staff. DeafACT called self-edit of communication needs “a huge step forward”. Published examples of the adjustments staff then make include SMS or email rather than phone calls, visual alerts in waiting rooms, and booking an Auslan interpreter.

**Translates to NHS as:** the 2025 AIS sixth step already says online records must let people review and request changes. Putting that in the NHS App, and making the field staff-visible across providers, is the English equivalent. It does not by itself book the interpreter.

**Source**

- People can now identify their disability, access needs for care on their Digital Health Record — Region Canberra, 16 February 2026. <https://region.com.au/people-can-now-identify-their-disability-access-needs-for-care-on-their-digital-health-record/940994/>

### United States — duty without a single national flag

There is no single national EHR “sticky AIS flag”. Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, the ADA, and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act impose an effective-communication duty on almost every provider that takes federal money. Enforcement is complaint-led via HHS Office for Civil Rights, with settlement agreements that force hospitals to maintain interpreter-booking logs and flag communication needs in the record.

**Translates to NHS as:** the lesson is the enforcement path (logs, named coordinator, settlements), not the data standard. England already has the data standard.

**Sources**

- Guidance on Nondiscrimination in Telehealth — HHS OCR and DOJ CRT, 2022. <https://web.archive.org/web/20231008100116/https://www.hhs.gov/civil-rights/for-individuals/disability/guidance-on-nondiscrimination-in-telehealth/index.html>
- 45 CFR §92.201 and 45 CFR Part 84 Subpart H — qualified interpreters and VRI technical standards. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-84/subpart-H>

### Australia NDIA PACE — booking guidance inside the operational IT

The National Disability Insurance Agency’s PACE system includes staff guidance and prompts for preferred communication method and interpreter booking. Not a health-record flag that follows the person into hospital. Useful as a worked example of booking guidance sitting **inside the operational IT**, not in a PDF.

**Source**

- Recommendation 6.3 — Access to appropriately skilled and qualified interpreters — Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, Disability Royal Commission progress report 2025. <https://www.health.gov.au/resources/publications/disability-royal-commission-progress-report-2025/volume-6-enabling-autonomy-and-access/recommendation-63-access-to-appropriately-skilled-and-qualified-interpreters>

## 36.2 Jurisdictions where in-person sign language is the planned-care default

### Scotland — closest transferable rule-set

Public Health Scotland *Interpreting, Communication Support and Translation National Policy*:

- Face-to-face BSL is “the preferred and main method”.
- Video interpreting may be used in an emergency, for short routine appointments (bloods, immunisations) where the BSL user has agreed in advance, or while waiting for a face-to-face interpreter.
- A face-to-face interpreter should still be sought as soon as possible in emergencies even when online is available.
- Contact Scotland BSL is a 24/7 VRS for making contact with public services. It is **not** a substitute for clinical face-to-face interpreting.

Enforcement sits in board policy plus the BSL (Scotland) Act 2015 national and local plans, not an AIS clone. This is the sentence that could be written into an English AIS implementation note or an ICB contract schedule without new primary legislation.

**Source**

- Interpreting, Communication Support and Translation National Policy — Public Health Scotland / NHS Scotland, sections 4.3.3–4.3.5. <https://publichealthscotland.scot/media/3059/interpreting-communication-support-and-translation-national-policy.pdf>

### United States — “primary consideration” (Title II)

ADA Title II (state and local government health providers) requires **primary consideration** of the disabled person’s requested auxiliary aid. If the person asks for in-person ASL, the public provider must honour it unless it can show another equally effective means. Title III (private clinics) is a weaker “effective communication” test and is where VRI disputes cluster.

HHS/DOJ telehealth guidance (2022) still requires the platform to admit an interpreter. When VRI is used, four technical quality tests apply: real-time full-motion video; an image large enough to show face, arms, hands and fingers; clear audio; staff trained on the kit.

**Translates to NHS as:** “primary consideration of the recorded preference” is tighter than AIS 2025’s “offer a choice for routine care”. It would close the grey zone in the anchor case.

**Sources**

- Guidance on Nondiscrimination in Telehealth — HHS OCR and DOJ CRT, 2022 (URL above).
- 45 CFR Part 84 Subpart H (URL above).

### Australia — on-site for complex / planned; do not import the payment model

TIS National recommends on-site interpreting for sensitive, complex or planned group work; VRI exists. A February 2025 Department of Health review of Auslan in primary care found free Auslan only guaranteed for people aged 65+, with younger users paying around A$245 or rationing NDIS hours.

**Do not import the payment model. Do import the explicit “on-site for complex / planned” recommendation.**

**Source**

- Review of Auslan interpreting service use in primary care — Australian Department of Health, February 2025. <https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-02/review-of-auslan-interpreting-service-use-in-primary-care.pdf>

### New Zealand / EU / EUD

No jurisdiction-level “in-person default for planned appointments, video as emergency-only” health standard, with a published compliance measure, was located in this pass beyond the Scotland and US Title II texts above. European Union of the Deaf material is advocacy and rights-framing, not an operational booking default with measured compliance. Mark as **not found in this pass**, not as “does not exist”.

## 36.3 Research comparing safety or DNA rates before and after such a policy

**Not found in this pass.** No peer-reviewed paper was located that isolates a “face-to-face default” or “sticky flag” policy and then publishes Deaf missed-appointment (DNA) rates or harm rates before and after.

Claims that introducing a default “reduced missed appointments” should be treated as **MODELLED or anecdotal** until a named paper is attached.

Still Ignored and Locked Out quantify current UK failure (80% of sign-language users had a booked professional fail to attend; 70% never asked about needs). They are not before/after evaluations of a policy change.

## 36.4 Technical solutions that stop the flag being lost between referrals or departments

| Approach | What it does | Fit for NHS / AIS |
|---|---|---|
| SNOMED-coded AIS subsets + mandated inclusion in the e-RS referral letter + Additional Requirements Manager at the receiving trust | Stops the need dying at the GP–trust boundary *if used* | Already specified in England. Failure is conformance. |
| HL7 FHIR terminology services (CSIRO Ontoserver / Australian National Clinical Terminology Service model) | Keeps the code set identical across vendors so “BSL interpreter needed” entered in EMIS is still that code in the acute EPR | England already uses SNOMED. Missing piece is vendor display rules that prevent the alert being one of forty ignored banners. |
| Patient-portal write-back (ACT MyDHR; NHS App sixth AIS step) | A flag the patient can see and correct is harder for a department to lose | Specified in AIS 2025; not yet evidenced as working across SWL. |
| Booking confirmation to the patient in the accessible channel, with interpreter name / NRCPD number | Cuts the “told it was booked, nobody came” problem | Locked Out priority 1. Still Ignored: only 32% of sign-language users get any confirmation; 68% want to book the interpreter themselves online. |

**Sources**

- Accessible Information Standard in the NHS e-Referral Service — NHS England Digital. <https://digital.nhs.uk/services/e-referral-service/document-library/meeting-the-accessible-information-standard>
- Clinical terminology and HL7 FHIR — Australian e-Health Research Centre / CSIRO. <https://aehrc.csiro.au/research/data-and-interoperability/clinical-terminology/>
- Locked Out full report — GOV.UK, 27 November 2025 (URL in prompt 35).
- Still Ignored 2025 (URL in prompt 35).

## What would translate usefully into strengthening AIS in the NHS

Ranked by closeness to existing instruments, not by campaign preference.

1. Write the **Scotland sentence** into English implementation guidance and ICB contract schedules: for planned, bookable appointments, in-person BSL is the default whenever the recorded need says BSL interpreter; VRI only with documented patient agreement or while waiting for the booked interpreter.
2. Adopt **ADA Title II “primary consideration”** of the recorded preference — tighter than “offer a choice”.
3. Make the interpreter booking **visible to the patient** in the NHS App, with NRCPD name.
4. Use **s.95 / s.251ZA** (now in force — see prompt 34) to require publication of AIS self-assessments and to ask named providers for the documents that show flags are acted on.
5. Fix **vendor alert design** so the communication flag is not drowned by other EPR banners. Healthwatch Kingston already recorded this failure mode in SWL EMIS.

Do not import Australia’s under-65 Auslan payment gap. Do not claim a DNA-rate improvement that has not been measured.
