# Prompt 100 — International comparative practice on adjustment timescales and contract-type priority

**Master index prompt 100.** International-context file in the Internal HR/IT SLA batch (prompts 97–102). Compiled 10 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment. Label every cell **international**. None of it fills a UK data cell in 97–99 or 101–102.

**What this prompt is for.** Comparator timescales and any documented policy that does (or refuses to) distinguish service-priority by contract type.

**Index check.** Do not paste ADA “undue delay” case law into a UK Equality Act cell. Australia’s 21-day Fair Work *flexible-working* reply is not a Disability Discrimination Act fulfilment clock. New Zealand’s one-month flexible-work reply is the same trap.

---

## Headline

No comparator in the asked set — United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, or the EU Employment Equality Directive — gives **private employers a statutory number of days to fulfil an agreed workplace adjustment.** The pattern is the UK’s: duty yes, days-clock no.

What exists instead:

- **United States (ADA / Rehab Act).** Statute has no days figure. EEOC Enforcement Guidance: unnecessary delay can itself be a failure to accommodate. Courts have treated three months as “no long delay” in one D.C. footnote, six months as capable of supporting a claim in the Fifth Circuit (2025), and multi-year waits as too long. **Federal agencies** under Executive Order 13164 must *write a time period into their own procedures*. Published examples cluster around **15–20 business days to decide**, then a further window to implement (Commerce: implement within 10 business days of approval). That is a federal-employer procedure, not a private-sector statute.
- **Canada.** Canadian Human Rights Commission: communicate “in a timely manner”; “excessive delays” can be a failure. No days figure in the federal guide. Provincial commissions say “promptly.”
- **Australia.** Disability Discrimination Act 1992: no response or fulfilment deadline. JobAccess templates ask a policy to name “who is responsible … within an appropriate timeframe” without setting the number. The **21-day written reply** is the *Fair Work Act* National Employment Standards **flexible-working** clock — a different legal product, with a 12-month service qualifier that casuals can meet if the work is regular.
- **New Zealand.** Human Rights Act 1993: no RA days-clock. Flexible-work reply is one month. A commercial guide attributes a 10-working-day acknowledgement recommendation to the Human Rights Commission; the official HRC reasonable-accommodation pages opened this pass say “timely,” not 10 days.
- **Ireland.** Employment Equality Acts: “appropriate measures,” no days. Complaint clock to the Workplace Relations Commission is six months from the last act.
- **EU.** Directive 2000/78/EC requires reasonable accommodation; the Commission’s May 2024 employer guidelines do not set days. Do not borrow written-reply deadlines from other directives (working-conditions, parental leave) and relabel them as RA clocks.

**Contract-type priority.** The only sourced document that squarely addresses staffing-firm vs client duty is **US EEOC guidance on contingent workers**. Both can be joint employers; **both** must provide a needed on-the-job accommodation absent undue hardship; they should put cost-sharing in the contract. It does **not** say serve permanent employees first. No Canada / Australia / NZ / EU / Ireland published SLA was found that ranks in-house tickets above agency tickets, or that explicitly says “we refuse to distinguish.” Equal-duty language is not a measured same-queue finding.

**Hard-don't.** Do not tell a UK reader that “the ADA gives 15 days.” That is an EO 13164 *agency procedure example*, not the ADA. Do not tell them Australia has a 21-day RA law.

## 100.1 What transfers, and what must not

The transferable observation is structural, not numerical. Every asked jurisdiction treats delay as evidence that can support a failure-to-accommodate finding, and none writes a private-employer days-clock into the primary statute. The UK WPC two-week *response* proposal (prompt 97–98) would be the outlier if enacted, and even that is a reply clock, not fulfilment.

What must not transfer:

- EO 13164’s 15- or 20-day federal *examples* into a UK private-employer cell.
- Fifth Circuit six-month “can support a claim” into “the US limit is six months.”
- Australia or New Zealand flexible-working reply clocks into DDA/HRA adjustment law.
- EEOC joint-employer guidance into a finding that UK agencies and hirers already run one ticket. Prompt 99 already recorded that the UK has not published that architecture.

## 100.2 Contract type

The US is the only asked jurisdiction with a dedicated regulator product on staffing-firm accommodations. Its rule is **shared duty**, not ranked queues. Cost and control can sit with different entities (a ramp on the client’s floor; a screen-reader licence the firm can buy). That is the same split-duty shape as UK EA s.41 / Schedule 8, expressed in ADA joint-employer language.

No asked jurisdiction produced a published MSP or shared-services SLA that says agency disability tickets wait behind payroll employees. Absence here matches prompt 99’s UK absence. Do not fill either gap with the other.

## 100.3 Unpublished or contested

- Measured private-employer median days in the US, Canada or Australia: **not found** as an official series (no BDF/TUC analogue opened this pass).
- NZ 10-working-day acknowledgement: secondary attribution only.
- Australia commercial “two weeks”: same.
- Member-state gold-plating of Directive 2000/78 with a national days-clock: not opened country-by-country this pass; Ireland, the closest EU employment-equality cousin in the dossier, has none.
- Sign-language interpreter booking SLAs inside named US federal or Canadian federal employers: not pulled this pass.
