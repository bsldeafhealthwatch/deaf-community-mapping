## Headline

The Equality Act 2010 duty to make reasonable adjustments sits with the **employer**, not with the occupational-health provider. OH is an adviser the employer may commission. ACAS (page last updated 17 December 2025) is explicit: after an assessment, “it’s up to the employer to decide whether to put in place the recommendations.” The Society of Occupational Medicine’s 2026 employer/OH guidance says the same thing in different words: only a tribunal can rule on disability status; the OH report “may include” wording such as “this may be a helpful adjustment for the employer to consider, subject to operational feasibility.” HSE’s own disability-adjustments page tells employers they “can seek help” from occupational health — it does not transfer the s.20 duty to the clinician.

No published Faculty of Occupational Medicine *Good Occupational Medical Practice* (2017, still the live FOM page) or SEQOHS / FOM ethics edition located on this pass contains a BSL-specific assessment standard, template or competency for Deaf or DeafBlind employees. SOM’s 2026 reasonable-adjustments guidance is general Equality Act / absence-management text. The closest professional prompt to BSL inside the OH world is a January 2023 Business Disability Forum slide deck to SOM, which told occupational-health practitioners: “Know where to get a British Sign Language (BSL) interpreter so that you can respond quickly if an employee requests this.” That is access to the *assessment appointment*, not a Deaf night-shift or lone-working protocol.

Fire safety is a different legal object. The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 puts the fire-risk-assessment duty on the **responsible person** for the premises. The Secretary of State’s supplementary guide *Fire safety risk assessment: means of escape for disabled people* (published 29 March 2007; last updated 12 September 2023) recommends flashing beacons and vibrating pagers for people with hearing impairment, a Personal Emergency Evacuation Plan (PEEP), and — in the PEEP interview questions — “Do you work alone in the building? Do you work out of hours?” BS 5839-1:2025 clause 17 covers fire-alarm warnings for people who are Deaf or have a hearing impairment (visual alarm devices plus portable / vibrating devices). None of that is an OH statutory product. None of it names BSL as a language. None of it is a night-shift or lone-working protocol written for Deaf staff.

**Hard-don't for anything public.** Absence of a BSL-named OH standard is not a finding that OH, or an employer, is in breach. Do not print “duty of care breach”, “non-compliant OH report”, or “unlawful lone-working practice” from this file.

## 92.1 The duty sits with the employer

Part 5 of the Equality Act 2010 applies to work. Sections 20 and 21 create the duty to take reasonable steps to avoid the substantial disadvantage caused by a provision, criterion or practice, a physical feature, or the absence of an auxiliary aid. The person who must take those steps is the employer (and certain principals). An occupational-health provider is typically a contractor giving advice under a service agreement. That advice can be evidence of what the employer knew, or ought to have known. It is not a second, parallel statutory duty running against the clinician.

ACAS and SOM both write the decision-right onto the employer. That is the gate for prompts 93 and 94: a thin or silent OH report on night-shift or lone-working is a *practice* finding, not automatically a finding that OH has broken a statute.

## 92.2 What OH standards actually say about Deaf / BSL

The live professional stack is generalist:

- FOM *Good Occupational Medical Practice* 2017 — communication and partnership in the GMC sense, not a BSL competency.
- SOM *Disability, Discrimination and Reasonable Adjustments* V1 (2026) — Equality Act mechanics and absence management. Hearing is not split out from other impairments.
- SOM’s noise / audiogram products (May 2023 position statement; May/September 2024 audiogram supplementary guidance) are about **occupational noise-induced hearing loss surveillance**, which is a different clinical object from a BSL-using employee’s workplace communication and emergency access.
- SEQOHS accredits OH *services*, not a Deaf-specific pathway.

The January 2023 BDF-to-SOM slide is the only sourced professional instruction located on this pass that names BSL inside an OH room: book an interpreter for the appointment. That is necessary and it is not sufficient for the anchor scenario (home / night / lone working).

## 92.3 Fire safety is a premises duty, not an OH product

The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 duty is on the responsible person. The 2007/2023 means-of-escape-for-disabled-people guide is the document that actually names flashing beacons, vibrating pagers, PEEPs and the lone / out-of-hours interview questions. BS 5839-1:2025 clause 17 is the alarm-system design recommendation. An OH report that says “visual fire alarm” is echoing that premises stack; it is not creating it.

A visual or vibrating alarm is the documented, citable provision beyond “just shout”. It is **not** a documented requirement that the employer install a BSL fire-warden protocol or a text-based lone-worker check-in. Those remain adjustment questions under s.20, decided case by case.

## 92.4 What is unpublished or contested

- No FOM or SOM BSL/Deaf assessment template.
- No published national OH-provider protocol for Deaf night-shift or lone working (prompt 94).
- PAS 79 is often still named in secondary blogs as if it were live; the housing part was withdrawn in 2021.
- Seafarer / Approved Doctor hearing standards (MCA manual, July 2020) are a **sector fitness-to-work** product for radio and alarm-hearing at sea. Do not paste them into a general-office OH cell.

Northern Ireland remains on the Disability Discrimination Act 1995 (as amended) for employment, not the Equality Act. Do not print an England s.20 sentence as if it were the NI rule.
