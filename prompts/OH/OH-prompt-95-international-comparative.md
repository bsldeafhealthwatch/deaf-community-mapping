## Headline

No comparator jurisdiction was found with a single national occupational-health *standard* written for Deaf or deafblind night-shift and lone-working as a combined product. What exists internationally is a **fire-alarm / employee-alarm layer** plus a **reasonable-accommodation layer**:

- **United States.** OSHA 29 CFR 1910.165 says the employee alarm “shall be capable of being perceived above ambient noise or light levels by all employees” and that “tactile devices may be used to alert those employees who would not otherwise be able to recognize the audible or visual alarm.” OSHA’s 2005 Safety and Health Information Bulletin on emergency-evacuation for employees with disabilities lists strobes and vibrating pagers as meeting that standard. ADA / EEOC employment guidance lists “appropriate emergency notification systems (for example, strobe lighting on fire alarms or vibrating pagers)” as a typical reasonable accommodation. ADA Standards §215 require visible alarms in new or altered public- and common-use spaces; employee work areas need only be *wired so that* visible alarms can be added later. That is a building-code-plus-accommodation model, not an OH clinic protocol for night work.
- **Australia.** JobAccess (live 2025) lists visual alerts and paging devices as workplace adjustments. NSW SIRA’s hearing-loss toolkit lists a vibrating pager for fire/evacuation. Advocacy (AAD 2025 employer guide) adds buddy systems and written emergency procedures. Safe Work Australia’s noise code is about *preventing* hearing loss and hearing warning signals above ambient noise — a different object. No National Occupational Health standard for Deaf lone workers was found.
- **Canada.** Provincial human-rights and fire-code rules apply. *Strudwick v Applied Consumer & Clinical Evaluations* (Ontario 2015) is a named employment case in which a newly deaf employee’s refused requests included a visual fire alarm and a vibrating pager; the court described the employer’s conduct as “horrendous.” That is litigation evidence of the same kit, not a national OH standard.
- **EU / other.** No EU-wide occupational-health directive was found that names sign-language users on night or lone shifts. National fire-alarm standards (EN 54-23 visual alarm devices) are the transferable technical layer; the UK already uses that family via BS 5839-1:2025 (prompt 92).

Named kit that recurs across jurisdictions: strobe / visual alarm devices, vibrating pagers or wearable receivers, bed-shakers in sleeping accommodation, buddy systems, text/app check-in. None of that transfers into a UK legal duty by itself.

## 95.1 What “standard” would have looked like, and what was actually found

A transferable *standard* would name the worker population, the working pattern (night, lone, mobile), the acceptable check-in channels, and the duty-holder. OSHA 1910.165 comes closest on the alarm channel (audible, visual or tactile; perceivable by all employees). It still does not write a lone-worker welfare-check rule for Deaf staff. EEOC writes the same kit as an accommodation example. Australia writes it as JobAccess / insurer toolkit language. That is three jurisdictions converging on strobes and pagers, not one jurisdiction standing up a Deaf night-work OH pathway.

## 95.2 Kit that is documented as adopted, not mandated as a single product

Wearable vibrating receivers (watch or belt-clip), panel-linked strobes, and buddy systems appear in US vendor and EEOC material, Australian JobAccess/SIRA/AAD material, and the UK RNID/PEEP stack (prompts 93–94). Adoption is employer-by-employer. No regulator was found that mandates a named manufacturer.

## 95.3 What does not transfer

- ADA Title I 15-employee threshold and “undue hardship” test.
- OSHA 1910.165 as if it were an HSE Approved Code of Practice.
- Ontario damages in *Strudwick* as a UK quantum or liability finding.
- Safe Work Australia noise-exposure numbers as a Deaf-communication finding.
- Fire-fighter / rail “must hear the alarm” medical standards (US NFPA 1582; Australian rail medical) — those are fitness-to-deploy rules for safety-critical hearing of auditory cues, the opposite problem from a BSL office or care worker who cannot use a phone check-in.
