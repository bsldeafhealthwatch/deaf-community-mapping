# Prompt 42 — Legal risk, liability, and right of reply when naming public bodies

**Master index prompt 42.** Fifth file in the Deaf Access Gap Monitor / BSL Policy Maker scoping batch (prompts 38–45). Compiled 8–9 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier. Gaps are logged as gaps. This file is research, not legal advice.

**What this prompt is for.** Treat legal risk and right-of-reply as a *cost line*, not a footnote. Prompt 41 already said Climate Emergency UK built reply in because scoring a public body in public *is* the product. This file asks what UK law actually does when someone publishes a “compliance gap” or “non-compliance” claim about a named NHS trust or local authority; whether any civic-tech cousin has been sued, threatened, or forced into a correction; and which precautions those cousins took that cost time or money.

**Limitation.** No solicitor signed this. Derbyshire and the Defamation Act 2013 are public case law and statute. Application to a specific sentence about a named trust is a reserved legal question.

---

## Headline

A local authority **cannot** sue for libel. That is House of Lords authority from 1993, restated in Scotland by statute in 2021. Individual officers **can**. Universities **can**. WhatDoTheyKnow’s published position is that they remove contested material at the pre-action letter even when they think they have a defence, except where there are *no* legal grounds — dead people, or public bodies under *Derbyshire*.

No documented case was found of Climate Emergency UK, TheyWorkForYou, FixMyStreet or Full Fact being taken to a defamation *judgment* for scoring or describing a named council or NHS body. The documented cost is not damages. It is **pre-action letters, reactive takedown, volunteer-hours on right of reply, and the decision not to fight**.

The dangerous sentence is not “Wandsworth Council has no published BSL fulfilment series.” The dangerous sentence is “Councillor X / Chief Executive Y illegally failed to comply.” The product design that copies the cousins therefore (a) names the *body*, (b) cites a source for every cell, (c) offers a right of reply before the score goes public, and (d) does not let current officers mark their own homework.

NHS foundation trusts are bodies corporate and are not the Crown. Whether *Derbyshire* covers them as cleanly as it covers a county council is **not a decided case in this search**. Log that as a gap, not as a clearance.

## 42.1 What the law actually says about naming the body

### Local authorities and “organs of government” cannot sue for libel

*Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers Ltd* [1993] AC 534, [1993] UKHL 18 (18 February 1993). The Sunday Times questioned investments from the council’s superannuation fund. The House of Lords held unanimously that a local authority has no common-law right to sue for defamation to protect its governing or administrative reputation. Lord Keith: it is “contrary to the public interest” that organs of government should have that right; the threat of a civil action is a “chill factor” on speech. The decision overruled *Bognor Regis UDC v Campion* [1972]. Individual councillors and officers keep their personal right to sue if *their* reputation is impaired.

- National Case Law Archive summary. <https://www.lawcases.net/cases/derbyshire-cc-v-times-newspapers-ltd-1993-ukhl-18-18-february-1993/>
- swarb.co.uk, HL 18 Feb 1993. <https://swarb.co.uk/derbyshire-county-council-v-times-newspapers-ltd-and-others-hl-18-feb-1993/>

Scotland put the same principle into statute: **Defamation and Malicious Publication (Scotland) Act 2021 s.2**, “Prohibition on public authorities bringing proceedings,” with the explanatory notes citing *Derbyshire* at p.547. Royal Assent 21 April 2021.

- Explanatory notes, paragraph 16. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2021/10/notes/paragraph/16>

### Individuals can still sue

*Derbyshire* is explicit. A scorecard that writes “the Director of Adult Social Services at X illegally refused BSL” is a different claim from “X Council has not published a BSL fulfilment figure.” The first names a natural person. Defamation Act 2013 s.1 then requires **serious harm** to that person’s reputation. s.2 truth, s.3 honest opinion, s.4 public-interest defence (reasonable belief that publication was in the public interest) are the statutory defences. s.4 abolished the common-law *Reynolds* defence and replaced it with the statutory test.

- Defamation Act 2013 s.4. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/4>
- Explanatory notes to s.4: based on *Reynolds* / *Flood*; subjective belief plus objective reasonableness; allowance for editorial judgement. <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/section/4/notes>

*Serafin v Malkiewicz* [2020] UKSC 23 is the Supreme Court’s reading of s.4: do not treat *Reynolds* factors as a checklist; ask whether the statement was on a matter of public interest and whether the publisher reasonably believed publication was in the public interest.

### Universities can sue — and WhatDoTheyKnow treats them as the live exception

*Duke v University of Salford* [2013] EWHC 196 (QB). The High Court rejected the argument that *Derbyshire* stops a university suing. Universities receive public money and are regulated; they are not organs of government. The university’s own claim was then struck out as abuse of process because the words were really about two officers, not the institution — but the capacity point stands.

mySociety’s 2022 SLAPP consultation response states this as an operational problem: universities are FOI authorities, but they “have a different status under libel law.” Moderators tell requesters they must be more careful phrasing a university request than a council request. mySociety asked for legislation to put universities back in line with other public authorities. No such statute was found on this pass.

- Brodies note on *Duke*. <https://brodies.com/insights/government-and-public-sector/the-right-to-sue-public-authorities-and-defamation/>
- mySociety SLAPP response, 2022 (HTML 2023). <https://research.mysociety.org/html/slapp-response/>

### NHS trusts and foundation trusts — gap, not clearance

NHS foundation trusts are bodies corporate under Chapter 5 of Part 2 of the National Health Service Act 2006. *Derby Teaching Hospitals NHS FT & Ors v Derby City Council* [2019] EWHC 3436 (Ch) recites that an NHS trust “must not be regarded as the servant or agent of the Crown.” That is a rating/status case, not a libel case.

**No judgment was found** in which an NHS trust or foundation trust sued a civic publisher for a published “non-compliance” or access-gap score. Whether *Derbyshire* covers an NHS FT as cleanly as it covers a county council is therefore **undecided on this search**. Treat “the trust cannot sue” as an argument, not a fact.

Bodies that *trade for profit* face an extra hurdle under Defamation Act 2013 s.1(2): harm is not “serious” unless it has caused or is likely to cause **serious financial loss**. A commercial interpreting agency (DAL, Sign Solutions) is in that class. An NHS trust is not obviously “a body that trades for profit.” Do not run the s.1(2) test as if it were a shield for naming a trust.

### Trading companies and charities

Lord Keith in *Derbyshire* distinguished governmental bodies from trading corporations and charities. A limited company that holds an NHS interpreting contract can sue, subject to s.1(2). Naming “DA Languages Ltd failed to fulfil 40% of bookings” is a different legal object from naming “SWL ICB has no published fulfilment series.” The dossier already treats DAL as a commercial holder of the SWL GP BSL contract. That naming is in-scope for Interpreter Regulation and Public Spending — and it is the naming that is closer to ordinary corporate libel.

## 42.2 Has any UK civic-tech project been sued for this?

### WhatDoTheyKnow — pre-action letters, not judgments

mySociety’s Ministry of Justice SLAPP call-for-evidence response (2022):

> “WhatDoTheyKnow does not have the legal resources to dispute libel claims even when we believe there is a decent case to be made. All legal complaints to date have stopped at the pre-action letter, as we have generally removed the contested material at this stage.”

Exception: they keep material where there are *no* grounds — defamation of the dead, or of public bodies under *Derbyshire*. They are “not prepared to pursue less clear-cut cases even where we might have a good case.” Example given: they **removed an audit report on a university** after threats on behalf of senior staff and governors.

WhatDoTheyKnow help page “How we run WhatDoTheyKnow”: they do not pre-check user content; they use **reactive moderation**; they remove “potentially defamatory material” on being informed, “in line with the approach used by major publishers such as the BBC and the Guardian,” specifically “to reduce the liability we have for the content posted by users.” They say they take no view on accuracy when they remove.

2021 transparency figures (mySociety moderation tag): **130** support-inbox threads labelled defamation; **66** high-risk defamation cases escalated to a review panel including the CEO and Chair of Trustees; **196** published requests had some material redacted (personal information or defamation). Context that year: 100,092 requests filed.

- SLAPP response. <https://research.mysociety.org/html/slapp-response/>
- How we run WhatDoTheyKnow. <https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/help/how>
- Moderation / transparency numbers, 2021. <https://www.mysociety.org/tag/moderation/>

**Finding.** The documented “legal challenge” in this cousin is a **letter plus a takedown**, not a trial. The cost is staff/volunteer time and the chilling of content, not a damages award. That is still a cost line.

### TheyWorkForYou — Hansard reuse and rankings, not a libel trial

Wikipedia (sourced to Hansard): in a 26 April 2007 business debate Theresa May said TheyWorkForYou had been “threatened with legal action for repeating what was printed in Hansard.” Jack Straw confirmed that a fair and accurate account of a debate is protected. Separately, *The Times* (early 2006) and Peter Luff’s Commons debate on written questions led the site to **remove absolute rankings** and add explanatory text — a product change under political pressure, not a court order. Summer 2006: Jack Straw, as Leader of the House, criticised the site for measuring MPs “in quantitative rather than qualitative terms.”

- TheyWorkForYou — Wikipedia, history section, citations 18–23. <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TheyWorkForYou>

### Climate Emergency UK / Scorecards — right of reply, no judgment found

2025 methodology: every UK council is sent the first mark; they cannot see individual question scores or the overall score at that point; **five weeks** to reply; **76%** responded in 2024 (74% in 2023). Audit then reviews first mark *and* the reply. If a council’s reply contradicts a WhatDoTheyKnow FOI, **the reply wins** for scoring, and CE UK annotates the original FOI.

No news report, judgment or CE UK blog post was found on this pass describing a libel claim, a pre-action protocol letter that forced a score change, or a solicitor’s letter that forced a takedown of a published Scorecard. Absence of a published fight is not proof that no letter was ever sent. It is a gap on “they were sued”; it is evidence that the product was designed to make a fight expensive for the subject (methodology + reply + audit) and cheap for the publisher to defend as honest process.

- 2025 methodology, Right of Reply and FOI sections. <https://councilclimatescorecards.uk/2025/methodology/>

### Full Fact — corrections machinery, not a libel defendant in this search

Published feedback and corrections policy; IFCN / EFCSN complaints route; senior review of all feedback; trustee report; option of an independent reviewer if the *subject* of a piece is unhappy. IFCN 2023 assessment: 32 fact-checks corrected that year, each noted at the foot of the article. Full Fact has been the *complainant* to IPSO (01710-24 Full Fact v Daily Express, upheld September 2024). No judgment was found in which Full Fact was the defamation defendant for a numbered claim about a public body.

- Feedback and corrections. <https://fullfact.org/about/feedback/>
- IFCN assessment record. <https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/application/public/full-fact/661a8b13ba7689d4814320d4>
- IPSO 01710-24. <https://www.ipso.co.uk/rulings/01710-24/>

### FixMyStreet, PledgeBank, IfG Performance Tracker

No defamation judgment about naming a council was found. FixMyStreet *The Times* 21 April 2007 recorded councils asking “who gave you permission,” which is political friction, not a claim form.

### Wider UK defamation in 2025–26 (context, not cousins)

Recent High Court defamation wins in this window are about **named individuals** and platforms (e.g. *Rzucek v Vinnicombe* [2026] EWHC 946 (KB), £40,000; Garrett v Schestowitz, January 2026, £70,000). They confirm that the live litigation is person-versus-publisher, not council-versus-scorecard. SLAPP reform remains incomplete (TBIJ, 5 February 2026, on the SRA/Carter-Ruck OneCoin episode). That is the climate in which a pre-action letter still works as a takedown tool against a small publisher.

## 42.3 Words that change the legal object

| Phrase | What it does | Risk class in this set |
|---|---|---|
| “No published BSL fulfilment figure was found for Trust X as at DATE, source URL” | States a search result | Same class as CE UK “no plan found” cells. Cite the search. |
| “Trust X’s AIS self-assessment is unpublished” | States a publication fact | Same. Official AIS guidance already tells bodies to publish. |
| “Trust X is non-compliant with DAPB1605 / the Equality Act” | Legal conclusion | Different object. CQC does not assure AIS (prompt 34). A volunteer monitor asserting “non-compliant” is doing the job the regulator has declined. Higher defamation and public-law heat, even if the *body* cannot sue. |
| “Chief Executive Y failed to book a BSL interpreter for patient Z” | Names a natural person and a clinical event | Individual libel + possible data-protection issue if Z is identifiable. Do not. |
| “DA Languages Ltd’s contract does not itemise BSL on the award notice” | Names a trading company and a public document | Corporate libel possible; s.1(2) serious-financial-loss hurdle; still cite the notice. |
| “Councillor / DASS A is responsible for the gap” | Names a natural person and attributes fault | Individual can sue. *Derbyshire* does not help. |

The dossier rule already in force — “log gaps as gaps, not filled guesses” — is also the defamation-minimising rule. A cell that says “not found” is a different statement from a cell that says “unlawful.”

## 42.4 Precautions the cousins actually paid for

These are cost lines. Prompt 41 said not to treat reply as a footnote.

**1. Right of reply before publication.**  
CE UK: five weeks; subject sees the questions marked, not the scores; 74–76% respond; reply can override an FOI answer. Labour: write the letters, track 400 inboxes, re-mark. That is part of the 5,000-hour pile, not extra to it.

**2. Published methodology and a frozen question set.**  
A court looking at s.4 “reasonable belief” will ask what process you used. CE UK consults 90+ organisations on the question set and publishes criteria. Full Fact publishes a corrections policy because IFCN requires it. A Deaf monitor that invents a RAG on the day of publication has a worse s.4 story than one that published the questions first.

**3. Reactive moderation / pre-action takedown.**  
WDTK’s chosen cost: remove first, argue never. They say they lack the money to defend even good cases. A solo project inherits that constraint unless it has legal insurance and counsel on retainer.

**4. Do not name living officers as the story.**  
*Duke* and *Derbyshire* both point here. The university claim failed because the words were about two people. The council cannot sue; the director can.

**5. Exclude the subject from marking.**  
CE UK will not accept current councillors or officers as markers (prompt 41). That is independence as well as law. An ICB comms team marking its own AIS cell is the official self-assessment, which already exists and which CQC does not assure.

**6. Privilege for fair reports of official records.**  
TheyWorkForYou’s Hansard threat was answered by Straw: a fair and accurate account of a debate is protected. FOI replies published on WhatDoTheyKnow sit in a different place — they are the authority’s own words, but the *request* can defame. A monitor that republishes a trust’s FOI answer and cites it is closer to the protected class than a monitor that writes a new accusation on top.

**7. Corrections log.**  
Full Fact’s 32 corrections in 2023 are a public cost and a credibility asset. A gap monitor that cannot correct a cell without republishing the whole site will accumulate legal and reputational debt.

**8. Escalation panel.**  
WDTK sends high-risk defamation, data-breach and threat-of-action cases to a panel that includes the CEO and the Chair of Trustees. A one-person project has no panel. That is a governance gap, not a moral one.

## 42.5 Other legal objects that are not libel

**Data protection.** WDTK’s 2021 inbox: 317 GDPR right-to-erasure threads, 96 data-breach threads, 33 rectification. Redactions on published requests are often personal information, not libel. A BSL monitor that republishes an FOI containing a patient’s name, an interpreter’s mobile, or a staff email is in the ICO’s territory. The dossier FOI templates already exist; they should stay framed as statistics and contract terms, not case files.

**Copyright.** TheyWorkForYou launched while Hansard was still Crown copyright and no licence existed; a licence followed. Republishing a trust’s PDF AIS self-assessment in full is a different act from quoting a figure and linking. Not researched to a conclusion here. Gap.

**Malicious falsehood.** Separate tort; requires malice and (usually) special damage. *Derbyshire* is about defamation. Do not assume the same bar applies. No civic-tech malicious-falsehood judgment found.

**Public-law / “official sounding.”** Prompt 39 already rejected “AIS Compliance Monitor” and “Access Gap Register” as names that read as NHS or CQC products. Publishing a “non-compliance” finding under a name that sounds official is both a positioning risk and a misrepresentation risk. Stay in the mySociety plain-English convention.

**Pre-publication counsel.** No published pound figure was found for a civic-tech pre-publication review of a scorecard. Commercial media pre-pub review exists as a practice; the cousins above rely on process (methodology, reply, reactive moderation) rather than a named QC sign-off. A funder may still require counsel. That would be a grant-condition cost, not a documented cousin cost.

## 42.6 What this means for the tool in this dossier

Copy the CE UK / WDTK stack, not a campaign press release.

1. **Name the body, cite the cell, date the search.** “Not found at URL on DATE” is the unit of publication. “Non-compliant with the Act” is a legal conclusion the product should not reach unless a court, CQC, PHSO or the ICB’s own published self-assessment has already said so — and then quote that source.
2. **Right of reply is in the critical path**, not a courtesy. Five weeks, written offer, log who replied. Budget the labour. Prompt 41’s 5,000-hour figure already includes this kind of work.
3. **Do not name living officers as the finding.** Name the ICB, the trust, the council, the company on the award notice.
4. **Treat universities, commercial contractors and named individuals as a different legal object** from councils. WDTK already does. SWL’s GP BSL holder is a company (DAL). That cell is closer to ordinary corporate speech.
5. **Assume a pre-action letter will come and that a small project will take the material down** unless *Derbyshire* is on all fours. Price that. Insurance and a solicitor who can read a letter-before-action are running costs, like WDTK’s 2018 £15k admin ask.
6. **NHS foundation trusts are a gap.** Do not print “trusts cannot sue.” Print “no case found; individuals can; process still required.”
7. **The official-sounding name problem is also a legal-adjacent problem.** A product called “AIS Compliance Monitor” invites the reader to think CQC has spoken. Prompt 39 already killed that name. Keep it dead.

Prompt 43 should take WDTK-at-~400-councils and the CE UK FOI-then-reply loop as the collection method, including the rule that a reply can override an FOI for scoring. Prompt 44 should treat legal insurance, counsel-on-retainer and a corrections log as budget lines next to volunteer hours.
