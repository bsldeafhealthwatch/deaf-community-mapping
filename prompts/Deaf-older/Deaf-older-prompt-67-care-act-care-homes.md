# Prompt 67 — Social care assessment and care home access: Care Act 2014 and CQC-regulated settings

**Master index prompt 67.** Second file in the Deaf/DeafBlind older people batch (prompts 66–71). Compiled 10 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment; do not wear a DEI or anti-DEI banner. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier. Gaps are logged as gaps, not filled guesses.

**What this prompt is for.** Test whether older BSL-using Deaf and DeafBlind people are visible in Care Act assessment practice and in the CQC-registered care-home market — separately from the general “sensory impairment / hearing loss” band. Four questions: (1) whether local-authority needs assessments document BSL or tactile communication as a matter of course; (2) how many English, and SWL, care homes are BSL-specialist or Deaf-led; (3) documented isolation of an older Deaf or DeafBlind resident inside a mainstream home; (4) CQC, safeguarding or ombudsman findings that name communication isolation as a factor in rating or harm.

**Duty reminder.** Publicly funded adult social care *is* in scope of the Accessible Information Standard (DAPB1605). Local authorities also sit under the Care Act 2014 and the Equality Act 2010. Do not collapse those three duties into one sentence.

**Index check.** Prompt 66 is `Deaf-older-prompt-66-state-pension-pension-credit.md`. This file does not repeat Pension Service findings. Domain 16 (end-of-life) already holds PHSO Alan Graham and family-as-interpreter harm in dying; this file covers living in assessment and placement, not dying. Proposed Domain 17: Ageing, retirement, and later-life isolation.

## Headline

Two different legal treatments sit on the same assessment desk. **Deafblind adults have a named specialist-assessor duty** in Care Act statutory guidance (paragraphs 6.91–6.96: assessor at QCF/OCN level 3 or above; qualified interpreter where the person uses sign language). **BSL-using Deaf adults who are not deafblind do not.** They sit under the general “provide an interpreter where communication is affected” line at 6.80. The BSL Advisory Board’s *Locked Out* report (27 November 2025) asks government to close that gap by expanding Care Act recognition to BSL users “similar to that granted deafblind individuals.” That request is itself evidence that the current statute does not treat the two groups the same.

The CQC care directory tags homes with a **“sensory impairments”** service-user band. It does not tag “BSL-specialist” or “Deaf-led.” There is therefore **no reliable public count** of BSL-specialist care homes in England, and none for the six SWL boroughs as a derived CQC extract. What can be named from provider and inspection pages:

- Inside the SWL footprint, the only CQC-registered specialist Deaf residential service found on this pass is **SignHealth Longley Road, 89 Longley Road, Tooting, Wandsworth SW17 9LD** — six places, overall **Outstanding** after an October 2025 assessment published 14 January 2026. Staff required to hold at least BSL Level 2; almost all Deaf. The inspector attended with a BSL interpreter.
- SignHealth’s **London Independent Living** service in Balham is independent/supported living for **Richmond and Wandsworth residents only**, not a second registered specialist care home.
- England-wide, a short named list exists (SignHealth’s five registered homes, Deaf Action’s Easthill on the Isle of Wight, Achieve Together’s Roper House in Canterbury and Gallaudet in Bath, plus a handful of others). That list is **not a census**.

The anchor isolation finding is not SWL. **Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman decision 24 017 923**, 29 October 2025, London Borough of Hammersmith & Fulham: Mr Y, profoundly deaf, BSL first language, placed in a mainstream care home whose staff had **no BSL or deaf-awareness training** contrary to his care plan; a Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards visit was done with **pen and paper**. He died shortly after transfer back to another home. Fault found; £450 to the granddaughter and a process review. The care plan had recorded the BSL need. Recording was not fulfilment.

## 67.1 Do Care Act assessments document BSL or tactile needs as a matter of course?

**The guidance distinguishes deafblind from Deaf. Practice, on the published record, records the need when someone pushes for it — and then often fails to staff it.**

### What the statutory guidance actually says

Care and Support Statutory Guidance, chapter 6:

- **6.80** (general): the authority “must ensure that the person is able to be involved as far as possible, for example by providing an interpreter where a person has a particular condition affecting communication — such as autism, blindness, or deafness.”
- **6.91–6.96** (deafblind, mandatory specialist route):
  - an **expert must be involved** in the assessment of adults who are deafblind, including supported self-assessment;
  - the specialist assessment must be carried out by an assessor or team trained to **at least QCF or OCN level 3**, or above where needs are higher or more complex;
  - training should cover communication, one-to-one human contact, social interaction and emotional wellbeing, mobility, assistive technology and rehabilitation;
  - deafblindness is a dual-sensory condition and cannot be assessed by treating hearing and vision separately;
  - “where necessary a qualified interpreter with training appropriate for the deafblind adult’s communication should be used.”

That is a named, trained-assessor duty for deafblind people. It is **not** written as a named duty for culturally Deaf BSL users who are not deafblind.

### What the BSL Advisory Board says the gap is

*BSL user experience of health and social care in the UK* (*Locked Out* successor pack), published on GOV.UK 27 November 2025, asks local authorities and others to:

> “Expand protections and recognition under the Care Act 2014 to explicitly include BSL users and deaf individuals – similar to that granted deafblind individuals.”

It also asks authorities to “implement a clear and accessible registration and reporting process… to specifically identify BSL users, deaf and deafblind individuals” and to “resume the statutory reporting of deaf registration data.”

BASW’s older sensory-loss resource (still the cited professional text) records the registration asymmetry that sits behind that ask: the Care Act **enables** a register of people with hearing impairments but **does not require** one; the statutory register duty is sight impairment; deafblind policy guidance requires authorities to “keep a record” of deafblind people. Wales is tighter (register of hearing-impaired people is required).

So: a BSL need *can* be written into a Care Act plan. There is no published national rate for how often it is written in without a family member or advocate forcing the point, and no published SWL rate at all.

### When the plan does record BSL, fulfilment still fails

LGSCO 24 017 923 (full facts in 67.3) is the worked example. Hammersmith & Fulham’s April 2024 care plan recorded that Mr Y’s first language was BSL and that he required a BSL interpreter. Because the council “was unable to find carers with BSL skills,” the plan said his daughter would teach home carers basic BSL. Family interpreting was later withdrawn. The subsequent care-home placement specified that staff should learn basic BSL. The home told the council it had received **no BSL or deaf-awareness training**. Recording happened. Staffing did not.

### Capacity and DoLS assessments are a second failure point

*Oldham Metropolitan Borough Council v KZ & Ors* [2024] (Mrs Justice Theis, 4 December 2024) is Court of Protection guidance, not a Care Act assessment study, but it is the nearest judicial statement on assessing a BSL-fluent deaf adult:

1. A mental-capacity assessment of a deaf individual fluent in BSL should ideally be done by an assessor suitably qualified to communicate at the relevant BSL level; if not, say why and what measures will close the gap.
2. The assessor should ideally have a background in deafness and the Deaf community; if not, say why they are doing the assessment anyway.

The judgment records earlier non-specialist assessments that had produced an inaccurate “borderline learning disability” label and “extreme language deprivation.” A service manager with BSL Level 1 acting as interpreter was described as “frankly astonishing.”

The same pattern appears in the Hammersmith DoLS visit of 15 November 2024: no interpreter booked, pen and paper used, the allocated social worker themselves calling it “highly inappropriate.”

### Professional practice material, not SWL audit

Research in Practice published an open-access guide on 16 December 2025 by Gesa Weitzel, a Deaf registered social worker, on completing Care Act assessments and reviews with Deaf adults. The existence of a 2025 how-to guide is evidence that generic adult-social-care assessment practice is not assumed to be Deaf-competent. It is not evidence of SWL compliance rates.

Hertfordshire County Council published a BSL explainer of “what a social care assessment is” in 2015. A BSL film about the process is not the same as a specialist assessor doing the process.

**ABSENT:** any published count, for England or for Croydon, Kingston, Merton, Richmond, Sutton or Wandsworth, of Care Act assessments in the last three years that recorded a BSL, Deafblind-manual, hands-on, or tactile communication need. That is the FOI question this file hands to the existing companion log.

## 67.2 How many CQC-registered care homes are BSL-specialist or Deaf-led?

**No reliable England figure. No reliable SWL extract from the CQC directory. A named-home list can be built; it is not a census.**

### Why the public dataset cannot answer the question

CQC’s care directory (CSV / API, updated weekly; 57,082 services on the 26 August 2026 file cited by third-party scrapers) records **service-user bands**, including “sensory impairments,” plus age bands, dementia, learning disability, mental health and physical disability. “Sensory impairments” catches hearing loss, sight loss, dual sensory loss and a long tail of other sensory need. It does **not** mean:

- BSL is the working language of the home;
- staff are Deaf or hold a specified BSL level;
- the home is Deaf-led;
- residents are culturally Deaf rather than late-deafened older people with hearing aids.

HousingCare-style “hearing impairment care homes” listings inherit the same band and produce county-level hundreds. Those hundreds are not BSL-specialist homes. Do not quote them as such.

CQC does not publish a “BSL” or “Deaf culture” filter. That absence is the gap the batch note predicted.

### A 2014 quality mark that has not become a CQC field

RAD and [sonus] launched a “Deaf-Aware Care” quality mark for mainstream residential homes in March 2014, with a ministerial pledge from Norman Lamb to promote it and a conversation with CQC about ratings. An 88-year-old Deaf man was quoted that residential care can be “hell” for BSL users. No evidence was found on this pass that the mark is a live, inspectable CQC attribute in 2026, or that any SWL home holds it.

Mainstream homes that train a handful of staff in basic BSL (Ashlea Mews, South Shields, October 2025 — four colleagues, six-week course, one deaf resident with dementia) are **not** specialist homes. Log them as adaptation anecdotes, not as capacity.

## 67.3 Documented isolation inside a mainstream care home

**The clean official case is LGSCO 24 017 923. It is London, not SWL. It is exactly the mechanism this prompt asked for.**

### LGSCO 24 017 923 — London Borough of Hammersmith & Fulham, 29 October 2025

- **Mr Y:** several health conditions, profoundly deaf, BSL first language. Daughter (Ms Z) main carer. Granddaughter (Miss X) a qualified BSL interpreter. Complaint brought by Miss X.
- **April 2024 care plan** recorded BSL as first language and a need for a BSL interpreter. No BSL-fluent home carers available, so the plan relied on Ms Z teaching carers basic BSL.
- **April 2024 respite at Care Home A:** family had accepted that staff might not use BSL if they faced him so he could see their mouths. Council visit on 1 May 2024 **did** include a BSL interpreter. No fault on this period.
- From 28 May 2024 the family stopped acting as interpreters.
- **Home-care window May–June 2024:** council took no effective action to put BSL-skilled carers in. Fault.
- **21 October 2024:** Mr Y moved to Care Home B after hospital discharge. Placement care plan said staff should secure basic BSL skills; outreach started. On **8 November 2024** Care Home B told the council it had difficulty communicating with him and had **received no training in BSL or deaf awareness**.
- **15 November 2024 DoLS visit:** no BSL interpreter. Meeting conducted with **pen and paper**. The allocated social worker flagged it the same day as highly inappropriate. DoLS team apologised. A later DoLS visit on 7 December did have family and interpreter.
- **18 November 2024 review:** “no-one except Mr Y’s family was able to communicate with him unless a BSL interpreter was present”; relationship with staff strained; a physiotherapist stopped further assessment because of communication.
- Mr Y was moved back to Care Home A, then admitted to hospital, and **died a few days later**.
- **Fault found** on: no interpreter at the 15 November DoLS visit; no BSL-skilled home carers May–June 2024; no evidence Care Home B staff received the BSL training the care plan required after 8 November.
- **Injustice:** distress and inability to engage for Mr Y; avoidable distress and uncertainty for Miss X. Because he had died, LGSCO did not seek a personal financial remedy for him.
- **Remedy agreed:** apology; **£450** to Miss X; council to review its processes on communication support for deaf service users (one month / three months).

BBC and MyLondon coverage (5 January 2026 / 24 December 2025) match the decision. Hammersmith & Fulham is not an SWL borough. The mechanism — plan says BSL, placement cannot sign, DoLS proceeds without an interpreter, family carries the language — is the one SWL directors of adult social services would have to disprove with their own figures.

### Other documented fragments, graded

- **RAD / [sonus] 2014:** 88-year-old Deaf man, residential care described as “hell” for BSL users. Advocacy testimony, twelve years old, not an ombudsman finding.
- **Gallaudet Home, Bath, CQC 30 August 2023:** specialist Deaf home, not mainstream, but inspectors found Deaf culture only partly embedded and the wider Deaf community “not always considered.” Overall Requires Improvement. Useful as a reminder that a “Deaf” label on a home is not automatic Outstanding.
- **Ashlea Mews, South Shields, October 2025:** four staff complete a six-week BSL course to reach one deaf resident with dementia. Adaptation of a mainstream home; not evidence the resident was previously able to converse with the staff group.
- **No named SWL safeguarding adult review** was found on this pass that identifies communication isolation of a Deaf or DeafBlind care-home resident as a factor in harm. **ABSENT**, not a failed search.

## 67.4 CQC ratings, safeguarding and isolation as an inspection factor

CQC **does** write communication isolation into inspection narratives when it inspects a specialist Deaf service, and it has used a BSL interpreter on those inspections (Longley Road 2025; Easthill 2022). It does **not** publish a national indicator of “Deaf resident, no signing staff.” AIS is **not assured by CQC**; commissioners are. CQC “may take action” if communication needs are unmet and maps AIS to fundamental standards (person-centred care, dignity, consent, safety). That mapping is already logged in AIS prompt 34. It has not produced a published count of care homes rated down *because* a Deaf resident could not speak to anyone.

What this pass can say, tightly:

- Longley Road’s Outstanding rating in January 2026 is explicitly tied to BSL-fluent, mostly-Deaf staffing, visual information, and cultural identity. Catriona Eglinton (CQC deputy director of adult social care, south London) is the named inspector-side voice on that report.
- Gallaudet’s Requires Improvement in 2023 is explicitly tied, in part, to incomplete embedding of Deaf culture.
- LGSCO, not CQC, is the body that put a named mainstream-home isolation sequence on the public record in 2025.
- CQC’s own public contact route for Deaf people is Convo (formerly SignLive), page updated 29 September 2025 — relevant to how a family might tell CQC about a home, not to how CQC scores homes.

No ADASS national publication found on this pass that counts BSL-flagged Care Act assessments or Deaf-specialist placements. ADASS is listed among the audiences of the November 2025 BSL Advisory Board action list; that is a request to ADASS, not an ADASS dataset.

## What not to write

- Do not treat CQC “sensory impairments” or HousingCare “hearing impairment homes” counts as BSL-specialist capacity.
- Do not describe Longley Road as SWL’s generic Deaf nursing home for frail older people. It is a six-place MH/LD Deaf service.
- Do not repeat Deaf Action’s 2023 “only BSL care home in the UK” line without the contest from SignHealth and Roper House.
- Do not import Alan Graham or Samantha from Domain 16 / AIS 37 as care-home findings.
- Do not write that Care Act assessments “must” use a Deaf specialist for every BSL user — that sentence is true for **deafblind** adults, and is the reform the BSL Advisory Board is still asking for in respect of Deaf BSL users.
- Do not add up the named-home table and print a national bed total.

Ask each of the six SWL local authorities (Croydon, Kingston, Merton, Richmond, Sutton, Wandsworth), last three complete years:

1. How many adult Care Act needs assessments recorded a BSL, Deafblind-manual, hands-on signing, or tactile communication need? Split 18–64 / 65+ if held.

3. How many current placements (snapshot date) have a recorded BSL or tactile communication need?
