# Prompt 68 — Loneliness, isolation, and social participation in later life

**Master index prompt 68.** Third file in the Deaf/DeafBlind older people batch (prompts 66–71). Compiled 10 September 2026. **This is the priority prompt of the batch.** Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment; do not wear a DEI or anti-DEI banner. An unsourced AI claim is the lowest tier. Gaps are logged as gaps, not filled guesses.

**What this prompt is for.** Find out whether older BSL-using Deaf and DeafBlind people are visible in UK loneliness data at all — separately from general older-age loneliness, separately from working-age Deaf isolation, and separately from the large “hearing loss in later life” literature. If the evidence is thin or folded into hard-of-hearing statistics, **that thinness is the headline**, not a reason to keep mining for a study that may not exist.

**Do not substitute.** Age UK, ONS Community Life Survey, Campaign to End Loneliness and the hearing-loss/loneliness scoping reviews are about a different population unless they isolate BSL or dual sensory loss. Prompt 67 covers care-home isolation as a placement failure. This file covers everyday later-life social life: clubs, churches, day centres, peer death, travel, video-call exclusion. Domain 16 covers dying.

---

## Headline

There is **no official UK loneliness prevalence** for older BSL-using Deaf people, and none for older DeafBlind people as a distinct group. The Community Life Survey 2024/25 — the government’s loneliness series — splits “often or always lonely” by age and by disabled/non-disabled. Disabled adults: **14%** often/always lonely against **3%** of non-disabled adults. Adults aged 65–74: **4%**; 75+: **5%**. It does not ask whether the respondent uses BSL, is culturally Deaf, or has dual sensory loss.

What exists instead is a short stack of small, local or mixed-sample studies, plus a qualitative mechanism that is consistent across them:

- **Manchester Deaf Centre** ageing survey, n=96, published via the National Lottery Community Fund around March 2020: **60%** knew other Deaf people who were lonely; **74%** did not involve themselves with the local hearing community; **56%** knew Deaf people in residential homes with no BSL-trained staff; **30%** were afraid to go out at night. A majority of *respondents themselves* (60/96) said they were not lonely. Local, not nationally representative, and not an ONS instrument.
- **RNID *It Does Matter*** (survey fieldwork feeding the Dec 2024 / Jan 2025 publications): 180 BSL users inside a 5,383 deaf-or-hearing-loss sample. Of BSL users who had experienced negative attitudes, **36%** said they felt lonely or isolated; **41%** wanted to avoid people; **53%** felt excluded. Not age-banded to 65+. Not a later-life prevalence study.
- **BSL Advisory Board *Locked Out*** (27 November 2025) does not publish a loneliness rate. It does publish the mechanism in official language: BSL users are “spread out and often socially isolated,” have to “travel long distances to see friends,” and “often cannot communicate with families or neighbours.” Lived-experience lines in the executive summary include “I’m lonely. No one to talk to” inside a care setting, and a housebound person paying “over £100 each time I go out to meet friends.”
- Club and centre closures over the last ten years are documented as individual local stories (Portsmouth Deaf Centre, February 2025; West Norfolk centre sale, April 2026; Huddersfield Society £100k appeal, June 2025; Deafconnect Northamptonshire collapse, January 2026). There is **no national register** of Deaf clubs opening or closing.
- Inside SWL, the only named older-Deaf social offer found on this pass is **Remark! Community’s Croydon lunch club** for Deaf people aged 55+, launched February 2026 at St Mary’s Parish Centre, Wellesley Road, Thursdays 10am–3pm. Remark runs four such London lunch clubs (Croydon, Eltham, Harrow, Camden) and describes itself as 14 clubs across London. No published evaluation of whether the clubs reduce loneliness.

The hearing-loss-and-loneliness literature is large and must not be quoted as if it were this population. Most of it is about acquired, often age-related, hearing loss in spoken-language older adults.

## 68.1 Is there a survey that measures loneliness among older Deaf / DeafBlind people specifically?

**No national one. Four sources get close. None is an official later-life BSL prevalence.**

### Official series — disabled and older, not Deaf

Community Life Survey 2024/25 (DCMS, 10 December 2025):

- 7% of adults in England felt lonely often or always.
- Disabled adults **14%** vs non-disabled **3%**.
- Age 65–74: **4%**; 75+: **5%** (both *below* the all-adult average).
- High indirect loneliness (score 8–9): disabled **18%** vs non-disabled **5%**.

ONS “Disability and loneliness” datasets (last edition 2021 in the series found on this pass) likewise cut by impairment *severity* and age, not by BSL. An ONS FOI on Deaf/hard-of-hearing population counts (FOI-2023-1648, 4 January 2024) pointed at Annual Population Survey health-condition rows — not a loneliness table.

**ABSENT:** a Community Life Survey, Opinions and Lifestyle Survey, or ELSA cut that names BSL, culturally Deaf, or DeafBlind.

### DeafBlind — qualitative and modelled, not a loneliness rate

- Sense, cited in Campaign to End Loneliness *Alone in the Crowd* materials: approximately **250,000** deafblind people aged over 70; projected **418,000** by 2030. Sense’s line that “at some point in their lives, every deafblind person will become socially and emotionally isolated” is an advocacy synthesis, not a survey percentage.
- Peter Simcock’s UK IPA study (*Ageing & Society* 2020 / 2023 papers; KCL thesis): **eight** older adults ageing *with* deafblindness, interviewed 2014–16, aged 48–83. Isolation and “medicine for loneliness” (human contact) are central themes. One participant describes Deaf peers walking away because tactile signing is effortful — isolation *inside* the Deaf world, not only outside it.
- World Federation of the Deafblind summary on older people with deafblindness (2023): isolation “mentioned by virtually all” interviewees. International, not a UK rate.
- Deafblind UK programmes (Winter Wellness, befrienders, Christmas Day calls) treat loneliness as a known operational problem. They do not publish a membership loneliness prevalence.

### What other countries measured that the UK has not

A November 2025 *Journal of Deaf Studies and Deaf Education* paper interviewed **12** older sign-language users — in **Finland**. The authors state “very little is known about the experiences of loneliness among older deaf adults” and call it “one of the first studies.” A 2012 Finnish Association of the Deaf survey (n=421) found **36%** lonely at least some of the time. That is a Finnish figure. Do not print it as UK.

US *Innovation in Aging* (2025) on “everyday challenges… aging with deafness” is ASL, not BSL, and is qualitative on family exclusion and “now there are almost no Deaf people here.”

## 68.2 Mechanisms that are documented — even without a prevalence rate

These recur across *Locked Out*, the Manchester survey, Simcock, RNID BSL-user quotes, and club-closure reporting. They are mechanisms, not percentages.

### 1. The Deaf peer network thins, then vanishes

Older culturally Deaf people built their social world in BSL at clubs, churches, residential schools and Deaf workplaces. As peers die, move into hearing-staffed care, or stop travelling, the remaining network is regional rather than local. *Locked Out*: “localism and geographical bias… do not serve the needs of deaf and deafblind people who are spread out.” Manchester case study “Margret, 73”: husband dies, she never learned to drive, “shies away from the hearing world,” struggles with NHS/bank/HMRC without interpreters.

Simcock’s deafblind participants add a second cut: even other Deaf people disengage when communication has to become tactile. Isolation can happen *inside* the signing community, not only at its edge.

### 2. Mainstream older-people’s services run in spoken English

Age UK day centres, lunch clubs and befriending schemes are built for hearing older people, including those with acquired hearing loss who still use speech. A BSL-first elder in that room is present and excluded. The Manchester survey’s 74% “do not involve themselves with their local hearing community” is the quantitative echo. Locked Out’s care-setting quote — “I watch people laugh and talk. I have to watch and wait for my moment to ask for a drink” — is the same mechanism after placement (already logged as a care-home fact in prompt 67; it is also a loneliness fact).

### 3. Travel to the nearest BSL group is the social life

Because density is low, the social infrastructure is borough- or region-wide. Locked Out records outings costing “over £100 each time” and nobody to take the person. Prompt 69 will take the transport half of this. The loneliness half is: if the person cannot travel, the peer network is theoretical.

### 4. Digital exclusion when vision goes

VRS, BSL video, Facebook live-streams and Zoom clubs assume usable sight. Deafblind BSL users lose that channel. *Locked Out* section on deafblind BSL signers: tracking, visual-frame and tactile BSL may be the only remaining route; VRS and BSL websites become inaccessible. Deafblind UK’s own offer then shifts to *voice* befriending and phone helpline hours — which only works for people who still have usable hearing or a hearing household. The remaining group has neither speech-on-phone nor sight-on-video.

### 5. Family is usually hearing

*Locked Out*: 90% of deaf parents have hearing children. RNID: 48% of a mixed deaf/HoH sample had negative attitudes from family; BSL users report being spoken *past*. Family-as-interpreter, already a Domain 16 and prompt 67 finding, is also a later-life isolation finding: the people in the house may not be conversation partners in the person’s language.

### 6. Do not confuse this with age-related hearing-loss withdrawal

The Shukla / Bott-Saunders scoping reviews and ELSA-style papers show hearing loss associated with isolation and loneliness across the life course. The mechanism there is effortful listening and withdrawal from noisy rooms. The mechanism here is **no shared language in the room**, plus a thinning language community. Merging the two hides the BSL-specific picture. That merge is the default in Age UK and care-home hearing-loss toolkits (Engage / Care England 2025 still cites RNID *It Does Matter* 40% lonely/isolated for “deaf people” inside a hearing-loss care-home argument).

## What not to write

- Do not print Community Life Survey 14% (disabled) or 4–5% (65+) as a Deaf-elder rate.
- Do not print RNID 36% as “over a third of older Deaf people are lonely.” It is 36% of a BSL-user stigma subset.
- Do not print the Finnish 36% as UK.
- Do not use hearing-loss / hearing-aid loneliness papers (Shukla, Bott, ELSA poor-hearing analyses, UNIGE 2025) as if they were BSL-community findings.
- Do not repeat *Locked Out*’s “only 2 residential homes that cater specifically to BSL users” without the contest already logged in prompt 67.
- Do not describe Remark’s Croydon club as proof that SWL has solved isolation. It is one weekly room.

## How this file should be used

The headline a briefing can carry is: **older BSL users are not a row in the national loneliness statistics.** The mechanisms are documented; the count is not. Prompt 69 tests whether transport is the binding constraint on the remaining peer network. Prompt 71 can carry testimony; this file should not be padded with unnamed “many Deaf pensioners told us” lines.
