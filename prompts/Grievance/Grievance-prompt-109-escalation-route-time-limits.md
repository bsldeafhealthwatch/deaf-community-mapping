## Headline

The live sequence in **England, Wales and Scotland** is: raise a formal grievance under the employer’s procedure (Acas Code of Practice 1, in force 11 March 2015, is the minimum); if that does not resolve the dispute, **notify Acas** and complete early conciliation; if no settlement, present an ET1 with the early-conciliation certificate number. Early conciliation is a **jurisdictional precondition** for most claims (Employment Tribunals Act 1996 s.18A). Northern Ireland uses the Labour Relations Agency, not Acas, on a shorter conciliation period.

The Equality Act claim clock, as of 10 September 2026, is still **three months starting with the date of the act** (EA 2010 s.123(1)(a)) — conventionally described as three months minus one day. From **1 October 2026** most claims, including Equality Act employment claims, move to **six months minus one day**, but only where the time limit *starts* on or after that date (Employment Rights Act 2025 s.152 and Sch. 12; Acas page updated 4 September 2026; business.gov.uk 30 July 2026). Commencement regulations were still awaited on the Lexis tracker as of early September 2026; Acas and the government’s implementation page treat 1 October 2026 as the live date.

**Going through a grievance does not stop the clock.** Acas is explicit: “If those procedures take a long time, you still need to notify Acas before your time limit runs out.” That is the interaction with 97–102: prompt 98 logged TUC and BDF survey waits of many months for *agreed* adjustments. A claimant who waits for the internal SLA or the grievance outcome before notifying Acas can run out of time. The only statutory pause is early conciliation itself (EA s.140B / ERA 1996 s.207B): Day A to Day B is not counted, and there is a one-month safety net after the certificate. Acas early conciliation can last **up to 12 weeks** for notifications from 1 December 2025.

A missed internal SLA is **not**, on its own, a finding of failure to make reasonable adjustments. No named judgment was found that treats breach of an employer’s self-set processing target as a free-standing Equality Act contravention. Delay *is* legally relevant in two narrower ways: (1) as part of the fact-specific reasonableness test under s.20 (did the employer take the step within a period that was reasonable?); (2) as the trigger for when time starts to run on an *omission* claim under s.123(3)(b) and (4) — *Matuszowicz v Kingston upon Hull City Council* [2009] EWCA Civ 22; *Abertawe Bro Morgannwg University Local Health Board v Morgan* [2018] EWCA Civ 640. Those cases do not mention SLAs. Do not print “missed SLA = discrimination.”

An agency, contract or outsourced worker does not face a different *sequence*, but does face an extra **respondent-identification** step. EA s.41(4) puts the reasonable-adjustment duty on the principal (hirer / end-user) *as well as* the worker’s employer. The ET1 and the Acas notification should name every intended respondent; a missing early-conciliation certificate against a named respondent is a jurisdictional bar to instituting that claim (*Abel Estate Agent Ltd v Reynolds* [2025] EAT 6; Court of Appeal later confirmed s.18A is a true precondition). AWR 2010 is still the wrong statute for this cell (prompt 97).

**Hard-don't for anything public.** Do not print that a missed SLA, a grievance outcome, or Acas’s process is unlawful or automatically evidence of discrimination. Do not treat the 1 October 2026 six-month clock as already running on acts that started before that date. Do not treat Northern Ireland as Acas territory.

## 109.1 The sequence, and what is and is not mandatory

Three layers get collapsed in ordinary conversation. Keep them separate.

1. **Internal grievance.** The Acas Code of Practice 1 (in force 11 March 2015) is the minimum procedure. Raise the complaint in writing; employer should investigate, hold a meeting, decide, and offer an appeal. Unreasonable failure to follow the Code can move a later compensatory award by up to 25% under TULRCA 1992 s.207A — that is a remedy adjustment, not a permission-to-sue rule. A claimant can go to Acas / the tribunal without exhausting the grievance. Doing so may cost them on the uplift, and a tribunal may still ask why they did not use the internal route, but the Code is not a statutory pre-action protocol.

2. **Acas early conciliation (Great Britain).** This *is* a statutory pre-condition for most relevant proceedings: ETA 1996 s.18A. Notify Acas inside the limitation window. Conciliation can run up to 12 weeks from 1 December 2025. Either party can decline to engage; Acas then issues the certificate. Without the certificate number on the ET1, the tribunal cannot entertain the claim as instituted (*Abel / Reynolds*). Amendments inside existing proceedings are a different, narrower question — do not treat that Court of Appeal nuance as a way to skip notification.

3. **Employment tribunal.** ET1. For an Equality Act reasonable-adjustment claim the limitation rule is s.123, modified by s.140B if Acas was notified in time, with a residual just-and-equitable discretion.

Northern Ireland substitutes the LRA and a one-month (+14 days) conciliation period. Do not write “Acas” across the UK.

The draft 2026 Code (consultation open until 23 September 2026) restates the Equality Act duty *inside* grievance and disciplinary process and pushes informal resolution first. Until it is approved, laid, and commenced, the 2015 Code is the one tribunals take into account.

## 109.2 The clocks — grievance does not pause them

As of the compile date the primary EA clock is three months from the act (s.123(1)(a)). Acas’s working formula is three months minus one day, ending at 23:59 on that day.

For a refused adjustment that is a positive decision (“we will not book an interpreter for team meetings”), time runs from that decision. For a missed SLA / silent queue, the claim is almost always pleaded as an *omission*. Then s.123(3)(b) and (4) apply. The Court of Appeal in *Matuszowicz* and again in *Abertawe v Morgan* held that even a non-deliberate continuing failure is deemed to occur at an artificial date: an inconsistent act, or the end of the period in which the employer might reasonably have been expected to do the thing. *Abertawe* adds that that period is assessed from the claimant’s point of view — a claimant who reasonably thought the employer was still working on it is not automatically timed out from the date the duty first arose.

That is the closest the law comes to “delay matters.” It is not an SLA. An employer’s unpublished or published “20 working days” target is evidence a tribunal *might* look at when fixing the s.123(4)(b) date. No authority found on this pass says the SLA number *is* that date.

**Headline interaction with 97–102.** Prompt 97 found no statutory processing clock. Prompt 98 found no official employer-side time series and survey waits measured in months. Acas’s 4 September 2026 page is unambiguous that a long grievance does not stop time. A Deaf BSL-using employee whose agreed meeting-interpreter is never actioned, and who waits for HR’s ticket and then for a grievance outcome before notifying Acas, can lose the primary window. Just-and-equitable is available and is more generous than the unfair-dismissal test; it is not a promise. *Henry v Leeds Community Healthcare* is one ET that extended time where disability and adviser delay featured; *Laverick v EE* is one that refused. Two cases, opposite ways. Prompt 114 is where published outcomes belong.

From 1 October 2026 the primary limit for most claims becomes six months minus one day, for time limits that *start* on or after that date. That softens, it does not remove, the interaction: a year-long internal queue (the TUC 2025 survey shape) still outruns a six-month clock. Early conciliation still only pauses *after* notification.

s.140B / s.207B mechanics, compressed: notify inside the primary limit → the days from the day after notification through the certificate date drop out of the count → then check the one-month safety net. *Luton BC v Haque* says apply those steps in order, not as alternatives. *Raison v DF Capital* [2025] EAT 86 says days of conciliation that fell *before* the limitation clock started (for example conciliation begun while still employed, claim running from a later dismissal date) are not banked.

## 109.3 Agency, contract and outsourced workers — same ladder, extra name on the form

Prompt 97 already established that s.41(4) puts the reasonable-adjustment duty on the principal as well as the employer, that Schedule 8 splits *which* adjustment sits with whom, and that AWR’s 12-week qualifier is not an RA waiting period. This prompt adds only the procedural consequence:

- Decide, on the facts, who did or failed to do the thing (agency booking desk, hirer’s meeting culture, both).
- Notify Acas against **each** intended respondent. A certificate naming only the agency does not automatically cover the hirer.
- Plead s.41 against the principal and, where relevant, s.39 / Part 5 against the employing agency; s.109/s.110 if the complaint is about an individual’s acts in the course of employment or agency.
- Expect a preliminary issue. *Ekpo v Harvey Nichols* is a worked example of the tribunal deciding whether the claimant was a contract worker and who the principal was. *Djalo v SsJ* [2025] EAT 67 is a reminder that s.41 does not stretch to every comparison a contract worker wants to run (there, pay versus direct employees). Neither case invents a different grievance or Acas step.

There is no extra pre-action protocol for agency workers. There is an extra risk of suing the wrong entity.
