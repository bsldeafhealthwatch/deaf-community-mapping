# Prompt 74 — Informal child interpreting: safeguarding, education, and language-development evidence

**Master index prompt 74.** Third file in the CODA batch (prompts 72–77). Compiled 10 September 2026. Dossier rule: stay unbiased; follow money and fulfilment.

**Hard “don’t” for any public page.** Do **not** write that using a child to interpret for a Deaf parent is “illegal” or “against safeguarding law” in England. This pass found **policy and professional guidance** that says it is inappropriate / must not be done — not a named criminal offence and not a sentence in *Working Together to Safeguard Children* that uses the words “Deaf parent” and “child interpreter.” Follow the same discipline as Templates prompt 49.

**Split reminder.** This prompt is about **hearing children** brokering for a Deaf parent. It is not about a Deaf child using BSL at school. Deaf-of-Deaf is prompt 75.

---

## Headline

England has a **professional-norm stack**, not a named statutory “never use a CODA child” clause.

- DHSC *Language interpreting and translation: migrant health guide* (updated guide; “It is inappropriate to use children as interpreters”).
- NHSScotland Interpreting, Communication Support and Translation National Policy: **“It is never appropriate under any circumstances to use a child under the age of 16 as an interpreter.”**
- Local safeguarding procedures (Sussex, London) state a child should never be used as an interpreter in child-protection work, and name BSL as one of the communication methods for which a professional must be considered.
- Equality Act 2010 s.20 is the duty on the GP, trust or school to make a reasonable adjustment — usually a professional BSL interpreter — for the **Deaf adult**. It does not create a stand-alone criminal offence if a child steps in when the adjustment fails.

No national finding was found that hearing CODA children are routinely identified as **young carers** under Children and Families Act 2014 s.96 / Children Act 1989 s.17ZA. The statutory definition *could* stretch to language brokering as “care,” but official young-carer guidance does not name BSL child-interpreting. That is a recognition gap, not proof that the duty is being used.

UK-specific research on the effect of habitual brokering **for a Deaf parent** exists and is not only an analogy from immigrant spoken-language brokering: **Jemina Napier** (Heriot-Watt) is the named UK lead. It is still a small, qualitative evidence base.

## 74.1 Does UK statutory guidance name children interpreting for a Deaf parent?

### What is statutory

**Working Together to Safeguard Children** (DfE; current package includes a 2026 statutory framework PDF on GOV.UK) is the multi-agency safeguarding code. It does **not**, on the public GOV.UK landing text reviewed on this pass, contain a dedicated paragraph on hearing children interpreting for Deaf parents. Absence in the national code is not permission.

**Children and Families Act 2014 s.96** inserts Children Act 1989 s.17ZA: a local authority in England **must** assess a “young carer” who may have needs for support. “Young carer” = a person under 18 who provides or intends to provide care for another person (not under a contract or as volunteering, unless the relationship makes it appropriate to treat them as a young carer anyway). The Act does **not** list “interpreting for a Deaf parent” as an example.

### What is official guidance, not a criminal statute

| Instrument | What it actually says | Deaf-parent specific? | Force |
|---|---|---|---|
| DHSC migrant health guide, “Language interpreting and translation” | Professional interpreter should always be offered. “It is inappropriate to use children as interpreters.” Children lack competency/health literacy; risk of vicarious trauma. | Written for spoken-language migrant health; the “children” rule is general and applies equally to BSL if a service follows the guide | Official guidance (England) |
| NHSScotland Interpreting, Communication Support and Translation National Policy | Children under 16 should **never** be used as interpreters. Family/friends not recommended except emergency. Separate BSL primary-care companion document: face-to-face interpreter must always be provided for children; if child-protection concerns, book a **separate** BSL interpreter for the parent so the child’s voice is heard | Yes on BSL in the companion document; the under-16 ban is general | National policy for NHS Scotland |
| NHS England AIS (DAPB1605) | Requires identification and meeting of communication needs, including BSL. Does not say “do not use the patient’s child.” | Deaf adult’s access, not the child’s welfare | Information standard (“must comply” from Jul 2025 — see prompt 34) |
| Equality Act 2010 s.20 | Anticipatory reasonable adjustment. Provider should not make the Deaf adult bring their own interpreter | Duty is owed to the disabled person (the parent), not framed as a child-protection offence | Statute |
| Sussex / London child-protection procedures | Accredited interpreters/signers must be considered; “A child should never be used as an interpreter.” BSL named among methods | Child-protection context; BSL named, CODA role not named | Local safeguarding procedures |

**What a school, GP or hospital should do instead** (the live instruction, compiled from the stack above): book a professional BSL/English interpreter (NRCPD/RSLI in health and safeguarding settings); do not use the child; if the family arrives with the child already interpreting, rearrange with a professional; in child-protection work, never use the child and do not use family as the interpreter for the parent.

## 74.2 Is a hearing CODA ever formally a “young carer”?

**Documented recognition gap. Not a documented national practice.**

The s.96 definition is wide enough that habitual interpreting, appointment-booking, form-filling and “being the ears” *could* be treated as providing care. No DfE, NHS England or Children’s Commissioner publication found on this pass names “child of a Deaf parent who interprets” as a young-carer cohort.

Why the role is easy to miss:

- The parent is not necessarily “ill.” Cultural-linguistic Deafhood is not an Activities-of-Daily-Living care need in the Carers’ Assessment template most schools use.
- The child looks like a competent bilingual helper, not a young carer missing school to wash or medicate a parent.
- Young-carer identification duties sit with the local authority and schools; they are not triggered automatically by a BSL flag on the parent’s GP record.

College of Policing first-person piece (25 May 2023) by a UK CODA-turned-officer describes exactly this role — “de-facto interpreter… conveying personal and complex information” — and flags vicarious trauma and contamination of a child’s evidence in domestic-abuse settings. That is professional reflection, not a young-carer statistical series.

**Confidence:** No reliable figure found for how many hearing CODA children are on a young-carer register.

## 74.3 UK research on brokering *for a Deaf parent* — not just the immigrant-CLB analogy

Child language brokering (CLB) in spoken-language migrant families is a large literature. Sign-language brokering (SLB) is smaller. The UK has a named researcher and published outputs. Do not pretend the UK evidence base is empty; do not pretend it is a randomised trial.

| Output | What it is | Population | UK-specific? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Napier, *Sign Language Brokering in Deaf-Hearing Families* (Palgrave, 2021) | Mixed-methods monograph. International survey n=240 heritage signers, 16 countries; interviews in Australia; **UK group interviews with 17 young hearing heritage signers aged 5–15 and 10 Deaf parents** (vignettes + artwork / photo elicitation) | Heritage signers (hearing and Deaf) with Deaf parents | **Yes — UK child/parent arm** |
| Napier & Ackroyd, *Communication & Medicine* 18(2), 2022 | Semi-structured interviews: hearing heritage signers brokering between Deaf signing parents and **UK healthcare professionals** | Young hearing people, UK | **Yes** |
| Napier et al., Sign LOUD (2025) | Deaf mothers, domestic abuse, communication, children brokering in reporting — Heriot-Watt / Police Scotland region partners | Deaf mothers + practitioners, Scotland | **Yes** |
| CODA UK & Ireland blog of the 2021 book (4 May 2021) | Author summary: SLB from a young age, in a range of settings, “mostly unseen… regardless of the availability of professional interpreters.” Themes: gift; shame resilience; cooperation/responsibility | Same study | UK-hosted summary |

Napier’s framing is important for public copy: brokering is not only harm. Families also describe it as cooperation and identity. A briefing that only prints “parentification / trauma” is incomplete against her published themes. A briefing that only prints “it’s a gift” is also incomplete — the same body of work records healthcare and safeguarding settings as high-risk.

**Do not treat US CODA psychology papers (Preston 1995; Buchino 1993 parentification) as UK policy evidence.** They are usable as international literature with the label on.

## 74.4 Documented “do not use a child under 18 as interpreter” policies

England does **not** have a single NHS England circular that says “no one under 18 may interpret for a Deaf parent at a medical appointment” in those words.

What does exist:

- DHSC migrant health guide: inappropriate to use children (no age cut in the sentence quoted; “children”).
- NHSScotland national policy: never under 16.
- Individual English trusts (example: Avon and Wiltshire Mental Health Partnership policy extract on this pass) define a child as under 18 and say it is inappropriate under any circumstances to use children as interpreters for clinical information, sensitive conversations or consent; rebook with a professional.
- Local safeguarding procedures: never use a child as interpreter in child-protection work.
- AIS / Equality Act: professional communication support for the Deaf adult.

**Scotland is the tighter written comparator inside the UK.** England’s written rule is “inappropriate” plus local safeguarding “never” in CP work, plus the Equality Act adjustment duty. That is enough for a complaint and a professional-standards case. It is not enough to print “illegal.”

## Public-copy line that survives prompt 49

“NHS and safeguarding guidance in the UK say a child should not be used as an interpreter. Scotland’s national interpreting policy says never under 16. England’s published health guide says it is inappropriate. The Equality Act requires the service, not the family, to provide a professional interpreter for a Deaf parent. There is no separate criminal offence titled ‘using a CODA child to interpret.’”
