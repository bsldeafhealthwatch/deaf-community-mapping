## Headline

**CODA** in UK organisational use means a person of any age with one or more Deaf parents, whether or not they sign. In practice the word is used almost entirely for **hearing** children. A **Deaf child of Deaf parents** is a different group — usually labelled Deaf-of-Deaf / DoD / DOD — and is not counted by any UK official survey as its own population.

Two separate “90 percent” statements circulate and are routinely mixed up:

1. **Most children of Deaf parents are hearing** (Schein’s 1989 “90-percent rule,” widely quoted including by CODA UK & Ireland).
2. **Most Deaf children are born to hearing parents** (NDCS public statistic: “more than 90%,” last reviewed May 2025). NDCS’s own footnote for that line is **Mitchell and Karchmer 2004**, a **United States** Annual Survey paper that actually argues the neat “10% Deaf-of-Deaf” figure is an overstatement for US special-education students.

**No UK body publishes an estimate of hearing children with at least one Deaf parent.** Census 2021 records main language (BSL = 22,000 people aged 3+ in England and Wales) and household language combinations, but not parental hearing status. CRIDE counts deaf children by communication mode and placement, not by whether the parent is Deaf. That absence is the finding.

## 72.2 The two “90 percent” statements — keep them apart

### Statement A — children of Deaf parents are mostly hearing

CODA UK & Ireland: “Most people with Deaf parents are themselves hearing — an often quoted figure places the proportion of hearing CODAs at 90% of the Deaf-parented population.”

This is Schein’s 1989 “90-percent rule” as restated in US and international Deaf-studies literature: ~90% of children born to Deaf parents are hearing, **and** ~90% of Deaf children have hearing parents. It is a rule of thumb, not a UK census table.

**Confidence:** Contested as a precise percentage; directionally accepted.

### Statement B — Deaf children are mostly born to hearing parents

NDCS Deaf statistics page, last reviewed **May 2025**:

> “More than 90% of deaf children are born to hearing parents, who may be completely new to deafness.”

NDCS footnote [2] is **Mitchell, R.E. and Karchmer, M.A. (2004) ‘Chasing the Mythical Ten Percent: Parental Hearing Status of Deaf and Hard of Hearing Students in the United States,’ *Sign Language Studies* 4: 138–163.**

That paper is US Annual Survey of Deaf and Hard of Hearing Children and Youth (1999–2000). Its actual finding is sharper than the slogan NDCS prints:

- fewer than **5%** of DHH students in US special education were *known* to have at least one deaf parent;
- the authors put an **upper bound below 8%** on Deaf-of-Deaf prevalence in that dataset;
- the “mythical ten percent” is described as overstated for that population.

BDA’s *BSL in Our Hands* campaign (launched 18 March 2024) uses the same “90% born to hearing parents” line against a “50,000 deaf children” denominator.

**There is no UK-specific published percentage of Deaf children who have Deaf parents.** NDCS, BDA and parliamentary briefings are recycling a US-derived “more than 90% hearing parents” line. Treating “~5–10% Deaf-of-Deaf” as a measured UK figure would be a citation error.

**Confidence:** Direction (vast majority of UK Deaf children have hearing parents) — Verified as the consensus used by NDCS/BDA. Exact UK percentage — No reliable figure found.

## 72.3 Is there a UK population estimate for hearing CODA?

**No reliable figure found.**

What exists instead:

| Dataset | What it counts | What it does not count |
|---|---|---|
| Census 2021, England and Wales | BSL as **main language**: **22,000** usual residents aged 3+ (0.04%). England ~21,000; Wales ~900. Up from 15,000 in 2011. | Hearing status of parents. Whether a BSL-using adult has a hearing child. Adult CODAs whose main language is English. |
| Census 2021 household language (TS025 / multiple main languages) | Whether adults and children in a household share English (or English/Welsh) as a main language. | Does not isolate “Deaf BSL parent + hearing English-speaking child.” |
| CRIDE 2025 UK-wide | **53,225** deaf children and young people under 20 reported by services (England 45,902; NI 1,527; Scotland 3,649; Wales 2,147). | Parental hearing status. Not in the questionnaire. |
| School Census (England) | Children with hearing impairment as primary or secondary SEN. 2024/25: 28,958 primary+secondary vs 45,902 CRIDE England. | Parental hearing status. |
| ONS / APS health conditions | Self-reported hearing difficulty in adults. | Not linked to children in the household as a CODA flag. |

A back-of-envelope from “Deaf adults have children, ~90% hearing” is not a population estimate. Do not invent one from Census BSL × fertility. Fertility of Deaf adults is not published as an official UK table that would support that sum.

Napier’s 2021 mixed-methods study is the closest UK research sample, not a census: international survey n=240 heritage signers across 16 countries; UK group interviews with **17 young hearing heritage signers aged 5–15** and **10 Deaf parents**.

## 72.4 Has the UK ever collected CODA as a group?

**Not in official statistics, health records, or the School Census.**

Checked on this pass:

- ONS Census 2021 language and household-language outputs — no CODA / parental-hearing variable.
- CRIDE annual service survey 2011–2026 — counts deaf children, communication, ToD posts, resource provisions. Parental hearing status is not a published field. A 2026 England core+thematic questionnaire example (Barnsley FOI) asks about other needs, not parental deafness.
- NHS newborn hearing screening / NHSP datasets — identify the child’s hearing, not whether the parent is Deaf.
- DfE SEND / EHCP statistical releases — “hearing impairment” as primary need. No parent-hearing split.

What does exist is **research and community self-identification**:

- CODA UK & Ireland CIC membership and camp registration (self-declared “child has a Deaf parent”).
- Napier (Heriot-Watt) sign-language-brokering programme, including a 2022 *Communication & Medicine* paper on young hearing people brokering between Deaf signing parents and UK healthcare professionals.
- Older international literature (Paul Preston, *Mother Father Deaf*, 1995 — US interviews) that UK writing still cites.

That is not a national count.
