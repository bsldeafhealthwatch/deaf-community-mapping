# Grok research prompt batch — Prompts 72–77: CODA (Children of Deaf Adults)

Numbered per the project's master prompt index (`prompts/SuperGrok_Research_Prompt_Pack.md` in the DeafCommunityMapping repo — confirmed next free number was **72** at time of writing, per the index's own "⚠️ NEXT PROMPT NUMBER" line; **update the index and that line in the same edit that adds this batch**).

**Why this batch exists.** The project has covered Deaf and DeafBlind adults across health, benefits, tax, legislation, and later life, but has never looked at CODA — children of Deaf adults — as their own group. This was a genuine gap, not a deferred item. CODA is not one population: a Deaf parent can have a Deaf child or a hearing child, and the two sit in very different positions. A Deaf child of Deaf parents (sometimes called a "Deaf-of-Deaf" child, DoDA) usually has full first-language access at home and different needs from a hearing sibling in the same family. A **hearing** CODA has full spoken-language capability but is not "just like any other hearing child" — the documented issue is informal interpreting: a hearing child of a Deaf parent is often relied on, formally or by default, to interpret medical, legal, financial or school information for their own parent, sometimes from a very young age. That is a distinct child-welfare and language-development question, separate from anything already in this project's AIS, DWP, or HMRC batches, which all assume a Deaf **adult** as the person needing access.

**Checked against existing prompts first.** No existing batch in this project's index covers CODA, hearing or Deaf, in any form. Prompts 34–37 (AIS) and 55–61 (HMRC) touch on family members being used as ad-hoc interpreters only as a side finding about the Deaf adult's access, not as a topic about the child's own welfare, education, or identity. This is the first dedicated batch on CODA. Flag clearly anywhere the evidence is thin, unpublished, or anecdotal only — do not fill a gap with a plausible-sounding generalisation, and do not collapse "hearing CODA" and "Deaf child of Deaf parents" into one figure or one set of needs — they are not interchangeable groups.

**72. Definitions, population, and the hearing/Deaf split within CODA**

As a UK Deaf-studies and family-sociology researcher, using the National Deaf Children's Society (NDCS), the British Deaf Association (BDA), academic literature on CODA (Children of Deaf Adults) and Deaf-of-Deaf children, and any UK census or ONS-adjacent data, establish: (1) the standard definitions used in the UK and internationally for "CODA," "Deaf-of-Deaf" (a Deaf child with Deaf parents), "KODA" (Kids of Deaf Adults, sometimes used for children still growing up as distinct from adult CODAs), and "SODA"/"GODA" (siblings/grandchildren of Deaf adults) if these terms are in active UK use; (2) whether any UK body publishes an estimate of the number of hearing children with at least one Deaf parent, and separately the number of Deaf children with Deaf parents, and how reliable those estimates are (most Deaf children are born to hearing parents — confirm the commonly cited ~90-95% figure and its source before treating it as settled, and confirm whether a UK-specific figure exists or whether this is only ever cited internationally); (3) whether the UK collects or has ever attempted to collect data specifically on CODA as a group in any official survey, health record, school census, or research study. Flag plainly if no UK population estimate exists — that absence is itself a finding, not a failed search. Give source title, publishing body, date, and direct URL for each element.

**73. Existing UK support, advocacy, and community organisations for CODA**

As a researcher cataloguing UK Deaf-family and CODA-specific support resources, using organisations such as CODA UK & Ireland (or any successor/current name), National Deaf Children's Society (NDCS) family support services, the BDA, Deaf-led family charities, and any UK university or third-sector CODA research/support project, find every current UK organisation, helpline, peer-support group, summer camp, or online community that specifically supports children (not adult CODAs looking back, unless that is genuinely the only UK provision available) growing up with one or more Deaf parents. For each one found, give: the organisation name, direct URL, whether it is still active (check for a live event, post, or contact response within the last 12 months — many CODA support groups are volunteer-run and can go dormant), whether it specifically distinguishes hearing CODA support from Deaf-of-Deaf child support or treats "CODA" as one undifferentiated group, and whether it targets children currently growing up in a Deaf-parented household versus adults reflecting on their own CODA childhood. This prompt exists specifically to check whether meaningful UK-specific support already exists (as opposed to only the well-known US organisation CODA International) — if a strong existing resource is found, say so plainly rather than assuming a gap.

**74. Informal child interpreting — safeguarding, education, and language-development evidence**

As a UK child-welfare and education-policy researcher, using Ofsted, the Children's Commissioner for England, safeguarding guidance from local authorities or the Department for Education, NHS/GP guidance on using family members as interpreters, and peer-reviewed or charity-published research on "young carers" or "child language brokering," establish: (1) whether any UK statutory guidance explicitly names children being relied on to interpret for a Deaf parent (as distinct from interpreting between two different spoken languages, which has separate published NHS/school guidance) as a safeguarding or young-carer consideration, and if so what it says a school, GP practice, or hospital should do instead; (2) whether a hearing child interpreting for a Deaf parent is ever formally recognised and supported as a "young carer" under the Children and Families Act 2014 s.96/97 identification duty, or whether this is a documented gap where schools and GPs do not recognise the role because it looks different from caring for an illness or disability of the parent's own daily living needs; (3) any UK research (education, psychology, linguistics) on the effect of habitual child-language-brokering specifically for a Deaf parent on the child's own education, wellbeing, or relationship with English/BSL as languages, separate from general child-language-brokering literature about bilingual immigrant families, which is a larger and better-studied but distinct population; (4) any documented NHS or school policy stating that a child under 18 should not be used as an interpreter for their Deaf parent's own medical or safeguarding-relevant appointments. Flag clearly where UK-specific evidence is thin and where findings are being drawn from the general child-language-brokering literature by analogy rather than from CODA-specific research.

**75. Deaf-of-Deaf children — early language access, education, and identity provision**

As a UK Deaf-education researcher, using the National Deaf Children's Society, the British Association of Teachers of the Deaf, Department for Education SEND guidance, and any published research on Deaf children of Deaf parents specifically, establish what is documented about the position of a **Deaf** child born to Deaf parent(s), as distinct from a hearing CODA: (1) whether UK early-years and school guidance recognises that a Deaf-of-Deaf child typically has full native BSL access from birth and different language-development needs and outcomes compared with the ~90%+ of Deaf children born to hearing parents who often experience language deprivation; (2) whether this distinction is reflected anywhere in SEND assessment, Educational Health and Care Plan (EHCP) guidance, or Teacher of the Deaf caseload practice, or whether "Deaf child" is treated as one undifferentiated category regardless of parental hearing status; (3) any documented identity, community, or belonging research on Deaf-of-Deaf children specifically (sometimes discussed in Deaf-studies literature as holding a distinct status within Deaf culture) and whether any UK Deaf school, unit, or organisation runs anything specific for this group; (4) whether mainstream ("hearing") school placement decisions or Local Offer guidance for Deaf children reference parental Deaf status as a relevant factor at all. Flag clearly where this is discussed only in international (especially US) Deaf-studies literature with no confirmed UK-specific source, rather than presenting international academic framing as established UK policy or practice.

**76. International comparative practice — child-interpreting prevention and CODA support**

As a UK comparative-policy researcher, using published policy, legal, and charity-sector sources from at least three comparator countries or nations (e.g. the USA, a Nordic country, Australia, or a devolved UK nation such as Scotland or Wales if provision differs from England), find any legal restriction, professional-interpreting-service guarantee, or funded child/family support model specifically designed to prevent a hearing child from being used as an interpreter for a Deaf parent, and separately, any funded or long-running national CODA support/education programme aimed at children currently growing up in Deaf-parented households (not only adult-reflection organisations). For each example found, state clearly what the policy or programme actually requires or provides, whether it is a legal duty or a voluntary/charity provision, how it is funded, and whether it is aimed at hearing CODAs, Deaf-of-Deaf children, or does not distinguish between them. Note explicitly if a jurisdiction is often cited as a model but on closer inspection turns out to be aspirational guidance rather than an enforceable right or a funded, delivered service — the project's standard is what is actually delivered, not what is stated as policy.

**77. Lived experience and the case for change — CODA-specific testimony and documented harm**

As a researcher compiling lived-experience evidence for a UK Deaf-access advocacy dossier, using published interviews, academic case studies, charity-published first-person accounts, documentaries, or memoirs by UK-based (or UK-relevant if no UK source exists) hearing CODAs and Deaf-of-Deaf adults reflecting on their childhood, find sourced, attributable testimony (naming the person or clearly citing the publication if anonymised) that speaks to: (1) being relied on as a child to interpret medical, legal, financial, or school information for a Deaf parent, and any documented consequence (missed school, distress, an incident where a child-interpreted medical or safeguarding conversation went wrong); (2) the experience of growing up as a Deaf-of-Deaf child within Deaf culture and community, as a positive or contrasting account to the more commonly documented isolation of Deaf children with hearing parents; (3) any UK charity, school, or advocacy body that has publicly called for a specific policy change (e.g. mandatory professional interpreter provision so children are never used, formal young-carer recognition, a national CODA support service) and what that specific ask is, with a source and date. Distinguish clearly between UK-sourced testimony and international (largely US) sourced testimony, since the bulk of published CODA lived-experience material is American. Flag if the available UK material is thin — that thinness is itself a usable finding for this project's "gap, not filled guess" standard, not a reason to substitute international testimony as if it were describing the UK system.

### Notes for this batch specifically

- Prompt 72 is the most important gate in the batch — do not let Grok default to a single "CODA" figure that quietly means only hearing CODAs, or only Deaf-of-Deaf children. Every later prompt in this batch depends on that split being kept clean.
- Prompt 73 should be treated with the same "confirm it's still alive" scepticism as this project applied to volunteer-run Deaf-elder groups (see the Deaf-older batch, prompts 66–71) — CODA peer-support groups in the UK have a documented history of starting, going quiet, and not being formally wound up.
- Prompt 74 is where a hard "don't" is most likely needed before anything goes public: do not let a public page state that using a child to interpret for a Deaf parent is "illegal" or "against safeguarding law" unless this prompt finds a specific, named duty that says so — follow the same discipline as the Templates section's prompt 49 Don't list.
- As with every prior batch, flag confidence per finding rather than presenting anything as settled — this project's standard is "sourced and dated," not "asserted."
