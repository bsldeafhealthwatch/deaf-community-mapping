    Contested & unstable figures

    Where a source disputes itself, or a figure is actively changing, it goes here first — flagged, not silently corrected. Resolved cases stay visible so the process itself is auditable.

    RESOLVED — RADF full conformance deadline is 30 September 2026

    Directly verified against two independent NHS England primary sources on 4 September 2026: the DAPB4019 standards summary page states "Full conformance dates: 30 September 2026." The full 44-page Implementation Guidance document (Amd 41/2025) was read in full — its Section 12 timescale table states "Date of full conformance: full implementation of all aspects of the Standard is required. By 30 Sept 2026." No 2027 date of any kind appears in either document.

    A cross-check via SuperGrok independently claimed a split date — 31 March 2027 for health, 31 October 2027 for social care — citing the same Amd 41/2025 documents as its source. That claim could not be reproduced when the actual documents were read directly. Lesson for the team: an AI cross-check citing a real document is not the same as that document actually containing the claim. Always open the cited page.

    The underlying admin-log flux (a product-owner request to change the date on 13 Aug 2026, reverted 3 Sept 2026) is real and shows the date has been under internal discussion — but the currently confirmed, in-force date is 30 September 2026.

    The £80–100m NHS cost figure is a charity estimate, not an independent costing

    This figure's origin is now traced precisely: SignHealth inflation-adjusted its own 2014 Sick Of It report figure (£30m) to arrive at "£80–100 million every year" as a present-day estimate — its own campaign page states this explicitly. The UK government's own Locked Out report (Nov 2025) then cites this SignHealth estimate directly in a footnote, presenting it as the cost "to HM Treasury."

    This is not dishonest on SignHealth's part — the methodology is stated openly. But no independent NHS or Treasury costing of Deaf healthcare accessibility failures appears to exist anywhere. A government report is repeating a charity's a decade-old estimate, inflation-adjusted, as if it were measured. That gap — not the figure itself — is the story.

    The vanishing 73,000: how many Deaf BSL users are there in England?

    The figure of 73,000 Deaf BSL users in England was used as a headline number throughout this project's earlier NHS interpreter-provision research (originating in RNID/SignHealth's Still Ignored, April 2025) — but a direct check of RNID's own current prevalence page finds no trace of it. Instead RNID's own material gives three different figures depending on definition: 22,000 (2021 Census, main-language, England & Wales), ~25,000 (RNID's own UK-wide main-language estimate), and 151,000 (BDA's wider figure, of which 87,000 are described as "deaf"). A fourth figure, ~87,000 (England & Wales), appears in a separate SWL-specific Healthwatch report. RNID itself states plainly there is "no consensus" on the total.

    The 73,000 figure has been retired from this dossier's NHS interpreter-provision domain. Any public-facing use of a BSL-user population figure should name its source and definition every time, rather than presenting a single number as settled.

    SWLSTG Corner House: the UK's only national Deaf CAMHS inpatient unit has closed (1 July 2026)

    An earlier version of this dossier flagged an unresolved age-range discrepancy for Corner House (11–17 per the trust's current page vs 6–18 per a 2021 trust video). Follow-up research (6 September 2026) resolved this as a genuine historical service change, not a citation error: the pre-2022 configuration served ages 8–18 (independently confirmed via The Limping Chicken, 21 Dec 2022), the service then closed for review and reopened 1 June 2023 with a narrower 11–17, 24/5 model — and it is this reopened version that most of this project's research describes.

    More significantly, the same research found that the inpatient unit has since closed again — this time permanently. SWLSTG's own news page (1 July 2026) states the inpatient service has closed, with its funding redirected to four new regional specialist community/outreach teams (South East, Central, North, South West), following an NHS England review conducted with Deaf young people, families, clinicians, and community representatives. Stated rationale: inpatient referrals had remained very low against roughly 200 referrals/year to the specialist community team, and the change follows the NHS 10 Year Plan's shift toward community-based care. Do not describe Corner House as a currently-operating inpatient service in any future use of this material.

    The grassroots-voice gap: does an independent Deaf voice on NHS access exist, and if not, why not?

    Two full rounds of research, including live social-media search, found the same pattern: charity, campaign, and institutional voices are well represented online, but individual Deaf BSL users' own first-hand accounts of NHS access — especially anything SWL-specific — are essentially absent. This is not simply a search failure. Three independent UK sources (the BSL Advisory Board's Locked Out report, a GOV.UK accessibility co-design study, and a financial-services regulator's own guidance) each independently confirm that BSL is a distinct visual-spatial language, not signed English — meaning that composing a fluent written-English complaint is a genuine second-language task for BSL-dominant people, not a natural extension of how they communicate.

    A grassroots BSL voice does exist, but on video platforms (YouTube, Facebook) and is almost entirely charity-produced rather than independently authored; no genuinely independent, SWL-local BSL grassroots account discussing NHS access was found anywhere. The practical implication has shifted from "search harder" to "this evidence base may need to be actively created" — for example via an organised, consent-based BSL-language community event with recorded video output, following the model of a Healthwatch Lancashire event that led to an ongoing local ICB relationship.

    PSR is now a confirmed funding route for Deaf/hearing-sector VCSE organisations — but only for two organisations, and unevenly

    The NHS Provider Selection Regime has now been directly confirmed as having funded VCSE organisations three times: SignHealth twice (Derby & Derbyshire ICB, £491,036/year for BSL-specific Talking Therapies; and South East London ICB, an urgent-circumstances stopgap award) and RNID once (Somerset NHS Foundation Trust, £46,000 for general hearing-aid aftercare — not BSL-specific). No equivalent PSR award was found for BDA, Action Deafness, Deafblind UK, or RAD in this research.

    This nuances the "PSR is a working route" finding: PSR works, but for a narrow set of organisations, and the BSL-specific clinical-therapy niche within it currently belongs to SignHealth alone.

    RESOLVED — the 19-vs-8 Deafblind interpreter count was a timeline, not a contradiction

    Directly resolved via NRCPD's live registration-figures table (checked 5 September 2026): the figures of 19 and 8 Registered Interpreters for Deafblind People (RIDB) are both real — they are snapshots of the same national register at different dates, not two competing claims. The register showed roughly 12 nationally around 2022; from 12 February 2025 (when NRCPD confirmed the figure to journalist Liam O'Dell amid a petition by Sarah Reed) through the government's 23 June 2026 funding announcement, the figure cited was 8 — used explicitly as that announcement's "before" baseline, with a target of 68 ("more than eightfold") funded by a £15,000 grant for competency-assessment weekends. As of 5 September 2026, the live NRCPD table shows 19, as those assessment weekends add successful candidates to the register.

    The number that actually matters for South West/South East London planning is neither 19 nor 8: only 4 of the 19 current RIDBs nationally sit in the combined London + South East regions, and just 1 in London itself. That is the correct local-capacity baseline. It also newly surfaced that DA Languages and LanguageLine — the two agencies already confirmed holding South London GP/hospital interpreting contracts — do not hold their own Deafblind interpreting bench, and instead subcontract that work to Deafblind Enablement, meaning DBE's own thin national capacity is the real ceiling on any Deafblind booking under those contracts, regardless of which prime the booking nominally runs through.

    See the DeafBlind-Specific Provision domain above for the full breakdown, the BSL Advisory Board's "Locked Out" findings and commissioning recommendations, and what remains genuinely unpublished (RIDB fulfilment rates, occupancy, and how many of the 19 actually take NHS work).
