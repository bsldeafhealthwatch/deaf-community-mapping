1. Population data

UK-wide figures, then South West London ICB local figures where they exist. Where no reliable local figure exists, this is stated plainly rather than guessed.

UK-wide

  • 18 million adults in the UK are deaf or have hearing loss or tinnitus. (RNID, still the current working figure as of January 2026.)
  • The number of British Sign Language users is genuinely disputed and depends on definition. Do not quote one number as "the" figure. Four figures are all in current official and advisory use: 22,000 people gave British Sign Language as their main language in the 2021 Census for England and Wales; around 25,000 is the UK-wide main-language estimate; 87,000 is the estimated number of deaf British Sign Language users; 151,000 is the total estimate of British Sign Language users of any kind, used throughout the November 2025 Locked Out report. An older figure of 73,000, used in earlier versions of this briefing, should be considered replaced by this range.
  • 53,225 deaf children and young people live in the UK, a 2 per cent decrease from the year before. Of these, 45,902 are in England. (Consortium for Research into Deaf Education, 2025 report.)
  • More than 450,000 people in the UK are deafblind, projected to rise past 610,000 by 2035 as the population ages. (Sense and Deafblind UK, modelled estimate.)
  • Estimates for Usher syndrome, the leading cause of acquired deafblindness, range from 5,200 people who are BSL users and affected by Usher syndrome, up to 10,000 to 11,000 people affected by all forms of Usher syndrome combined. The range exists because sources use different definitions.

South West London ICB — verified local data

The six boroughs together have a population of approximately 1.55 million people: Croydon about 410,000; Wandsworth about 340,000; Merton about 219,000; Sutton about 214,000; Richmond about 197,000; Kingston about 173,000.

  • 878 requests for a British Sign Language interpreter were logged at GP practices across South West London between October 2024 and June 2025. This counts requests, not unique patients. By borough: Wandsworth 466, Croydon 185, Sutton 72, Merton 68, Kingston 61, Richmond 26. The high Wandsworth figure is explained by two Deaf care homes run by Achieving Together, and by Oak Lodge School, a specialist Deaf school, both located in that borough.
  • Sutton has the highest hearing loss prevalence in the South West London footprint, at 15.4 per cent of its population, according to an April 2026 analysis of the NHS Hearing Loss Data Tool ranking London boroughs.
  • A rough planning estimate for the deafblind population across the six boroughs, applying national prevalence rates to local population figures, is between 8,000 and 11,000 people. This is a modelled estimate only. No NHS or council dataset specific to deafblind people in South West London currently exists publicly.

Gap: no published local authority sensory registers

No published 2025 to 2026 register totals for deaf or deafblind residents were found for any of the six South West London boroughs: Croydon, Kingston, Merton, Richmond, Sutton, or Wandsworth. Local authorities are required by law to keep such a record for deafblind people. See the Gap Tracker document for the full list of items requiring direct contact to resolve.

2. Policy summary

What has actually changed in NHS policy, and what it does and does not require in practice.

The Accessible Information Standard, updated 30 June 2025

NHS England published an updated version of the Accessible Information Standard, known as AIS, on 30 June 2025. Two changes matter most. First, a new sixth step called "Review" was added: services must now regularly recheck a person's communication needs, not just record them once and leave the record unchanged. Second, every NHS organisation must now name a senior, executive-level person responsible for the standard being met, not only an operational lead.

A separate and more powerful legal change happened alongside this. A section of the Health and Care Act 2022, called section 95, came into force on 7 July 2025. This section allows information standards like the AIS to become legally compulsory, not just advisory. This is a genuine and significant legal change — the Accessible Information Standard is no longer simply "guidance" in the way it was before July 2025.

However, full enforcement is still being built. The Care Quality Commission currently treats the standard as something services "must have regard to," alongside its own inspections, rather than running a separate inspection regime purely for the standard. Every provider is expected to publish a formal self-assessment of how well it meets the standard, but the deadline for that is March 2027, so the practical, on-the-ground enforcement is still around a year away from being fully visible.

A related finding matters here: a Freedom of Information request by RNID, published 27 January 2026, found that 92 per cent of NHS staff in England have not completed training on the Accessible Information Standard. This is the clearest evidence that policy change on paper has not yet reached most frontline staff.

The Single Patient Record

As part of the Government's 10 Year Health Plan, published July 2025, the NHS committed to a Single Patient Record: one secure record for each patient instead of separate records at every service. The full version, accessible through the NHS App, is planned from 2028. An earlier interim target is for clinicians to have improved access to patient records for at least 80 per cent of patients by March 2027. This record is intended to carry a person's communication needs — for example, that someone needs a British Sign Language interpreter — consistently across every NHS service they use, rather than having to explain this every time. A related technical requirement, the Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag, has a compliance deadline for providers of 30 September 2026.

Locked Out — the BSL Advisory Board report, 27 November 2025

This is the most significant Deaf and DeafBlind health policy document published since Still Ignored in April 2025. It was written by Dr Joanna Atkinson for a subgroup of the Government's BSL Advisory Board. It sets out twelve priorities, including: giving people control over booking their own interpreters; full enforcement of the Accessible Information Standard including the new legal powers described above; a national video relay service operating 24 hours a day; a rule that only properly registered interpreters should be used, and specifically that children should never be used as interpreters for their family members; and bringing back specialist sensory impairment teams within councils and the NHS, which have been cut in many areas.

Importantly, the report contains a separate section listing fourteen specific, urgent improvements needed for deafblind people — the first time a major national report has given deafblind people this level of dedicated attention, rather than treating deafblindness as a footnote to deafness.

Care Act 2014 — duties to deafblind people

Local councils have had legal duties toward deafblind people since 2014, and these duties are still current — there is no newer national guidance replacing them. Councils must: keep a record of deafblind people in their area; make sure that anyone assessing a deafblind person's care needs has specific training in deafblindness, not just general social care training; provide specialist one-to-one support workers, called communicator-guides, where needed; and name one senior, director-level officer with overall responsibility for deafblind people's services. This last point mirrors the AIS requirement for a named senior lead described above, and is worth checking for each of the six South West London councils.

3. Organisations: Manage Closely

High power or influence, and high interest or direct involvement in Deaf and DeafBlind healthcare access. These are the most important relationships to maintain actively.

Founded 191118 million+ deaf and hard of hearing people supported UK-widernid.org.uk

The UK's largest charity for deaf people and those with hearing loss. Led the campaign that secured official recognition of British Sign Language as a language of England, Scotland and Wales under the BSL Act 2022. A primary architect of national NHS accessibility policy for deaf people.

Still Ignored, April 2025, co-authored with SignHealth

The most significant deaf healthcare access report published in the UK, based on responses from over 1,100 deaf and hard of hearing people.

  • 70 per cent of respondents were never asked about their communication needs at an NHS appointment.
  • 67 per cent of British Sign Language users had been denied interpretation.
  • 56 per cent said they did not fully understand what they were told at their appointment.
  • Fewer than 1 in 5 British Sign Language users said they trust the NHS to meet their needs.
  • 1 in 10 have avoided calling an ambulance or attending Accident and Emergency.
  • 1 in 4 have avoided seeking help for a new health concern altogether.
RNID's Director of Health, Crystal Rolfe, said: "Imagine not being able to understand a cancer diagnosis, or having to rely on a family member to tell you that you're seriously ill or even dying."

Follow-up finding, 27 January 2026

A Freedom of Information request by RNID found that 92 per cent of NHS staff in England have not completed training on the Accessible Information Standard — the clearest evidence that the June 2025 policy update has not yet reached frontline practice. RNID has said publicly that the update "does too little" on enforcement.

British Sign Language user population — corrected figures

RNID's own prevalence page, updated June 2026, confirms there is no single agreed number for how many people use British Sign Language. Treat any single figure with caution. See the Population section above for the full range of figures now in use.

7. Funding crisis and NHS commissioning routes

Deaf-led organisations across the UK are under serious financial pressure. This section sets out the evidence for that, and the specific, real routes available for the NHS to fund Deaf and DeafBlind organisations directly.

The scale of the problem

Employer National Insurance contributions rose to 15 per cent, and the threshold at which they start being paid fell to £5,000, from 6 April 2025. The National Council for Voluntary Organisations estimates this cost the charity sector £1.4 billion in the 2025 to 2026 financial year. A survey by the Charity Finance Group in November 2025 found that 77 per cent of charity leaders reported their finances were worse than the year before; 35 per cent had made redundancies; and 51 per cent had left staff vacancies unfilled. A survey of disability service providers estimated an average extra cost of £266 million across its membership, against typical profit margins of under 2 per cent — meaning at least one Deaf service provider reported it would have to hand back an NHS contract as financially unviable.

A real example: Deaf Action, Edinburgh

Deaf Action's Deaf Social Worker service was cut in September 2024. Its specialist equipment service, including home alerting systems, was cut in March 2025. Its Community Care Assistant Service was cut in November 2025. Redundancies were confirmed in April 2026, affecting one deaf social worker, two equipment service roles, and one community care assistant role. The charity's income for the year to 31 March 2025 was £3.6 million against spending of £3.9 million.

Chief Executive Philip Gerrard, who holds an MBE, said: "Those services have been dismantled, one after another, and the result is that deaf people are now being pushed into generic systems that are not designed for BSL users."

No UK Government or NHS England national response has followed this — it was treated purely as a local council-level budget decision. This matters: there is currently no national safeguard protecting specialist Deaf services from being cut at local level, even where the human cost is well documented.

Real routes to direct NHS funding

1. The Provider Selection Regime

This is the specific set of rules, distinct from wider public procurement law, that governs how ICBs and the NHS buy healthcare services. It allows a direct award, or an award to "the most suitable provider," without a full competitive tender, where fairness and value for money criteria are met — and inequality reduction and social value are explicitly scored criteria. This is not a theoretical route. SignHealth was awarded a contract worth around £61,500 to provide independent mental health advocacy for Deaf people in Bury in 2025. Cumberland Council tendered a Deaf and dual-sensory advice and assessment service in September 2025, specifically reserved for small and voluntary sector organisations. Note also that these wins are not guaranteed to last: Norfolk Deaf Association, previously an established local audiology provider, lost its contract in a 2025 re-procurement.

2. Section 256 and 257 of the NHS Act 2006

These sections of the law allow the NHS to make payments directly to a voluntary organisation for services that improve or protect people's health, rather than only funding a local council. This remains a lawful and available route, most often used to pool budgets between the NHS and councils. NHS England also publishes a ready-to-use model grant agreement specifically for making grants from an ICB to a voluntary organisation, meaning the legal paperwork does not need to be created from scratch.

3. The Cabinet Office voluntary sector spending target

Government policy from February 2025 requires central government departments to set a two-year target for direct spending with voluntary and community organisations, starting from 1 April 2026. This applies directly to central government, not automatically to local ICBs — but it is the strongest available political precedent to use when asking South West London ICB to set an equivalent local target.

The key finding for South West London

No published example of South West London ICB directly commissioning a Deaf charity or Deaf-led organisation for Accessible Information Standard delivery was found. The British Sign Language interpreting service for GP practices is commissioned from DA Languages, a commercial language agency — not from any Deaf-led organisation. None of the Deaf-led organisations described in this document — deafPLUS, the Royal Association for Deaf People, Merton Centre for Independent Living, or Deaf First — currently hold an NHS contract in South West London on the public record. The patient frustrations documented by Healthwatch South West London's survey may be a direct consequence of this commissioning choice.

What does not currently exist

There is no dedicated national NHS or Government grant scheme specifically for Deaf health projects. The nearest available money is a small £15,000 fund announced in June 2026 to assess the availability of specialist deafblind interpreters — a workforce planning exercise, not a service delivery fund, and far too small to be relied upon. A funding strategy should be built around the real routes described above: Provider Selection Regime contracts, Section 256 and 257 payments, and the NHS model grant agreement — not around a national Deaf-specific grant that does not currently exist.
