# Domain 13: Victims & Prisoners — Deaf/DeafBlind Access to Justice

**Last verified:** 4 September 2026
**Re-check cadence:** Quarterly, or on any Victims and Prisoners Act 2024 commencement update
**Confidence level:** Medium (strong qualitative/case evidence; weak on hard national statistics — see gaps)

---

## Summary

This domain covers two distinct but related populations: Deaf/DeafBlind people as **victims and witnesses** navigating the criminal justice system, and Deaf/DeafBlind people **in custody** as prisoners. Both share a root cause — a justice system built around spoken/written English with only patchy, inconsistently enforced BSL provision — but the failure modes differ. For victims, the issue is access to reporting, victim services, and being believed/understood. For prisoners, it is isolation, safety, and exclusion from rehabilitation. Courts are, per parliamentary testimony, "the only place someone is guaranteed a qualified interpreter" — everywhere else in the justice pipeline (police contact, prison induction, prison healthcare, probation) provision is discretionary and frequently absent.

## Key Organisations & Actors

- **Ministry of Justice (MoJ)** and **HM Prison and Probation Service (HMPPS)** — responsible for prison conditions and reasonable adjustments in custody; found liable (via settlement, not full trial) in the HMP Lewes case
- **British Deaf Association (BDA)** — the primary source of sustained scrutiny on Deaf prisoners specifically, via its 2016 report *Throw Away the Key? How Britain's Prisons Don't Rehabilitate Deaf People* and continued campaigning (most recently tied to BBC's *Reunion*, a drama on Deaf ex-prisoners' experience)
- **SignHealth** — operates **InterpreterNow**, a video relay interpreting service usable in some prison/justice contexts where face-to-face isn't available
- **NRCPD** — the qualification standard the Prison Service is advised (not mandated by hard statute) to require for justice-setting interpreters, distinguishing general BSL competence from the higher bar needed for legal work
- **Police and Crime Commissioners (PCCs)** — under the Victims and Prisoners Act 2024, now have new duties to review and report on local compliance with the Victims' Code, which is a fresh accountability lever worth testing via FOI
- **Independent Public Advocate (IPA)** — new role created by the 2024 Act for major incidents; unclear (not yet researched) whether any guidance addresses Deaf/DeafBlind victims specifically

## Current Policy / Legal Position

- **Victims and Prisoners Act 2024** (c.21, Royal Assent 24 May 2024, commencement ongoing in phases) — the core modern statute. Relevant provisions:
  - Section 5: places a duty on service providers under the Victims' Code to comply "unless they have good reasons not to" — a duty with a built-in discretion clause, worth scrutinising for how "good reasons" gets used to excuse non-provision of interpreters
  - Sections 23–26: new duties on criminal justice inspectorates to consult the Victims' Commissioner
  - Section 27: removes the requirement for a victim to complain via an MP for certain complaints
  - Definition of "victim of crime" (s.1(1)-(2)) does **not** require the crime to have been reported or a conviction secured — relevant for advocacy framing since many Deaf/DeafBlind people may not report at all due to access barriers, and would still count as victims under this Act
- **Equality Act 2010** — same reasonable-adjustments duty underpinning the health domain applies to prisons and courts, and was the legal basis for the HMP Lewes settlement
- **No standalone legal right to a BSL interpreter in custody** — courts are the guaranteed setting; prisons and police custody are not, per direct parliamentary testimony (Cluster C domain 7, legislation, should cross-reference this — the BSL Act 2022 gave BSL legal recognition as a language but did **not** itself create enforceable individual service rights, which is precisely the gap victims and prisoners fall into)

## Known Gaps & Contested Figures

- **No current national statistic for the number of Deaf/DeafBlind prisoners in England & Wales — now confirmed via a specific, current, official source.** A SuperGrok cross-check reportedly found that HMPPS's own **Offender Equalities Annual Report 2024–25** (27 Nov 2025) states disability data "continues to be limited," with published analysis confined mainly to sex, age, ethnicity, and religion. This has not been independently re-opened and read this session, but the claim — that the gap still exists and is officially acknowledged as of Nov 2025 — is consistent with, and considerably strengthens, the standing BDA criticism dating to 2016. **Recommend the team open the actual HMPPS report directly to confirm the exact wording before using it in any public-facing material.**
  - The same cross-check surfaced historic, non-official estimates worth logging clearly as such, not as statistics: BDA's 2016 report states it could identify "approximately 400 prisoners" who had *disclosed* a hearing loss or deafness, while noting the charity itself suspected this figure to be inaccurate; a 2000 figure identified 56 Deaf prisoners; and a 2014 NOMS disclosure reportedly found that of ~1,000 prisoners recorded as "deaf," about 92% were actually hard of hearing rather than BSL-using Deaf people — a useful illustration of how blunt, non-BSL-specific categories obscure the actual BSL-using population size. These figures come from the same BDA 2016 PDF already in this domain's source list; the specific page locations have not been re-checked this session.
- **No current data on BSL provision rates in police custody suites** — nothing found this session; likely needs individual police force FOIs rather than a national figure existing anywhere.
- The 16/30 Disability Liaison Officer BSL-training figure is from **2009**, cited in a **2016** report — 17+ years stale, included here only as historical baseline, not current evidence
- Whether the Victims and Prisoners Act 2024's new PCC reporting duties on Victims' Code compliance will actually surface Deaf-specific data is untested — this is a live opportunity, not yet a fact
- No figures found on **DeafBlind-specific** justice provision (intervenors/guide-communicators in court or custody) — likely intersects with Domain 3 (DeafBlind-specific health provision) and should stay cross-referenced rather than duplicated

## Money Trail

- No dedicated national budget line for BSL interpretation in prisons was found — the pattern mirrors NHS provision (Domain 1): interpreting costs likely sit with individual prison/HMPPS budgets or are handled ad hoc, which is consistent with the "inconsistent and sometimes non-existent" provision reported by multiple sources
- **InterpreterNow** (SignHealth's video relay service) is charity-operated, raising the same VCSE-vs-commercial money-trail question as DA Languages in Domain 1 — worth checking whether HMPPS pays SignHealth per-use, holds a contract, or whether provision is genuinely ad hoc/uncoordinated
- The HMP Lewes legal settlement (quantum not disclosed in source found) represents a real cost to the taxpayer arising directly from a *failure* to provide interpretation — a concrete "cost of non-compliance" data point worth pairing with Domain 12's waste-vs-grassroots framing: reactive legal settlements vs. proactive provision spend

## FOI Targets Arising

1. **HMPPS / MoJ** — number of prisoners in England & Wales who have declared BSL as a primary/preferred communication method, and how this is recorded (or whether it is recorded at all)
2. **Individual prisons (starting with SWL/London-area prisons relevant to the six-borough footprint)** — BSL interpreter booking data for healthcare, disciplinary, and induction appointments over the last 12 months
3. **MoJ** — total value and number of legal claims/settlements since 2020 relating to failure to provide BSL/Deaf communication access in custody (the HMP Lewes case may not be unique)
4. **Local Police and Crime Commissioners (SWL boroughs)** — under the Victims and Prisoners Act 2024's new reporting duty, whether Victims' Code compliance reporting captures communication-access data disaggregated by need type (BSL vs. other)
5. **SignHealth** (not FOI-able as a charity, but a direct information request) — clarify InterpreterNow's usage/funding relationship with HMPPS and any police forces, to establish whether this is a funded contract or informal/charity-subsidised provision
