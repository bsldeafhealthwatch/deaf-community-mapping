# Domain 3: DeafBlind-Specific Provision (Guide-Communicators, Intervenors)

**Last verified:** 5 September 2026
**Re-check cadence:** Quarterly, or as the Jun 2026 interpreter-funding initiative (target 68 interpreters) progresses
**Confidence level:** Medium — the structural finding (no UK national equivalent to the US programme) is solid; the 450,000 population figure's methodology is now directly confirmed (see below), though it should still be quoted with its 2017/2022 basis; interpreter-supply figures remain contested between sources

---

## Summary

There is no UK national statutory equivalent to the US FCC's National Deaf-Blind Equipment Distribution Program — UK deafblind support is fragmented across local-authority sensory services, Care Act/NHS Continuing Healthcare/Direct Payments funding for communicator-guides and intervenors, Access to Work (Domain 5, if employed), and charity provision (Sense, Deafblind UK, local societies), commissioned locally rather than nationally. This fragmentation compounds an already acute interpreter shortage: NRCPD's own register lists just 19 Deafblind interpreters nationally (1 in London), while a June 2026 government announcement cited a starting figure of only 8 and set a funded target of 68 — a more than eight-fold increase — backed by just £15,000 in assessment funding. Population figures are heavily contested depending on definition: over 450,000 people in the UK have combined sight and hearing loss (the broad "deafblind" definition), while roughly 12,000 Deafblind people with communication needs is the much narrower operational figure used to set the interpreter-funding target — these are not interchangeable numbers and conflating them would badly misstate either the scale of need or the adequacy of the current response.

## Current Policy / Legal Position

No dedicated UK national statute or programme provides deafblind equipment or communication support as a matter of national entitlement, unlike the US FCC model. UK provision instead runs through the same general frameworks already documented across this project: the Care Act 2014 (local authority social care duties, not independently reviewed in full this session — see also Domain 7's flagged gap on this Act), NHS Continuing Healthcare eligibility, Direct Payments, and Access to Work where the person is in employment (Domain 5). This means deafblind-specific support is currently a patchwork of general disability/social-care entitlements applied to a specific population, rather than a population-specific statutory scheme — directly comparable to the pattern found in Domain 6 (Neurodivergence overlap), where no dedicated scheme exists either.

## Known Gaps & Contested Figures

- **Deafblind interpreter count: 19 (NRCPD register) vs. 8 (government announcement)** — this is the same contested figure flagged in Domain 8 (Interpreter regulation) and should be resolved once, not independently re-investigated per domain. Possible explanation not yet confirmed: NRCPD's 19 may include registrants who are not currently actively taking Deafblind-specific work, while the government's "8" may reflect a narrower "currently practising" definition — this is speculation and needs direct confirmation from NRCPD or the relevant department.
- **Population figure: 450,000 (broad, combined sight-and-hearing-loss definition) vs. 12,000 (narrow, "with communication needs" definition)** — both figures are used by credible sources but measure fundamentally different populations. The 450,000 figure includes many people whose deafblindness is age-related and mild, not requiring intervenor/communicator-guide support; the 12,000 figure is the operationally relevant one for interpreter-supply planning. Public-facing material must always specify which definition is being used and never present one as if it were the other.
- **£15,000 to grow the interpreter pool more than eight-fold (8 to 68) is a strikingly small sum** relative to the scale of the stated ambition — this is flagged here and in Domain 7/Domain 8 as worth direct scrutiny: is £15,000 genuinely sufficient to fund competency assessments for up to 60 additional interpreters, or is this an underfunded target?
- No figures were found on actual unmet need (e.g., how many deafblind people who want an intervenor/communicator-guide do not currently have access to one) — the domain currently describes system structure and interpreter supply, not a demand-side gap figure.

## Money Trail

- £15,000 (Jun 2026, Signature/NRCPD-delivered) is the only national-level funding figure found specific to Deafblind interpreter capacity — see the proportionality concern above
- Local-authority intervenor/communicator-guide contracts (e.g., Hertfordshire, 18 months) exist but no aggregate national spend figure across all local authorities was found or is likely to exist given the fully devolved commissioning model

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026): 450,000 Figure's Methodology Confirmed, Plus Deafblind UK's August 2025 Impact Report

Direct primary-source retrieval (not a Grok cross-check) of Sense's own "Deafblindness statistics in the UK" page and Deafblind UK's own August 2025 impact report.

**The 450,000 figure (✅ Confirmed methodology, previously only known as a headline number):** Sense's own page states directly: "In 2022 it is estimated that there are over 450,000 people in the UK who are deafblind." The underlying research was commissioned by Sense and carried out by the Operational Research Society in **2017**, using prevalence estimates from the Centre for Disability Research (2010) combined with ONS population projections. Sense projects this will rise to **over 610,000 by 2035**. The figure also carries a demographic breakdown not previously recorded in this project: over 302,000 of the 450,000 are aged 70+; the estimate splits to roughly 237,500 female and 213,700 male. Regionally, Wales has the highest prevalence rate (8.6 per 1,000 population, 2015 data) and Northern Ireland the lowest (5.3 per 1,000). **Important caveat to carry forward:** this is a 2017-vintage model applied with 2022 population data, not a fresh count — it should be described as "Sense's modelled estimate, last recalculated 2022" rather than as a current headline census figure, though it remains the best available UK-wide estimate and is now traced to a named methodology rather than being an unsourced number.

**Deafblind UK's Impact Report 2024-25 (✅ Confirmed, published August 2025 — this is the exact report the original research prompt asked about):**
- Repeats the 450,000 UK figure, noting it is "rising annually"
- **4,346 members** received direct support in 2024-25; **188 unpaid carers** also supported; **1,905 professionals** received training
- **9,312 helpline calls** handled — Deafblind UK's own framing is "approximately one intervention every 15 minutes"
- **473 volunteers**; **599 members** attended "Deafblind Friendly" social events; **1,233 deafblind members** received one-to-one technology support, plus **1,802 non-members** (family/professionals/support staff)
- **1,671 schools** hold the charity's awareness resource pack; **299 awareness talks** delivered to organisations; **1,200+ participants** (described as global) attended the 2024 Deafblind UK Conference
- Self-reported wellbeing improvement among supported members: **2.2 to 4.8 out of 5** (pre/post support) — this is the charity's own outcome measure, not an independently validated clinical scale, and should be attributed as such
- Notable institutional development: launch of the **Deafblind UK Education and Research Centre** at Birmingham City University, described by the charity as "the first of its kind in the UK" — worth independently verifying this "first of its kind" claim before repeating it, as it is exactly the kind of unqualified superlative this project's verification discipline flags for caution
- The charity states it works across 21 different ethnic groups and multiple communication methods (Deafblind Manual, BSL, Makaton)

**What this does NOT tell us:** the report is an organisational impact/activity report, not a needs assessment — it measures Deafblind UK's own service reach, not unmet demand. The "one call every 15 minutes" framing is a helpline-volume statistic, not a wait-time or capacity-adequacy figure. This project's existing gap (no data found anywhere on actual unmet need for intervenor/communicator-guide support) remains unresolved by this new source.
