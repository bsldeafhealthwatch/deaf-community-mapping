## Summary

The legal duty to provide BSL interpretation in NHS settings rests on the Equality Act 2010's "reasonable adjustments" requirement and the Accessible Information Standard (AIS), but implementation is inconsistent, unenforced, and — per the most authoritative recent research — failing the majority of Deaf BSL users. RNID and SignHealth's *Still Ignored* report (April 2025) is currently the best-evidenced national picture. Funding is fragmented (no ring-fenced national interpreting budget; costs sit with individual NHS bodies/ICBs), workforce supply is a hard constraint (7-year training pipeline, small interpreter pool), and a key national digital fix — the Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag — has an implementation deadline that is, as of this week, unresolved even within NHS England's own published record.

## Key Organisations & Actors

- **RNID** and **SignHealth** — co-authors of *Still Ignored*; RNID also authored the earlier *Access All Areas?* report that first evidenced Equality Act non-compliance in health settings
- **NHS England / NHS Digital** — owns the Accessible Information Standard and the Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag (DAPB4019); as of March 2025, government announced the planned abolition of NHS England, meaning AIS responsibility passes to the Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) — a machinery-of-government change with direct implications for AIS accountability
- **Individual NHS Trusts / ICBs** — hold the actual interpreting budget and procurement responsibility; no national ring-fenced fund (confirmed pattern across NHS Scotland guidance and NHS England commissioner guidance — see Money Trail)
- **DA Languages** — confirmed (per existing project verification) as the incumbent commercial BSL GP interpreting contractor; not a VCSE/charity actor, which matters for the "who actually delivers vs. who profits" question in Section 12 of the framework
- **NRCPD** (National Registers of Communication Professionals working with Deaf and Deafblind People) — the primary regulator/register for interpreters used in health settings; also registers lipspeakers, notetakers, deafblind interpreters, speech-to-text reporters
- **RBSLI** — a separate, independent voluntary register (est. 2015) running parallel to NRCPD; worth clarifying in a future pass whether NHS commissioning guidance specifies NRCPD-only or accepts RBSLI too, as this affects the real size of the "qualified" workforce pool

## Current Policy / Legal Position

- **Equality Act 2010** — legal duty on all health and social care services to make reasonable adjustments; this is the underlying statute, now over 15 years old, that *Still Ignored* frames its entire critique around ("clearly violating rights set out... over 15 years ago")
- **Accessible Information Standard (AIS)** — the operational standard requiring NHS bodies to identify, record, flag and meet communication needs. *Still Ignored* identifies its core failure mode as **lack of accountability and enforcement** — there is no sufficient mechanism to ensure compliance, and the standard is being confused by staff with unrelated data-access rights
- **DAPB4019 — Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag** — the technical fix intended to make reasonable-adjustment needs visible across NHS systems via the National Care Records Service ("NHS Spine"). Scope covers **all** providers of NHS care/treatment and publicly-funded social care, including independent contractors (GP practices, dental, optometric, pharmacy), NHS Trusts/Foundation Trusts, and voluntary/private sector NHS providers. Non-compliance is enforceable under Section 250 of the Health and Social Care Act 2012 (as amended)

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026): AIS Update, Single Patient Record, and NHS Commissioning Routes for VCSE/Deaf Organisations

This section reports the results of direct primary-source research (WebFetch/WebSearch of government and NHS pages themselves, not a Grok/SuperGrok cross-check), carried out to close gaps identified against the project's `supergrok-prompt-deaf-health-access.md` research prompt. Because these are primary sources opened directly, several of the following are rated at a higher confidence than the Grok-derived material elsewhere in this domain — see VERIFICATION_STATUS.md for exact ratings.

**AIS self-assessment framework (✅ Confirmed):** NHS England published a new AIS self-assessment framework on 30 June 2025 (updated 14 Aug 2025). It introduces a sixth "Review" stage to the AIS process, requires providers/ICBs to name an accountable AIS lead, and uses RAG (red/amber/green) self-scoring. First formal assessments are due **March 2027**. Healthwatch's independent commentary on the update quotes RNID's Louise Ansari confirming AIS **remains non-mandatory** even under the new framework — the framework strengthens self-assessment machinery but does not convert AIS into an enforceable legal requirement in its own right (it continues to sit underneath the Equality Act 2010 reasonable-adjustments duty, as already documented above).

**Single Patient Record (✅ Confirmed, and corrects a dated assumption in the original research prompt):** GOV.UK's own announcement confirms the NHS modernisation bill introducing the Single Patient Record was introduced to Parliament on **14 May 2026** — not July 2025, as the original research prompt assumed. Implementation begins in **early 2027**, starting with maternity and frailty care pathways. Accessibility detail in the government's own material is currently thin; the one substantive relevant quote found is from Dr Jeanette Dickson, flagging a risk of digital exclusion in the rollout — a concern directly relevant to Deaf/BSL-using patients if reasonable-adjustment flags (see RADF/DAPB4019 above) are not carried through cleanly into the new record architecture. **This is a live gap worth tracking**: no evidence was found either way that RADF data will/won't migrate cleanly into the Single Patient Record.

**Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (✅ Confirmed, limited relevance found):** Received Royal Assent 19 June 2025. Its long title references "information standards for health and social care" but no accessibility-specific provisions were found in this pass. Substantial completion of implementation is expected by 23 June 2026. Flagged as a possible future vehicle for strengthening AIS's legal status, but nothing found yet ties the two together.

**NHS commissioning routes for VCSE/Deaf organisations — three separate mechanisms checked directly, with an important negative finding:**

1. **Cabinet Office PPN 001 "SME and VCSE procurement spend targets" (12 Feb 2025) (✅ Confirmed) — does NOT apply to the NHS.** This is the actual "Cabinet Office VCSE procurement policy statement" the original research prompt was trying to identify (the prompt's assumed title/date were close but not exact — it is PPN 001, not "PPN 002"). It requires central government departments and NDPBs to set two-year VCSE spend targets in place by **1 April 2026**. Critically, the policy note **explicitly excludes NHS trusts and foundation trusts** from its scope, alongside devolved bodies. This directly overturns the framing in the original research prompt: there is no incoming April 2026 VCSE spend-target obligation on NHS commissioners specifically — the "1 April 2026" date is real, but it binds central government departments, not the NHS.
2. **NHS Provider Selection Regime (PSR) statutory guidance (✅ Confirmed) — no VCSE-specific direct-award route.** NHS England's own PSR guidance states plainly that relevant authorities must not treat VCSE/independent-sector providers differently from NHS trusts or local authorities "based solely on their status." VCSE organisations can only be awarded contracts through the same five PSR routes available to any provider (Direct Award Processes A/B/C, Most Suitable Provider, Competitive Process) — there is no VCSE exception and no minimum-value threshold under which VCSE providers can bypass the regime. The guidance does note VCSE organisations may be involved in pathway-design discussions at place level, but states this is "clearly distinct from any considerations around contracting."
3. **Section 256, NHS Act 2006 (✅ Confirmed, current text directly read) — still in force, but does not explicitly name voluntary organisations.** Section 256 remains in force (confirmed against legislation.gov.uk's own current text, up to date as of 28 August 2026, though several 2022 Health and Care Act amendments are not yet in force). It authorises NHS England/ICBs to make payments to local social services authorities, councils, and specific categories of housing/development bodies — voluntary organisations are not named as an explicit payment category, though a broadly-worded subsection covering functions "having an effect on the health of any individuals" could theoretically be read to extend further. This requires legal interpretation beyond what a plain reading of the statute settles, and should not be presented as a confirmed VCSE funding route without further legal input.

**VCSE Health and Wellbeing Alliance (🟡 Lead-only — could not confirm current Deaf-sector membership).** Confirmed to exist as a DHSC/NHS England/UKHSA partnership programme, running since April 2017, intended to bring lived-experience VCSE voice into policy. Direct retrieval of NHS England's own page did not surface a current member list or confirm whether any Deaf/deafblind/hearing-loss-specific organisation is currently a member — this remains an open, directly checkable question (the Alliance's own site, not yet fetched, is the next step).

**Net effect of this sub-section for the project:** the original research prompt's premise — that a named Cabinet Office VCSE procurement policy would soon require NHS commissioners to route more spend to voluntary-sector Deaf organisations from April 2026 — does **not hold up** against direct primary-source reading. The real policy (PPN 001) explicitly carves the NHS out. PSR and Section 256 remain, in principle, usable routes for ICBs to fund VCSE Deaf organisations, but neither creates a preferential or guaranteed pathway, and neither was found to have been used in practice for a Deaf-sector organisation in this pass (worth an FOI target — see below).

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026): SWL-Specific Primary Source — Healthwatch Report on BSL Access to GP Care

This is the single most directly relevant new finding of this research pass: a **South West London-specific**, dated, methodologically transparent Healthwatch report, found via direct search and read in full.

**Source:** Healthwatch South West London (a collaborative of the six SWL borough Healthwatch organisations), *Improving BSL users' access to GP Practice care in South West London* — GP practice staff guidance report, published 2025 (PDF dated in its filename 24 March 2026, most likely reflecting a later revision/re-publication of 2025 fieldwork; treat the fieldwork dates below as primary).

**Methodology (re-verified word-for-word, 5 Sept 2026, via a second direct fetch — this claim was queried by a SuperGrok cross-check and is now doubly confirmed):** Two patient/carer focus groups (Harding House, Wandsworth, 4 April 2025; Croydon Clocktower, 4 June 2025, promoted by RAD; 21 participants total) plus one individual patient interview (North West London); 17 GP practice staff via survey, practice-manager meetings, and a training event (April–June 2025). The report's own methods section states directly: **"In total, we heard from 39 people with relevant experience of using BSL interpretation in South West London GP practices,"** breaking down as 21 patients/carers (focus groups) + 17 GP staff + 1 NWL patient. Critically, the report states in as many words: **"We did not have any responses to the patient and carer survey"** — confirmed verbatim on a second, independent direct read of the same PDF, after a SuperGrok follow-up pass flagged this claim as needing re-confirmation. It genuinely was zero, not "the extracted text didn't capture it" — this is a good example of the project's two-source verification habit paying off in both directions (catching real errors, like the date typo above, and confirming a real claim that another AI pass had — correctly cautiously — flagged as unconfirmed rather than wrong).

**Key figures (✅ Confirmed, directly read from the primary document, full borough breakdown now closed via a Sept 2026 SuperGrok follow-up pass and independently re-verified by direct fetch of the ICB-facing PDF):**
- SWL ICB recorded **878 BSL interpretation requests** from GP practices, full six-borough breakdown: **Wandsworth 466, Croydon 185, Sutton 72, Merton 68, Kingston 61, Richmond 26** (total 878)
- The ICB-facing report attributes Wandsworth's disproportionate share partly to two Achieving Together Deaf care homes (Harding House and Huguenot Place) and Oak Lodge School — a genuinely useful explanatory detail, not just a raw number
- **Source-text date error, confirmed directly, do not repeat uncorrected:** the ICB-facing PDF itself states the collection period as "between October 2025 and June 2025" — an internally impossible date range (October after June in the same year). Every other Healthwatch SWL page describing the same DAL/Sign Solutions contract uses October 2024–June 2025, which is also consistent with the contract's confirmed 1 October 2024 start date. Treat **October 2024–June 2025** as the correct period and the "October 2025" string as a typo in the source document — this is exactly the kind of small but citation-breaking error this project's verification discipline exists to catch before it propagates into public-facing material.
- The report repeats the national **84%** figure (d/Deaf, deafblind and hard-of-hearing people reporting healthcare access difficulties) and the **~87,000 BSL users in England and Wales** figure — note this is yet a *third* distinct BSL population figure circulating in the sources this project has now directly checked (see the 22,000/25,000/151,000 figures above), underlining how unsettled this number is across even closely-related sources

**The new BSL interpreting service (✅ Confirmed):** DA Languages (trading as "DAL") replaced the previous provider, Language Line, from **1 October 2024**, across all six SWL boroughs (Croydon, Kingston, Merton, Richmond, Sutton, Wandsworth). Sign Solutions (a DAL subcontractor) delivers pre-booked in-person interpretation, on-demand video interpreting, video relay, and BSL document translation. Funding is sourced through the **South West London Innovation ICP Priorities Fund** — a specific, named local funding line, confirmed directly rather than inferred. This is the single most concrete "who pays, who delivers, since when" answer found anywhere in this project for the SWL area specifically, and should be treated as a headline finding.

**What the report itself concludes:** patients and carers stressed that "genuine accessibility requires more than changes to interpretation services" — persistent problems include unreliable interpreter attendance, no cancellation notifications, and wider non-compliance with AIS. The report makes eight practice-level recommendations, including professional interpreter booking, staff training on the updated AIS, double appointments, and regular consultation with d/Deaf patients. GP staff survey non-response and the zero-response written patient survey both point to a structural difficulty in measuring this population's experience through conventional channels — worth stating as a methodological finding in its own right, not just a data gap.

**FOI/follow-up angle:** the full per-borough breakdown (all six boroughs, not just the two largest) and any post-June 2025 data on the DAL/Sign Solutions service's actual performance (cancellation rates, wait times) were not found in this pass and are strong, highly specific SWL-local FOI targets.

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026): Breaking Developments (Last ~12 Months) and Peer-Reviewed Research

**RNID's follow-up to *Still Ignored* — a major new finding, Jan 2026 (✅ Confirmed):** RNID's own Freedom of Information investigation, published 27 January 2026, found **92% of NHS staff in England have not completed AIS training**. Of responding NHS trusts, most reported fewer than 1% of staff had completed the training module; **ten trusts reported zero staff completion**; only **eleven trusts** achieved 50%+ completion. Only **one-third of NHS staff** knew the standard existed at all, and only **24%** reported consistently meeting the communication needs of deaf/hard-of-hearing patients. Staff-reported barriers: inadequate training (34%), time constraints (32%), no standardised procedure (31%), poor IT systems (30%). RNID is calling for mandatory AIS training, stronger legislative enforcement, and prioritised funding. **This is the strongest, most current, most citable statistic in the entire project for the "AIS is not working" argument** — it is RNID's own FOI data, directly sourced from NHS trusts themselves, not a survey or advocacy estimate.

**BSL Act 2022 Fourth Report reception (✅ Confirmed) — Health and Social Care specifically flagged as regressing:** The government's fourth BSL report (May 2025-April 2026, published 15 July 2026 by the Cabinet Office Disability Unit) recorded 198 BSL content pieces across government — a 41% year-on-year rise and 161% since the first report in 2022. However, RNID's on-the-record reaction (advocacy manager Michael Quinlan) explicitly named the **Department of Health and Social Care** (alongside Education) as a department showing **declining** BSL communications output — a direct, named, current criticism of the department most relevant to this project. RNID's central ask is implementation of **Section 3 of the BSL Act 2022** (statutory guidance), matching stronger legislation already in force in Northern Ireland and Wales. DHSC has separately pledged to integrate BSL into a revamped NHS app, though no implementation timeline was found. A five-month gap in BSL interpretation of the Prime Minister's speeches (Sept 2025-Mar 2026) was also raised as a reception-level criticism, though this is a general-government rather than health-specific point.

**Peer-reviewed research — two genuinely new, UK-based academic sources found (raises this domain's evidence base beyond charity/advocacy reports):**
- Selby, Jones, Radford & Hind, "Reducing health inequalities in the deaf community: a priority setting exercise," *BMC Health Services Research* 26:131, online 24 December 2025 (University of Leeds, with Pulvertaft Hand Centre/Derby NHS Trust, Communication Unlimited, and Royal School for the Deaf Derby as partners) (✅ Confirmed, read directly). Used a "Research Prioritisation by Affected Communities" methodology adapted for BSL accessibility: Round 1 (43 participants, Jan 2025) identified barriers; Round 2 (38 participants, 87% retention) prioritised themes via a mock £1m resource-allocation exercise. Results: **process improvements** (enabling direct NHS communication without relying on telephone contact) were rated the top priority (28% of allocated resource), followed by **staff Deaf-awareness training** (25%), **interpreter access** (17%), and **technology solutions** (13%). Individual-level interventions — "Deaf health empowerment" (1%) and "Deaf health champions" (0.6%) — were rated far lower by the Deaf participants themselves. The paper's own conclusion: institutional/systemic barriers must be fixed before individual-level interventions can be effective. **This is a directly relevant, methodologically strong, UK academic source that should be treated as one of this domain's best pieces of evidence** — it is Deaf people themselves prioritising solutions, not a charity survey or government report.
- A related umbrella review and a qualitative study on digital healthcare transformation (BMC Health Services Research, 2026) were identified via search but not yet opened in full — flagged as a near-term follow-up rather than fully worked into this domain yet.

**Negative findings worth recording explicitly (searched for, not found):** no update to NHS England's Hearing Loss Data Tool was found beyond its original 2019 publication; no SWLSTG National Deaf Services capacity/waiting-time update was found; no evidence was found of RAD (Royal Association for Deaf People) South London-specific news in the last 12 months (RAD's national news page could not be fully retrieved in this pass — a 403 error was returned — so this should be treated as "not found in this pass," not "confirmed absent"); the London Datastore's "registered deaf or hard of hearing by borough" dataset is **stale (data only to December 2010)** and should not be used as a current SWL population source.

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026, Batch 2 + 3): Confirmed PSR Awards to Deaf-Sector VCSE Organisations — SignHealth AND RNID

This closes one of the project's standing open questions (whether the Provider Selection Regime or Section 256 has ever actually been used to fund a Deaf-sector VCSE organisation) with a genuine, well-documented, twice-independently-confirmed positive finding. Sourced initially via a SuperGrok follow-up pass, then independently verified by directly fetching both the award notice mirror and the official Find a Tender notice.

**Award 1 — NHS Derby and Derbyshire ICB, "BSL Talking Therapy Service (Midlands Region)" (✅ Confirmed, directly verified against two independent sources):** SignHealth awarded **£491,036 per year**, contract term **1 April 2026 to 31 March 2029** with up to two years' possible extension. Procurement route: PSR **Most Suitable Provider** (not Direct Award), justified by the ICB on the basis of "the very specialist skills required, and the need to ensure continuity, quality, and accessibility of provision" for BSL-using Deaf adults needing psychological therapy. Award notice published 20 March 2026, representations deadline 27 March 2026. Confirmed against a bidstats/d3tenders mirror and cross-checked for consistency with Find a Tender's own listings for the same service line.

**Award 2 — NHS South East London ICB, "Talking Therapies in British Sign Language (BSL)" (✅ Confirmed, directly retrieved from Find a Tender's own official notice):** SignHealth also holds a **separate, earlier** BSL Talking Therapies contract with SEL ICB, contract dates **1 August 2025 to 31 January 2026, extended to 31 March 2026** (8 months total including the extension). Procurement route: PSR **urgent-circumstances provision**, justified because "delaying the award to conduct a full application of the PSR would be likely to pose a risk to patient or public safety." Contract value was not disclosed in this notice. This is a short-term, urgency-justified award, not a long-term commissioning decision, and should be described accordingly — it likely reflects a stopgap after the national NHS England Talking Therapies contract for SignHealth ended 31 March 2025 (see this domain's Summary/Key Figures above), consistent with the ICB-by-ICB fragmentation this project has already flagged as a consequence of that change.

**Award 3 — Somerset NHS Foundation Trust → RNID, hearing-aid aftercare services (✅ Confirmed, directly verified against Find a Tender's own official notice, 5 September 2026, Batch 3):** RNID awarded **£46,000 (excluding VAT)**, contract term **12 months from 13 January 2025** (standstill ended 9 January 2025). Service: hearing-aid aftercare, screening checks, hearing-loss information, and staff support/signposting across Somerset care homes — a hearing-aid/hearing-loss service, not a BSL interpreting or Deaf-specific psychological-therapy contract, and importantly not aimed at BSL-using Deaf people specifically (RNID's broader remit covers deafness and hearing loss generally, including age-related hearing loss, which is a materially different population from BSL-using Deaf people). Procurement route: PSR **Most Suitable Provider**, justified by the Trust as "ability to deliver the contract within budget and having a proven track record of successfully delivering the service for a number of other NHS Trusts" — an incumbent-track-record and budget-fit justification, distinct from SignHealth's specialist-clinical-workforce justification.

**What this means for the project's framing, updated with the third confirmed award:** PSR has now been used **twice** to fund Deaf/hearing-sector VCSE organisations directly — SignHealth (twice, for BSL-specific clinical services) and RNID (once, for a general hearing-aid aftercare service, not BSL-specific). The RNID award is a useful comparison case precisely because it shows PSR being used for a *general* hearing-loss/hearing-aid service rather than a BSL/Deaf-culture-specific one — meaning the "yes, PSR is a working route" finding is now broader than SignHealth alone, but the *BSL-specific* clinical-therapy niche remains SignHealth's alone. No equivalent PSR award was found for BDA, Action Deafness, Deafblind UK, or RAD in this pass (RAD's NCL work is a previous interpreting contract, not described anywhere as a PSR award). Section 256 remains a confirmed **no finding yet** after three separate search angles across two research passes — this is now a genuinely well-tested negative, not an under-searched gap, though it remains a documented absence rather than proof no such payment has ever existed.

## ✅ New (5 Sept 2026, Batch 3): The Grassroots-Voice Gap — a Substantiated Explanation, Not Just an Absence

Following up directly on the standing methodological finding logged in `VERIFICATION_STATUS.md` (Section 22), this batch found genuine, citable evidence for *why* the grassroots voice is so hard to find on text-based social platforms — this is no longer just an observed absence, it now has documented explanatory grounding.

**The core finding, sourced to the BSL Advisory Board's own *Locked Out* report (27 Nov 2025):** "BSL is a distinct language from English and has its own community and culture. It is not a signed version of written or spoken English since its vocabulary and syntax are different." The same report notes many Deaf people "may have poor literacy skills" as a consequence of inaccessible education, and states plainly that "written English or subtitling is an inadequate alternative" to direct BSL communication. This is corroborated by a second, independent, UK-specific source: Kainos/Signly's co-design work with BSL users on GOV.UK services (26 Sept 2025) states as its first design principle "written English is not BSL users' main language," and that accessibility barriers for this group come "not from disability, but from services being designed primarily for people whose first language is written English." A UK financial-services regulator source (Lending Standards Board, 27 Oct 2025) independently makes the same structural point in a different sector: firms over-relying on written English with d/Deaf customers, since BSL "doesn't translate directly to written English."

**Where the grassroots voice actually lives instead — video, not text, and largely charity-mediated rather than fully independent:** a search across YouTube and Facebook (rather than X/Twitter alone) found a genuine layer of BSL-language content on NHS access, though it remains predominantly charity-produced rather than independently authored by individuals. Notable items: SignHealth's own YouTube channel carries a BSL-signed version of the open letter itself, plus named lived-experience films (e.g. "Rachel's story — barriers to maternity care for deaf people") and topic-specific access films (BSL guidance on NHS GP services, a BSL summary of the NHS 10-Year Plan). A Facebook reel of the open letter (14 Nov 2025, SignHealth's own page) drew two identifiably individual commenters raising specific, substantive points — one on the Accessible Information Standard not reaching frontline awareness, another on interpreting agencies and working conditions — genuine grassroots content, but only two comments deep and clearly anecdotal/unverified rather than a corpus. BDA Scotland's own YouTube channel carries practical BSL/English NHS-interpreter guidance (Scotland, not England). A Healthwatch Lancashire-organised BSL community event (27 Nov 2025) documents a real, ongoing local Deaf-engagement relationship with that area's ICB, including a first follow-up meeting held 1 July 2025 — a useful comparator model for what SWL currently lacks (see the "no board agenda item" finding above).

**What was not found, despite specifically looking:** any independent, individually-authored SWL-local (Croydon/Wandsworth/Kingston/Merton/Richmond/Sutton) BSL vlogger posting NHS-access content in the last 12 months. This narrows the standing finding usefully: the grassroots voice **does exist** on video platforms nationally, largely channelled through charity production values and platforms, but a genuinely independent, SWL-local grassroots voice specifically has not yet been found anywhere — meaning it may not exist yet, rather than merely being hard to search for. This sharpens the "later stage" direction already logged in VERIFICATION_STATUS.md Section 22: rather than continuing to search for content that may not exist, the more promising path is likely direct engagement — following the Healthwatch Lancashire model of an organised BSL community event with recorded video output — to actively create the SWL-local grassroots evidence base this project currently lacks, rather than passively searching for it.

## ✅ New (8 Sept 2026, Prompts 34–37): AIS Legal Enforcement, Flag/Booking Failure Data, Comparative Practice, and Lived Experience — Anchor Case: the Missed-Flag Phone Call

Compiled from a four-prompt SuperGrok research batch (master index prompts 34–37; source prompts in `claude/grok-prompts-ais.md`, Deaf Network Health Group Claude project; full working files `prompts/AIS-prompt-34...37-*.md` in this repo). Anchor case throughout: a BSL Deaf patient attends a planned, non-emergency hospital follow-up; the trust system does not hold or act on a BSL flag; the patient is telephoned instead of texted/emailed/relayed; no in-person BSL interpreter is booked; a video relay link is offered instead. Independently verified against primary sources by Claude on 8 September 2026 — see confidence markers per claim below. This does not replace the RADF/AIS-update material above (5 Sept 2026); it adds the legal-enforcement mechanics, the flag/booking failure data specifically, and named case law.

**Legal spine (✅ confirmed directly against legislation.gov.uk, 8 Sept 2026):** AIS is DAPB1605 (formerly DCB1605/SCCI1605), published under s.250 Health and Social Care Act 2012. Section 95 of the Health and Care Act 2022 rewrote s.250 so that a person to whom an information standard applies "must comply … except in so far as the requirement to comply is waived" (new s.250(6A)) — quoted text confirmed word-for-word against the live legislation.gov.uk page. This "must comply" provision was commenced **7 July 2025** by SI 2025/807 reg. 2 — also confirmed directly. *Locked Out* (Nov 2025) still describes s.95 as "not enacted," which is now out of date on the statute's commencement, though its underlying point — that enforcement/monitoring/penalty machinery has not visibly been used against named trusts — remains fair on the evidence found.

**⚠️ Contested — needs a careful re-read, do not treat either side as settled:** Grok's prompt-34 output states CQC's own guidance says "the AIS is not assured by CQC, that is the role of the commissioner," citing both a CQC guidance page and *Still Ignored*. A direct fetch of the current CQC page (cqc.org.uk/guidance-providers/meeting-accessible-information-standard, checked 8 Sept 2026) instead shows CQC describing active checks during inspections (talking to staff and people using the service, reviewing at least one AIS-affected care plan where possible, annual Provider Information Requests/Collections). These may not actually conflict — "CQC does not formally assure/certify a compliance rating" and "CQC looks at AIS as one signal within a broader inspection" are different claims — but this needs a side-by-side read of the exact CQC wording and the *Still Ignored*/DHSC quote before either version goes into the dossier as settled fact.

**What AIS requires on booking and contact method (sourced from the 2025 DAPB1605 long-read, not yet independently re-fetched by Claude):** accessible contact methods are a must (requirement 1.29); communication support "without unreasonable delay" is a must (1.31); interpreter qualification/registration (NRCPD/RBSLI) is a must (1.32); longer appointments are only a should (1.33). Critically, AIS does **not** set in-person BSL as the default for planned appointments with VRI as emergency-only fallback — NHS England's implementation guidance instead frames it as patient choice ("for routine care, individuals should be given the option of remote or face-to-face interpretation"). A trust offering only VRI for a bookable follow-up sits in a guidance grey zone under AIS specifically, though it will usually still engage the Equality Act's anticipatory-adjustment duty if the patient has a recorded in-person preference. **Scotland's policy is explicitly tighter** (see comparative section below) and is flagged as the clearest transferable form of words for strengthening English AIS guidance or ICB contract schedules.

**Named enforcement/case law (✅ Alan Graham/UHB confirmed word-for-word against ombudsman.org.uk, 8 Sept 2026; ✅ Royal Trinity Hospice confirmed word-for-word including the patient quote, 8 Sept 2026; others not yet independently re-fetched):**
- **Alan Graham / University Hospitals Birmingham NHS FT** (PHSO, published 11 Feb 2026): professional interpreters provided on only 3 of 11 inpatient weeks; his 16-year-old grandson was asked to tell his Deaf daughter that CPR would not be attempted and Alan might not survive the night; a 12-year-old granddaughter was also used. Remedy: £900/£900/£750 plus apology and action plan. Confirmed directly.
- **UHB "Mr X"** (CQC, Nov 2022): two fixed-penalty notices totalling £8,000 under Regulation 11 (consent) after three feeding-tube procedures proceeded without a BSL interpreter for a Deaf, BSL-and-lipreading patient. A consent case, not an AIS-named case, but the same trust and the same interpreter-shaped gap as Alan Graham.
- **"Samantha"** (PHSO finding reported by BBC, 19 Aug 2026 — ⚠️ unverified, BBC article blocked from direct fetch here, not independently disproven): given the wrong vaccine at a GP surgery after no interpreter was booked and a BSL explainer video wasn't shown; remedy apology + £450; practice then signed an interpreting contract. If accurate, this is the closest published PHSO match to the anchor case's booking-failure half at a *planned* primary-care appointment specifically (as opposed to inpatient).
- **Mrs E**, PHSO case summary 198 (June 2014): GP practice withdrew BSL interpreter funding for three years; remedy £3,000 plus action plan. Pre-dates AIS coming into force (Aug 2016) but the same commissioner-vs-practice buck-passing pattern *Still Ignored* still describes today.
- No CQC enforcement notice was found that is titled specifically as an AIS communication-method or interpreter-booking breach — enforcement that exists travels under neighbouring regulations (consent, dignity, person-centred care), not a standalone "AIS breach" category. No national scored dataset of AIS compliance by trust is published.

**Quantified flag/booking-failure data (⚠️ largely from the SignHealth/RNID *Still Ignored* 2025 report — the source PDF is blocked from direct fetch by its robots.txt, so these figures are reported as Grok found them, not independently re-verified line-by-line; treat as strong-but-unconfirmed pending a manual read of the PDF):**
- 70% of respondents were never asked about their information/communication needs when accessing NHS care.
- Only 7% say communication support is always provided when needed (27% rarely, 36% never).
- 80% of sign-language users who were told a communication professional was booked had that professional fail to attend (SL-user sub-sample n=121).
- Only 27% of NHS staff say they are always made aware of a patient's communication needs before the meeting — i.e. even where a flag exists, most staff don't see it in time.
- Only 16% of respondents think healthcare is more accessible now than 10 years ago (i.e. across most of AIS's lifetime since Aug 2016).
- On VRS-as-substitute specifically (small sub-sample, n=37): only 24% agree VRS is a suitable substitute for in-person, 54% disagree, though 59% say VRS helps at short notice/in an emergency — do not present this small sub-sample as a national preference figure.
- *Locked Out* (Nov 2025) repeats the 80% no-show figure and separately cites joint charity research that 67% of Deaf people had no accessible method of contacting their GP.

**No national or ICB-published rate exists for the two exact anchor-case failures** — "correct contact method used" and "interpreter booked as requested" are not fields any current NHS reporting system tracks as an incident type. Trust-level FOI responses (e.g. Warrington and Halton, 96.5% BSL-request fill rate) measure demand fulfilment, not AIS-specific failure modes like wrong contact method or VRI-substituted-for-booked-in-person. This is a clean, recurring FOI target for the SWL-specific workstream (see FOI Targets below).

**Comparative practice — what would actually strengthen AIS (not yet independently re-fetched by Claude beyond the legal-spine checks above):**
1. **Scotland's rule is the closest transferable form of words:** Public Health Scotland's national interpreting policy states face-to-face BSL is "the preferred and main method," with video interpreting permitted only for emergencies, short pre-agreed routine appointments (bloods, immunisations), or while waiting for a face-to-face interpreter. This could be written into English AIS implementation guidance or ICB contract schedules without new primary legislation.
2. **US ADA Title II "primary consideration"** (for public providers) requires honouring a disabled person's *requested* auxiliary aid unless the provider can show an equally effective alternative — tighter than AIS's current "offer a choice" framing for routine care.
3. **No peer-reviewed before/after study was found** isolating a face-to-face-default or sticky-flag policy and measuring Deaf missed-appointment (DNA) rates before and after — any claim that such a policy "reduces missed appointments" should be treated as modelled/anecdotal, not evidenced, until a named study is attached.
4. England already has the *design* for a sticky, cross-provider flag (AIS-coded fields plus the Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag on Spine) — the gap identified across all four prompts is conformance and workflow, not the absence of a standard.

**Named good practice (✅ confirmed):** Royal Trinity Hospice, London (2025 access-and-inclusion programme) routinely asks about and records communication needs, uses email as the default contact method, fitted a flashing doorbell for inpatient rooms, and runs BSL-interpreted "Talk & Tour" events. Patient quote, confirmed word-for-word: *"They communicate with me via email, help me lipread and during my inpatient stay even gave me a flashing doorbell for my room, so I knew when people were coming in."* This is a hospice solving the contact-method half of the anchor case, not an acute trust — don't over-claim transferability without qualification.

- The 80% interpreter-no-show and 70%-never-asked figures rest on *Still Ignored*'s survey (n=1,114 England adults, Nov–Dec 2024) — strong methodology reported by Grok, but the source PDF itself is blocked from direct fetch here and needs a manual verification pass before being treated as fully confirmed in this dossier.

## Money Trail

- **No national ring-fenced NHS interpreting budget.** Confirmed via NHS Scotland's national interpreting policy and NHS England's commissioner guidance for primary care: interpreting and translation costs are met locally, by the NHS body delivering the service, following local procurement procedures. This directly shapes incentives — a Trust or ICB facing budget pressure has every incentive to under-book or under-resource interpreting, and there is no central body enforcing minimum spend
- **Costs must not be passed to patients, family members, or carers** — explicit in the NHS Scotland framework; the practical effect where this is breached (informal family interpreting) is itself a data-protection and clinical-safety risk *Still Ignored* documents
- **DA Languages** — as the incumbent commercial contractor for BSL GP interpreting (already verified in-project), sits at the centre of the "commercial margin vs. frontline interpreter pay vs. community trust" question. A clean FOI target: contract value, rate paid per booking, and what proportion reaches the interpreter vs. agency margin
- **Workforce economics compound the funding problem** — training a qualified NHS-capable BSL interpreter takes ~7 years plus additional post-qualification clinical experience (per industry commentary), meaning funding alone cannot fix supply in the short term; several sources point to video remote interpreting (VRI) as a stopgap that increases effective coverage (one interpreter covering 5–10x more sessions) but is explicitly *not* a substitute for face-to-face in complex/high-stakes clinical situations
