## Prompt 1 — GDPR and data protection as a barrier to VRS/interpreter-relayed access

```
I'm researching UK data protection law (UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018) as a barrier to Deaf British Sign Language (BSL) users accessing services via Video Relay Service (VRS) or a live BSL interpreter on a phone/video call. I need primary-sourced, dated findings, not general commentary.

Context: In the UK, a Deaf BSL user who wants to handle a banking, DVLA, HMRC, insurance, or NHS matter by phone typically needs a third party present on the call — either a professional BSL interpreter (in person or via a VRS platform like SignVideo, InterpretersLive!, or similar) or, in some cases, a video relay operator. I want to know whether UK GDPR / DPA 2018 rules, or organisations' own interpretation of them, create a genuine barrier to this, separate from the more commonly cited "fraud prevention" or "identity verification" justification.

Please research and report back on each of the following, with a direct source (organisation name, document title, URL, and publication/retrieval date) for every claim:

1. Does UK GDPR or the Data Protection Act 2018 contain any provision that a bank, DVLA, HMRC, insurer, or NHS body could point to as a legal basis for refusing to proceed with a call where a third-party interpreter is relaying/present? (E.g., special category data under Article 9, the "necessary for compliance with a legal obligation" basis, or a data minimisation argument.) Quote the actual statutory text if you find it, don't just assert it exists.

2. Have the ICO (Information Commissioner's Office), the FCA, or any UK financial/telecoms regulator published guidance specifically addressing whether an interpreter or VRS operator counts as a "third party" for GDPR purposes when relaying a Deaf customer's call, and whether consent from the Deaf person is sufficient to authorise their presence on the call?

3. Search for any published complaint, ombudsman decision, tribunal case, or news report where a UK organisation cited GDPR / data protection specifically (not just "security" or "fraud" generally) as the reason for declining to proceed with an interpreter-relayed or VRS-relayed call. I already know of one Irish case (Kalinova v. Permanent TSB, 2022, Workplace Relations Commission, cited "cannot do business through a third party" — but that case does not appear to invoke GDPR/data protection specifically, only general "security" and "regulatory requirements" language). I need to know if there is a UK-specific case that DOES invoke data protection law by name.

4. Search RNID, SignHealth, the British Deaf Association (BDA), and the BSL Advisory Board's "Locked Out" report (27 Nov 2025, gov.uk) for any mention of GDPR or data protection as a named barrier to BSL/VRS access — as opposed to the more commonly documented barriers (funding, interpreter shortage, awareness).

5. Is there any published guidance (from the ICO, a professional interpreting body like NRCPD, or a Deaf-sector charity) on how a Deaf person should be able to give valid consent for an interpreter to be present on a data-sensitive call, and whether current practice (verbal consent given via the interpreter itself) creates a circular consent problem worth flagging?

6. Search for any equivalent EU country experience (since UK GDPR closely tracks EU GDPR) where a data protection authority or court has ruled on this exact question — an interpreter or relay operator's presence on a call involving personal or special category data.

For every finding, state clearly whether it is: (a) a primary source you opened yourself and can quote directly, (b) a secondary report describing a primary source you have not opened, or (c) something you could not find evidence for after searching. Do not present (b) or (c) as if they were (a).
```

## Prompt 4 — The exact enabling legislation for the UK's relay universal-service obligation

```
UK telecoms regulatory research question. I need to identify the precise legal mechanism that gives Ofcom the power to mandate "Relay UK" (text relay) as a universal service for disabled telecoms users, and to establish exactly why video relay service (VRS) has never been given the same statutory footing.

I've already ruled out Communications Act 2003, Section 151 — I checked it directly on legislation.gov.uk and it is a purely definitional section (defines terms like "electronic communications network," not a disability-access duty).

What I know so far: Ofcom's General Conditions of Entitlement include "General Condition 15" (GC15) and reference "Universal Service Condition 4" (USC4, applying to BT specifically) as the operative instruments requiring text relay provision, which Ofcom describes as delivering "equivalence of access for disabled end-users as required by the Universal Service Directive."

Please research and report:

1. What specific section(s) of the Communications Act 2003 (or its subsequent amendments, e.g., via the Digital Economy Act 2017 or any statutory instrument) empower Ofcom to set General Conditions or Universal Service Conditions relating to disabled end-user access? I want the actual enabling primary legislation clause, not just "Ofcom's General Conditions," since those are secondary/regulatory instruments made under some primary Act power.

2. Trace the EU Universal Service Directive (2002/22/EC, as amended, and its UK-retained/onshored form post-Brexit) provisions on disabled-user telecoms access — which article(s) require member states to ensure "equivalent access," and does the Directive's text distinguish between text relay and video relay, or is it technology-neutral (meaning the UK's choice to only mandate text relay is a domestic policy choice, not something the Directive itself limited)?

3. Find Ofcom's original 2011 "Review of Relay Services" consultation and any subsequent statement/decision documents — what was Ofcom's stated legal reasoning for choosing to mandate text relay via GC15/USC4 but not extend an equivalent universal service condition to video relay? Was this a legal limitation (the Directive/Act doesn't allow it) or a policy choice (Ofcom decided against it on cost/practicality grounds)? Quote the actual reasoning if you can access the consultation or statement documents.

4. Has any UK parliamentary committee, Law Commission review, or academic legal commentary specifically criticised or examined this gap — i.e., argued that Ofcom already has the legal power to mandate universal VRS and is choosing not to use it, versus arguing that new primary legislation would be required?

5. Since the BSL Advisory Board's "Locked Out" report (gov.uk, 27 Nov 2025) recommends a national 24/7 VRS, does the report itself state what legal/regulatory mechanism would be needed to implement that recommendation — new primary legislation, an Ofcom General Condition, or something else? Quote the report's own language on this if it addresses it.

Cite the actual legislation.gov.uk section numbers, Ofcom document titles/dates, and any parliamentary or academic source directly. Flag anything you cannot verify directly.
```
