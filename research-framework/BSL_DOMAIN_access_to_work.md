# Domain 5: Access to Work (DWP)

**Last verified:** 8 September 2026
**Re-check cadence:** Quarterly, or on any DWP backlog/funding-cap announcement
**Confidence level:** Medium-High (core AtW/NAO/Rimmer figures independently confirmed 8 Sep 2026 by opening the primary sources directly — see Verification note at the end of this file. Employer-survey and business-case figures remain Lead-only per the previous pass.)

---

## Summary

Access to Work (AtW) is the UK-wide DWP scheme that funds workplace support — including BSL interpreters — for disabled employees and jobseekers. It is currently defined by three linked failures: chronic processing delays (official average now over four times the 25-working-day target), a large and growing backlog, and an annual funding cap that has been frozen since April 2024 even as interpreter fees rise. Deaf and hard-of-hearing claimants are the single largest primary-condition category by spend (28% of provision spend, £90m in 2024-25), making AtW's dysfunction a direct, quantifiable Deaf-community issue rather than a generic disability-scheme complaint. DWP announced a staffing surge in May 2026 aiming to clear the backlog by September 2027; that outcome is not yet independently audited. A separate but equally load-bearing finding, added in a later pass: only 21% of GB employers had heard of Access to Work in 2024 (down from 26% in 2022), and no employer survey exists that isolates awareness of Equality Act duties specifically toward Deaf/BSL staff — meaning the scheme's dysfunction is compounded by most employers not knowing it exists in the first place. A third finding completes the employer-facing case: because Access to Work pays up to 100% of approved BSL interpreter costs, the employer's real financial exposure is not the interpreter invoice but the uncapped cost of getting reasonable adjustments wrong — official 2023/24 figures put the median disability-discrimination tribunal award at £17,218 (mean £44,483, maximum £964,465), and one documented BSL-specific failure (*Rimmer v SSWP*, 2024) cost £49,880 plus a public judgment.

## Employer Awareness & Compliance

The scheme-side figures above (backlog, cap, processing time) describe DWP's own performance. A separate and equally important question is whether employers even know Access to Work exists, and whether they understand their own Equality Act 2010 duties toward Deaf/BSL-using staff specifically. This is directly relevant to the "Businesses & employers" audience the framework's Audience Map (Section 8) already flags as needing its own evidence.

**Safe public-facing headline:** official DWP research found that three-quarters of GB employers (76%) had not heard of Access to Work in 2024, and awareness had fallen since 2022. Among SMEs the figure is the same. Only about one in five employers know the scheme exists.

**Do not use:** any claim of the form "X% of UK employers are unaware of their Equality Act duties to Deaf staff" — no traceable employer survey asking this specific question exists. Use instead the juxtaposition below: high self-reported confidence on bundled disability law sits next to low confidence recruiting disabled people, low AtW awareness, and DWP's own case-review finding that employers had failed to make reasonable adjustments in a meaningful share of sampled AtW cases.

## Business Case: Cost of Provision vs. Cost of Inaction

The Employer Awareness section above establishes that most employers don't know Access to Work exists. This section makes the complementary case for the employers who do know but hesitate on cost grounds: **the state usually pays for the interpreter; the employer pays for getting it wrong.** Access to Work is designed to pay up to 100% of approved BSL interpreter/support-worker costs (and communication support at interview), so an employer's real cash exposure is not the interpreter invoice — it's admin time, the processing-delay gap (Domain 5's own 109-day average finding), and any hours above the frozen annual cap. Against that, disability-discrimination compensation is uncapped, and defending a claim commonly costs tens of thousands in legal fees regardless of outcome.

**Defensible one-line argument:** Access to Work pays 100% of approved BSL interpreter/support-worker costs and 100% of communication support at interview. A lost disability-discrimination claim is uncapped. Official 2023/24 MoJ figures put the median disability-discrimination award at £17,218 and the mean at £44,483 (maximum that year: £964,465). Legal fees to defend a discrimination claim commonly run £15,000–£50,000+ even before any award. One documented BSL-failure case (*Rimmer v SSWP*, 2024) cost £49,880 plus a public judgment and mandatory deaf-awareness training. This is not "interpreters cost X, tribunals always cost Y" — it is that the state will usually pick up the interpreter bill, while the employer picks up the claim.

### How to use this without over-claiming

**Safe business-case paragraph:**
> Under the Equality Act 2010 you already have a duty to make reasonable adjustments for a Deaf employee. For BSL interpreting at work and at interview, Access to Work is designed to pay 100% of the approved support-worker cost, so the cash outlay is usually not the interpreter fee. What you do pay for, if you get this wrong, is uncapped. In 2023/24 the official median disability-discrimination award was £17,218 and the average £44,483. Defending the claim typically costs tens of thousands in legal fees whether you win or lose. In *Rimmer*, a public body that treated BSL provision as "too difficult and too expensive" was ordered to pay £49,880 and to train its staff. One week of booked interpreting is a rounding error next to that.

**Do not say:**
- "The average Deaf tribunal costs £X" — there is no official Deaf-only award series.
- "Interpreters cost the employer £Y a year" — if AtW is approved, that invoice is not the employer's.
- "Reputational damage will cost £Z" — no UK study prices that for this fact pattern.
- That AtW is instant — NAO processing times mean the employer may have to bridge weeks or months. That bridging cost is the main *real* provision cost, and it is still usually cheaper than a claim.

**Caveats to keep in any public-facing use:**
- AtW delays and the frozen funding cap can leave a gap the employer has to cover temporarily — that is a provision cost, not a reason to refuse the adjustment.
- MoJ award-table figures are the minority of cases that reach a remedy hearing; most claims settle. Settlement plus legal fees plus management time is the typical "cost of inaction," and it is rarely published.
- *Rimmer* was a Jobcentre service-provision case, not a private-sector dismissal. It remains the cleanest recent UK judgment on "we didn't book BSL" — use it as an Equality Act illustration, not as an "average employer payout."

## Current Policy / Legal Position

AtW is a discretionary, demand-led DWP grant scheme, not a statutory entitlement with a fixed award formula — this distinguishes it from PIP/DLA (Domain 4). The funding cap and processing targets are administrative, not set in primary legislation, meaning both are changeable by DWP policy decision without new legislation. The core current policy fact is that the cap has been **frozen** for two consecutive years (2025-26, 2026-27) while interpreter fees are reported to have risen — a policy choice with a direct, quantifiable effect on BSL users' access to funded interpretation. No legislative change was found in this pass; all developments are administrative/operational (staffing, backlog-clearance targets).

## Known Gaps & Contested Figures

- **Funding cap: £69,260 vs £69,620.** NAO/Commons Library state £69,260; RNID's own DeafATW survey report states £69,620. Both are described as the 2025-26 figure — this needs resolving against DWP's own primary factsheet before either number is used publicly.
- **No published BSL-only share of the £227m support-worker spend line.** The NAO breaks out "deaf/HoH condition category" spend (£90m) and "support-worker" spend (£227m, which includes both interpreters and non-BSL job aides) as two different, overlapping cuts of the data — there is no single confirmed "AtW spend on BSL interpreters" figure.
- **No BDA- or NDCS-specific statistical brief on AtW** was located in this pass beyond the RNID/DeafATW survey and PAC written evidence — worth checking directly with BDA/NDCS whether one exists.
- DWP's own claimed outcomes (payment delays "eliminated," 96% of urgent cases cleared within 28 days) are self-reported in a press release, not yet independently audited by NAO/PAC — treat as a DWP claim, not a confirmed outcome, until a follow-up NAO/PAC review appears.
- **No UK representative employer survey isolates awareness of Equality Act 2010 duties specifically toward Deaf or BSL-using employees.** The closest available figures are general disability-duty confidence (88%, bundled, not Deaf-specific), Deaf-attitude polling of the public/managers (RNID), and Deaf employees' own reports of workplace treatment (demand-side, not an employer awareness measure) — none of these substitute for the missing figure, and public-facing material should never conflate them.
- **No official average cost of defending a discrimination claim exists** — only practitioner-published fee ranges (£15,000-£50,000+). No official Deaf-specific tribunal award series exists either; the median/mean/maximum MoJ figures cover all disability-discrimination claims, not BSL-specific failures. *Rimmer* is the one directly on-point documented case, but it is a single case, not a series, and a service-provision claim against a public body rather than a private-sector employer dismissal.
- **No reliable UK figure prices reputational/brand damage from a documented Deaf-accommodation failure, nor the replacement/productivity cost of losing a Deaf employee** — both are plausible real costs but currently unquantified from any source found in this pass.

## Money Trail

- £90m (2024-25) spent on deaf/HoH customers = 28% of total AtW provision spend — the single largest primary-condition category, meaning Deaf/HoH claimants are disproportionately central to this scheme's budget and its failures
- £227m support-worker spend (2024-25) has grown 125% in real terms since 2018-19 — a genuine funding increase at the scheme level, but one that coexists with a frozen per-claimant cap and record processing delays, meaning more total money is moving through a slower, more capped system
- No confirmed BSL-interpreter-only spend figure exists — this is the single most valuable number this domain lacks, and a natural FOI target
