# Domain 17: Video Relay Service & Telecoms Equity (Home / Office / Travel)

**Last verified:** 6 September 2026
**Re-check cadence:** Quarterly, or immediately if Ofcom opens a new relay-services consultation, or if the BSL Advisory Board's "national 24/7 VRS" recommendation is formally accepted or rejected by government
**Confidence level:** Medium-high on the regulatory and procurement case; low on UK-specific third-party-refusal and travel/workplace evidence (both flagged as open gaps below, not settled facts)

---

## Summary

Hearing people have an unconditional right to communicate: pick up any phone, dial any number, and reach whoever they're calling. Deaf BSL users using Video Relay Service (VRS) do not have the equivalent. UK VRS is not a universal citizen entitlement — it is a patchwork of per-organisation contracts. A Deaf person can video-call *into* an organisation that has specifically paid a VRS provider (their bank, if it opted in; the DVLA; DWP), but cannot open a video call and reach *any* UK number the way a hearing caller can from any phone. This is a direct, traceable regulatory choice: Ofcom considered and explicitly rejected a universal VRS mandate in 2011 on cost grounds, and built the current text-relay-only universal-service regime instead. The clearest proof the model is a choice, not a technical necessity, is Scotland's own Contact Scotland BSL — a genuine any-number VRS confirmed 6 Sept 2026 (operating in some form since 2015, with any-number/anyone-anytime 24/7 access confirmed from 2019) — and the short-lived SignHealth/NHS England "BSL Health Access" service (2020–21), which briefly delivered exactly the universal, non-per-organisation model this domain argues for, before funding was withdrawn and provision reverted to fragmented, organisation-by-organisation contracts. The access gap is not uniform: it manifests differently at home (data/broadband cost, and reliance on a single national emergency app), in the office (no confirmed UK evidence yet, but a live gap in the evidence base, not a confirmed non-problem), and while travelling (mobile data/roaming dependency, again evidenced only by logical inference from the technology's requirements, not by documented incident).

## Current Policy / Legal Position

There is no statutory or regulatory requirement for a universal, any-number VRS in the UK. The only enforceable universal-service obligation for disabled telecoms access covers **text relay** (Relay UK), under Universal Service Condition 4 and General Condition 15. Video relay is regulated only at the margins: Ofcom mandated emergency-only VRS (999 BSL) from 2022, but explicitly ruled out (in its 2011 consultation) a general, unrestricted VRS obligation on cost grounds. Everything else — NHS 111, DWP services, DVLA, Motability, individual banks — is a voluntary, individually procured contract between that organisation and a VRS provider, meaning access depends entirely on whether the specific organisation a Deaf person needs to reach has opted to pay for it. **Resolved 6 Sept 2026:** the enabling clause is Communications Act 2003 ss.45 and 51(2)(c) — the same power Ofcom already uses to mandate emergency VRS (GC C5.11–C5.12). Extending everyday VRS coverage would be a modification of an existing General Condition under this same statutory power, not new primary legislation — see the "Enabling Legislation — Resolved" section below for the full chain. The BSL Advisory Board's *Locked Out* report (Nov 2025) is the most significant live policy document: a government-published recommendation for a national 24/7 VRS in England and Wales, which as of this research pass has not been accepted or rejected by government — a clear, trackable advocacy target.

## GDPR & Data Protection (added 6 Sept 2026, Grok Batch 4 + independent verification)

A distinct barrier from the "third-party refusal" question above: whether UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018 give an organisation a *legal basis* to decline a call because a BSL interpreter or VRS operator is relaying it. This turned out to invert the expected finding — the UK regulator's own position is that GDPR does **not** block this, and the real, documented GDPR-related barrier is a different one: interpreting agencies and services withholding booking information from each other, not organisations refusing to speak on a relayed call.

**Core finding, independently verified:** neither UK GDPR nor the DPA 2018 contain any provision authorising an organisation to refuse to proceed with an authenticated customer because a sign language interpreter or VRS operator is present on the call. Organisations that invoke "GDPR" for this are, per the only two regulators who have addressed it directly (ICO informally, and Ireland's DPC formally), misapplying the framework — the correct analysis is: authenticate the customer, then apply the same security standard regardless of channel.

- **ICO's informal 2018 position — CONFIRMED, but citation corrected.** The substance Grok reported ("GDPR does not prevent individuals from contacting organisations using services such as textphone, NGT or even using a sign language interpreter... The matter at hand is not one of consent, but that the organisation should have appropriate security in place...") is real and independently verified — but it lives on Ofcom's **text relay guide** page (`https://www.ofcom.org.uk/phones-and-broadband/accessibility/text-relay-guide`, section "Do banks and other businesses have to accept calls via text relay?"), **not** the general accessibility FAQ page Grok initially cited. The FAQ page contains no GDPR text at all — only a bare pointer to "the Equality and Human Rights Commission and the Information Commissioner." **Use the text-relay-guide URL going forward.**

- **Irish DPC guidance, 21 May 2021 — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified on repeat fetch.** `https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/blogs/can-i-talk-account-holder-contacting-organisations-behalf-someone-else`. Case Study 2: an organisation refused a sign-language-interpreter-relayed call and cited "GDPR and data protection concerns" as the reason. Both services and interpreting agencies often do not share data that would improve accessibility and safety, despite allowances for this use of personal data within GDPR and AIS laws. For example, the individual's name, sex or gender or the clinical setting of an individual requesting a BSL interpreter are not always released... interpreters cannot make informed decisions about whether they are appropriate for the booking." (Minor wording note: verification found "The introduction of GDPR," without "the" — a trivial variance from the version first reported, not worth flagging further.) **This is the real, documented GDPR barrier in this domain: over-cautious non-sharing of booking metadata between agencies, which can cause an unsuitable interpreter to be booked (e.g. a conflict of interest in the small, interconnected BSL community) — not organisations refusing to let a Deaf person use an interpreter at all.**
- **NUBSLI/LanguageLine complaint, 18 Jun 2019 — reported, not independently re-opened this pass.** A professional interpreters' body complained that LanguageLine withheld client names from booked interpreters, "citing GDPR compliance" — consistent with, and corroborating, the *Locked Out* booking-data-sharing finding above. 🟡 Lead-only pending direct verification of nubsli.com's own statement.
- **FCA — no specific guidance found on this question.** GC19/03 (FCA guidance on vulnerable customers, July 2019) is a real, correctly-titled document, but contains no interpreter/BSL/translation-specific content in the sections checked. The FCA's own privacy page (`https://www.fca.org.uk/privacy/personal-data-used-contact-us`) does contain a real statement about using "a third-party translation service" for non-English-speaking callers that "does not record the calls or keep any information about individuals" — but this is about language translation generally, not BSL/Deaf users specifically, and should not be cited as BSL-specific FCA guidance.
- **No UK case found where an organisation refused an interpreter/VRS call and named GDPR specifically as the reason** (distinct from the general "security"/"cannot deal with a third party" language in Kalinova, which does not invoke GDPR by name). Confirmed absence after a dedicated search pass (Financial Ombudsman Service decisions, EHRC, UK news, tribunal records) — the Irish DPC case study is the only documented instance anywhere of an organisation using the words "GDPR and data protection concerns" to justify this refusal.
- **Special-category-data question (recording "Deaf"/"requires BSL interpreter" as health data): no dedicated ICO or NRCPD guidance found** analysing this as a distinct legal question, separate from the AIS/GDPR booking-sharing finding above. The "circular consent" framing (can a Deaf person validly consent to an interpreter's presence if the only way to give that consent is through the interpreter) has no published regulatory analysis — the ICO's own 2018 position sidesteps it entirely by stating this "is not one of consent" but of equivalent security, which is itself a data point worth citing (the regulator does not think consent is the right frame at all).

**What this changes about the domain's argument:** the "GDPR is a barrier" framing needs to be precise. It is *not* a barrier to a Deaf person using an interpreter or VRS to reach an organisation — the UK and Irish regulators are aligned that it isn't, in writing. It *is* a documented barrier to the interpreting supply chain working well (agencies not sharing booking metadata), which is a narrower but real and separately worth citing problem. An organisation that tells a Deaf customer "we can't do this because of GDPR" is, per the only regulatory guidance that exists on the question, simply wrong — which is itself a useful, quotable fact for advocacy purposes.

### Special-category classification, AIS/RADF legal basis, and the under-recording hypothesis (added 6 Sept 2026, Grok Batch 4 continued + independent verification)

A second, harder question than "can GDPR block the call" (answered above): is recording "Deaf" or "requires a BSL interpreter" itself special-category (Article 9) health data, what legal basis does the NHS's own Accessible Information Standard (AIS) machinery actually name for recording and sharing it, and is GDPR-caution a documented cause of the well-evidenced "re-explain your needs every time" problem?

**"Deaf" and "requires a BSL interpreter" are treated differently by the NHS's own guidance — this is a real, citable distinction, not a workaround.** NHS England's AIS implementation guidance (published 30 Jun 2025, updated 21 Jul 2025) draws the line explicitly: **✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified against the exact URL.** "It is these needs and how they best can be met (not the underlying disability itself) that should be recorded," and "Recording that a person is 'deaf', for example, does not explain whether they are able to read written English, if they use British Sign Language (BSL) or are a lipreader and/or hearing aid user." The guidance goes on to state plainly that disability information "is classified as special category (or sensitive) personal data and is subject to stricter requirements and should only be recorded where necessary." Source: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/accessible-information-standard-implementation-guidance/

**ICO's own definition confirms "Deaf" is health data, and that inference counts too — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified against the exact URL.** ICO, *What is special category data?* (last updated 9 Apr 2024): health data includes "any information on injury, disease, disability or disease risk," and data becomes special category through inference if an organisation either *intends to infer* a special-category fact or *treats someone differently* on the basis of an inference — an OR condition, not a narrow one. Source: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data. No ICO page or UK case law was found that decides whether a bare "BSL interpreter needed" flag (as opposed to a "Deaf" diagnosis label) is itself Article 9 data — this remains a genuine open legal question, not merely an unresearched one.

**RADF's actual named legal basis is implied consent — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified against the exact URL, verbatim.** NHS England's Reasonable Adjustment Digital Flag (RADF / DAPB4019) action checklist states: "Information recorded on the reasonable adjustment digital flag is shared under the lawful basis of implied consent and, as such, people can raise objections to their data being shared at any time, unless there is a valid reason why it should be shared." Source: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/the-reasonable-adjustment-digital-flag-action-checklist-what-you-need-to-do-to-achieve-compliance/. **Flag for the record:** this sits in tension with the AIS implementation guidance's own language that shared/integrated records should carry AIS data "with consent" — the two NHS documents do not use the same consent standard, and no reconciling NHS legal note was found. Treat this as a genuine internal inconsistency in NHS guidance, not a dossier error.

**Financial services regulators have gone on the record that GDPR is not a valid excuse for under-recording vulnerability data — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified; citation corrected.** The FCA and ICO's joint statement on vulnerability data (published 27 Mar 2026, updated 20 Jul 2026) states UK GDPR "do[es] not prevent organisations from sharing or using personal information where it is appropriate and necessary to protect individuals or provide them with the support they need." The statement exists specifically because firms were treating data protection law as a reason not to collect, record, or share this kind of data. **Correct URL (Grok's original was unverifiable/guessed): https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/corporate-documents/joint-fca-and-ico-statement-regulatory-expectations-regarding-firms** — use this one, not any fca.org.uk root-domain citation. A "Deaf / uses BSL" flag on a bank's CRM sits in the same vulnerability-data bucket this statement addresses.

**The lived-experience "have to re-explain every time" problem is real, current, and quantified — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified, stats match exactly.** RNID + SignHealth, *Still Ignored: the fight for accessible healthcare* (England, 2025): 70% of deaf/hearing-loss respondents had never been asked about their information or communication needs (Table 3); 77% said staff had never or rarely been made aware of their needs before the appointment (Table 8). Source: https://rnid.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Still-ignored-the-fight-for-accessible-healthcare-2025.pdf. **Important honesty point carried over from the previous verification pass: this report does not name GDPR as the cause.** It frames the failure as AIS non-implementation and staff training (a related 2026 RNID FOI found 92% of NHS staff in England had not completed AIS training). Do not cite this statistic as evidence that GDPR-caution specifically causes under-recording — it is evidence of the under-recording itself, attributed by the charities themselves to a different cause.

**Where GDPR genuinely is named as the reason data isn't shared: interpreter bookings, not patient records — ✅ Confirmed, independently re-verified, exact URL found.** NUBSLI's 18 June 2019 statement: LanguageLine "withhold[s] client names when confirming bookings with freelance interpreters, citing GDPR compliance." Source: https://www.nubsli.com/nub-posts/providing-a-clients-name-upon-confirmation-of-a-booking-gdpr-breach/ (this URL was not supplied by Grok and was located independently — the domain file's earlier "🟡 Lead-only pending direct verification" note on this item is now superseded: **upgrade this citation to ✅ Confirmed**, with the corrected URL above). This corroborates, rather than duplicates, the *Locked Out* booking-data-sharing finding already in this domain file — GDPR-as-excuse is a real, named, documented pattern specifically in the interpreter-booking supply chain, not in NHS patient-record flagging.

**Net honesty position for this sub-section:** the hypothesis "staff avoid writing 'Deaf' on records because of GDPR" has no documented NHS or bank policy behind it (still 🟠/unproven as a stated *cause*) but the *outcome* it would predict — needs not recorded, not shared, person re-explains every contact — is itself extremely well evidenced. Separately, GDPR-as-excuse is now confirmed as a real, named phenomenon in two adjacent domains: financial-services vulnerability data (regulators had to say "stop doing this") and interpreter-agency booking data (NUBSLI and the BSL Advisory Board both document it directly). The precise, defensible claim for advocacy use is: "GDPR is a documented, regulator-acknowledged excuse for not sharing disability/access data in financial services and interpreter logistics — there is no equivalent published finding yet for NHS patient-record flagging, where the documented cause is AIS non-implementation instead."

## Third-Party Call Refusal — Dedicated Search Pass (added 6 Sept 2026, Grok Batch 4 Prompt 3 + independent verification)

Permanent TSB* (Ireland, WRC, Jul 2022, €8,500 awarded for indirect disability discrimination after a bank refused to continue a call once a professional BSL interpreter was on the line)? A dedicated search pass was run specifically to answer this, rather than relying on the absence noted incidentally in earlier passes.

**Confirmed finding, independently re-verified via a separate search: there is no published UK Financial Ombudsman Service decision, EHRC (GB) investigation, court judgment, county-court or employment-tribunal Equality Act case, or FCA enforcement action matching this exact fact pattern** — a UK bank, insurer, or government department refusing to continue a live phone or video call specifically because a professional BSL interpreter or VRS operator was on the line, citing identity, fraud, or "we cannot deal with a third party." This absence was tested by two independent searches (Grok's dedicated pass, and a second independent verification pass covering FOS, EHRC, tribunal databases, and general UK news with different search terms) and both came back empty. **Several UK lived-experience accounts describe the same mechanics as Kalinova, but none produced a reasoned legal decision — treat as anecdote, not case law.**

## Enabling Legislation — Resolved (added 6 Sept 2026, Grok Batch 4 Prompt 4 + independent verification)

The domain's second long-standing open question — exactly which statutory clause empowers Ofcom to mandate relay services, and whether the Act itself limits any *universal* VRS mandate to text only — is now resolved. **This also directly answers the "is the everyday/any-number VRS gap a legal limit or a policy choice" question that runs through this whole domain: it is confirmed as a policy choice, not a statutory limit.**

**The enabling clause: Communications Act 2003 ss.45 and 51, specifically s.51(2)(c) — ✅ Confirmed verbatim against legislation.gov.uk, with one citation correction.** Section 45 gives Ofcom its power to set "conditions," which must be one of several defined types, including a "general condition" (authorised by ss.51, 52, 57, 58 or 64) or a "universal service condition" (authorised by s.67). Section 51(1)(a) allows a general condition "for protecting the interests of the end-users of public electronic communications services," and s.51(2)(c) — the operative clause — specifically allows conditions that "specify requirements in relation to the provision of services to disabled end-users." Both quoted subsections independently verified word-for-word on legislation.gov.uk. **Citation correction:** Grok reported s.51(2)(c) was inserted by SI 2020/1419 (the EECC/EU Exit onshoring regulations); legislation.gov.uk's own amendment annotations show it was actually inserted earlier, by **SI 2011/1210** (The Electronic Communications and Wireless Telegraphy Regulations 2011, in force 26 May 2011). SI 2020/1419 amended other parts of s.51 (subsections (2)(b), (2)(ba), (8)–(9)), not (2)(c). This is the same "correct substance, wrong citation" pattern caught twice already in this domain's GDPR research (Sections 25–27) — the legal analysis is sound, but always cite SI 2011/1210 for this specific insertion, not 2020/1419.

**Ofcom has already used s.51(2)(c) for exactly this purpose — for emergency VRS. ✅ Confirmed verbatim.** Ofcom's own *Ex post evaluation of the emergency video relay regulation* (30 May 2025) states its powers "derive from the Communications Act 2003 ... Under section 51(1)(a) ... This includes a broad power under section 51(2)(c) to set conditions specifically for services provided to disabled end-users." Confirmed independently against the exact Ofcom PDF. **This is the single most important legal fact in this domain's whole "policy choice, not legal limit" argument: if the Act could not support a VRS mandate, emergency VRS (GC C5.11–C5.12, live from 17 Jun 2022) could not exist as a General Condition. It does exist, using this exact power — so extending it to everyday, any-number VRS would use the same statutory mechanism, not require new primary legislation.**

**Why text relay sits under a different legal history, and why that history doesn't limit VRS: the *Number (UK) Ltd v Ofcom* case. ✅ Confirmed — case exists, holding matches, and it is genuinely cited in Ofcom's own 2012 statement.** CJEU Case C-16/10 (17 Feb 2011) held that a Universal Service Condition can only require the designated provider (BT) to supply a retail service to its own customers — it cannot lawfully impose a wholesale relay obligation on BT for other providers' customers. This is why Ofcom's 17 October 2012 statement revoked the BT-only Universal Service Condition 4 for text relay and moved the obligation onto all communications providers via General Condition 15 (now GC C5.8–C5.10) — a general condition under ss.45+51, not a universal service condition under s.67. Confirmed independently that this reasoning appears at the stated location (para 3.28) in Ofcom's own 2012 statement.

**The 2011–12 decision not to mandate unrestricted VRS was explicitly a cost and proportionality judgment, not a finding that the Act couldn't reach video. ✅ Confirmed verbatim, cost figures match exactly.** Ofcom's 2011 *Review of Relay Services* consultation and its 2012 follow-up cite an estimated cost of **£41.6 million/year** for a medium-demand unrestricted video relay service and **£113.4 million/year** for high demand, and conclude "a requirement to provide an unrestricted VR service would not be appropriate at this time," proposing VR "on a restricted basis" instead. Both figures and the quoted conclusion independently confirmed verbatim against the primary Ofcom consultation PDF (these figures already appeared in this domain's Key Figures table from the original research pass — this confirms them a second time via a different verification route, strengthening rather than duplicating that entry).

**The EU framework (Universal Service Directive Art. 7/23a; EECC Art. 111) is technology-neutral — it requires "equivalent access," not text-relay specifically, and the EECC's own recitals name sign-language relay as a form the concept already covers.** Not independently re-verified this pass (lower priority than the UK statutory chain above; the consolidated EU directive text is stable, low-risk material) — flagged 🟡 Lead-only pending a dedicated open of the consolidated Directive text, though Ofcom's own paraphrase of "equivalent access, not text-only" is corroborated by its own subsequent conduct (mandating an emergency VRS, not just more text relay).

**No published source states plainly "Ofcom already has the power for universal VRS and is choosing not to use it" as a formal legal holding** — Grok's own search found this only as a substantive inference from Ofcom's own language (2021/2025 statements), not as a Law Commission review, select committee finding, or academic legal commentary devoted to the point. The BSL Advisory Board's *Locked Out* report (Priority 3, 27 Nov 2025) specifies the *service* it wants (a national 24/7 VRS, any UK number, including health/emergency support) but — so far as found in the published HTML — does not specify the *legal instrument* by which government should deliver it. This is a genuine, if narrow, evidence gap: the strongest advocacy document in this domain does not itself point to s.51(2)(c) as the lever, even though Ofcom's own conduct shows that lever exists and works.

**Net position for this domain's central argument:** the "any UK number" gap is now confirmed, end to end, as a policy and funding choice rather than a legal one. Ofcom has the statutory power (s.51(2)(c), already used for emergency VRS); the EU framework it transposes does not confine the mandate to text; and the 2011–12 decision not to go further was explicitly about the estimated £41.6–113.4m annual cost and interpreter supply, not about any gap in the Communications Act. Extending General Condition C5 from emergency-only to any-number, everyday VRS would be a modification of an existing condition under the same statutory sections Ofcom already relies on — not a request for new primary legislation. This sharpens the ask for advocacy purposes: the request is "modify General Condition C5," not "change the law."

**Why ~23,000 (SLI, Jul 2023–Jun 2024) and ~20,000 (Ofcom, calendar 2024) are compatible, not contradictory:** these are genuinely different, overlapping-but-distinct 12-month windows measuring the same underlying call stream, and Ofcom's report itself notes that a later fall in *total calls received* reflects fewer non-connecting/nuisance calls, not a fall in calls that actually reached emergency services — so a lower calendar-2024 total sitting below the year-2 (mid-2023-to-mid-2024) total is expected, not anomalous.

**Recommended figures for this dossier going forward:**
- For "how many 999 BSL calls in 2024" (calendar year): **~20,000**, Ofcom, 30 May 2025 — the regulator's own published number, and the correct one to cite for any calendar-year claim.
- For the most recent full service year: **~18,000**, Sorenson/SLI *Annual Report 2024–2025*, a **22% decrease** on the ~23,000 recorded the prior service year — itself a notable, previously unflagged trend worth citing (call volume has been falling in the most recent service year, which invites its own follow-up question about why).
- **Never cite 24,000** as a 999 BSL figure.

## VRS vs. Face-to-Face Interpreter Costs — Resolved, With a Correction That Undercuts the Headline Comparison (added 6 Sept 2026, Grok Batch 4 Prompt 9 + independent verification)

This prompt filled the one gap none of the previous eight touched: what does booking a live, in-person BSL interpreter actually cost in the UK, and how does that compare to VRS? Independent verification confirmed most of the real-world rate data Grok found, caught one likely-fabricated FOI citation, and — most importantly for this domain's argument — found that Grok's core premise (that agencies price VRS lower than face-to-face) does not hold up: where rate cards actually exist, UK agencies charge the **same rate regardless of delivery mode**. The "VRS is cheaper" cost gap in Grok's arithmetic comes entirely from call-length and minimum-booking assumptions, not from any agency actually discounting VRS.

**✅ Confirmed: NUBSLI publishes regional day/half-day rate guidance, last updated April 2026 — but it is a non-binding median survey, not a rate card with an hourly figure or a stated minimum.** https://www.nubsli.com/guidance/interpreter-fees/, independently re-fetched. Day rate £225–£375 depending on region and qualification (RSLI £335–£375 in London; £245–£335 in Scotland); half-day £115–£190. No hourly rate is published — interpreters "generally charge for work in half or full day increments." The page states explicitly: *"the published figures are guidance, each professional decides their own fees."* Correct the domain's framing: this is a survey median, not a binding minimum rate, and there is no clean hourly figure to build a per-minute comparison from.

**✅ Confirmed verbatim: Bristol City Council's April 2025 Charging and Cancellation Policy (version 7) publishes real BSL figures.** https://www.bristol.gov.uk/files/documents/7905-translation-and-interpreting-charging-policy/file, independently re-fetched. BSL short duration £158.60; BSL half day £195.20; BSL full day £390.40; additional time billed hourly. (Separately, general non-BSL face-to-face interpreting has its own lower minimum fees — £48.50 community language, £145.00 court/police/probation, billed in 15-minute blocks — not to be confused with the BSL figures.) No minimum booking period is stated for BSL specifically in this document.

**🔴 Correction — the domain's central "VRS is cheaper" premise does not hold where rate cards actually exist.** Grok claimed several UK agencies (Lingo Service, Pearson BSL, Martin Fox-Roberts, Sign Solutions/ABTC) publish rate cards that separate VRS/remote pricing from face-to-face pricing, implying VRS is priced lower. Independent verification found the opposite for every checkable source:
- "Lingo Service" could not be found as a real, distinct entity — likely a hallucinated or garbled name. Drop this citation.
- Pearson BSL (https://pearsonbsl.com/how-much-does-an-interpreter-cost/, confirmed) — half-day (≤3.5hrs) £170, full day (≤7hrs) £320, +£50/hr beyond — applies to "standard interpreting assignments onsite or remote," with **no VRS/F2F price split**.
- Martin Fox-Roberts (https://martinfoxroberts.com/interpreting, confirmed) — half day £190 (min 3hrs), full day £375, evening £285 (min 3hrs) — the page states explicitly: *"These fees are applicable for both 'Face to Face' and 'Remote' working."* Same rate, either mode.
- Sign Solutions (signsolutions.uk.com/video-interpreting/ and /face-to-face-interpreting/, confirmed as real pages) publish **no pricing figures at all** on either page — purely descriptive.

**This matters for the dossier's argument, not just for citation hygiene: the real cost differential this domain can evidence is not "VRS is priced lower per hour than face-to-face" (no source supports that), but "a short VRS call avoids triggering a multi-hour minimum-booking fee that a face-to-face booking of the same duration cannot avoid."** The two are different claims, and only the second is currently supported by evidence.

**✅ Confirmed as standard UK industry practice: a 2-3 hour minimum booking fee, but not structured as "minimum plus travel on top" as separate line items.** terptree.co.uk (industry source): *"suppliers providing BSL Interpreting Services will most often charge a 3-hour minimum fee... This is standard practice in this Industry."* Corroborated by Martin Fox-Roberts' own stated 3-hour minimums, and by NUBSLI's 2018 dispute with an NHS England-quoted figure of "£40-60 per hour with a three-hour minimum call out fee plus travel expenses" (NUBSLI disputed the hourly figure as inflated, not the minimum-fee structure itself). Correction to Grok's framing: terptree's own explanation is that the 3-hour minimum exists *to compensate for travel time/scheduling gaps*, not as a fee with travel charged separately on top — the minimum and the travel cost are the same thing, not two additive charges. Separate mileage may additionally apply under some agencies' terms, but this was not independently confirmed.

**🔴 Correction — the Ashford and St Peter's NHS FT "FOI 11663" citation appears to be fabricated; the Birmingham and Solihull Mental Health NHS FT FOI citation could not be found at all.** Extensive independent searching (WhatDoTheyKnow, the trust's own FOI disclosure log) found no trace of request 11663 at Ashford and St Peter's — the trust's real, findable interpreting-related FOIs in this period are numbered 6753 (2019), 6828 (2019), and 8567 (2022); by 2024 the trust's FOI numbering had only reached roughly 9115, making "11663" implausible for this trust in the covered timeframe. **Do not cite FOI 11663 anywhere in this dossier.** No FOI request about interpreting, translation, or BSL costs could be found at all for Birmingham and Solihull Mental Health NHS FT on WhatDoTheyKnow — this citation should also be dropped as unverifiable.
- **Real substitute found and confirmed:** Ashford and St Peter's NHS FT **FOI 8567** (requested 29 Apr 2022, responded 27 May 2022) is genuine and usable: face-to-face interpreting cost the trust £203,198.77 (via one agency, Hounslow) + £22,608.58 (via a second, Woking) over two years; BSL interpreting billed at £120 (standard) / £360 (24/7); telephone interpreting (LanguageLine) £77,143.32; total interpreting spend £320,782.42 over the period. **The trust withheld its actual per-hour/per-minute rate breakdown under FOIA s.43 (commercial prejudice)** — meaning the trust itself would not disclose a VRS-vs-face-to-face unit-cost comparison even when directly asked, which is itself relevant supporting evidence for the next finding below.

**✅ Confirmed via independent search: no UK public-sector document directly compares face-to-face and VRS spend for the same population — and there is a structural reason why not.** Checked: Nottinghamshire ICB's 2022 "Review of Interpretation and Translation Services" engagement report (real, independently fetched) — explicitly **excludes BSL** from its scope entirely ("this service and piece of work didn't cover British Sign Language, as it is commissioned by the local authority") and contains no VRS/F2F figures. Inbox Translation's NHS-wide FOI survey (2019/20 data; ~£8.6m estimated BSL spend, ~13% of a ~£66m total NHS interpreting spend) found that **84% of NHS organisations could not separate spend by delivery method in their own finance systems** — direct evidence that the underlying accounting data most NHS bodies hold does not support a VRS-vs-F2F cost comparison even if someone wanted to make one. NHS England's own "Improvement framework" long-read excludes BSL from scope entirely. Grok's "not found" conclusion holds and these three sources should be added to the dossier as corroborating "the data infrastructure to make this comparison doesn't exist" evidence, not just "nobody has published it yet."

**✅ Confirmed via independent search, including a direct read of the *Locked Out* report itself: no Ofcom, government, NHS, or Deaf-sector source has ever published a figure-backed "VRS is more cost-scalable" claim.** The BSL Advisory Board's *Locked Out* report (GOV.UK, ~27 Nov 2025) states only a general technology-efficiency claim — *"Cost savings can come from better use of technology, which improves the efficiency of the booking process, saves staff time and reduces cancellations and missed appointments"* — with no VRS-vs-F2F figures attached. The report does cite Contact Scotland BSL's "3,000 to 4,000 calls per month" as an example of a working service, again with no comparative cost figures. **New figure worth adding to the dossier: the *Locked Out* report separately states the system-wide cost of excluding Deaf people from health and care access at £80–100 million/year** — this is a distinct figure from the Ofcom £41.6m–£113.4m/year *universal-VRS-service* cost estimate already in the dossier and must not be conflated with it; it measures a different thing (cost of exclusion vs. cost of provision).

### Net assessment

This batch produced the most consequential correction of the whole nine-prompt series so far — not a wrong URL, but a wrong premise. Grok's arithmetic concluding VRS is "roughly 3-7x cheaper" per contact is built on two explicitly-flagged assumptions (a 10-20 minute VRS call length; a £20 travel add-on) layered on top of a claim — that agencies price VRS lower than face-to-face — that independent verification found to be false everywhere it could be checked. The real, defensible version of this domain's cost argument is narrower and more structural: it is not that VRS is priced cheaper per hour, but that a short VRS call avoids ever triggering the 2-3 hour minimum-booking floor that UK interpreting agencies apply uniformly regardless of delivery mode — and that NHS finance systems are not even set up to measure the comparison Grok's prompt asked for, as the 84%-cannot-separate-spend finding and the Ashford & St Peter's trust's own s.43 withholding both independently demonstrate. This round also caught what looks like a genuinely fabricated FOI citation (11663) rather than a merely miscited real one — the first clear fabrication (as opposed to wrong-but-real citation) in this whole research thread — alongside a real, usable substitute (FOI 8567) found in its place. The *Locked Out* report's £80-100m/year cost-of-exclusion figure is a valuable new addition, distinct from the existing Ofcom provision-cost anchor.

## Known Gaps & Contested Figures

- **Resolved 6 Sept 2026: the 999 BSL call-volume discrepancy is fully reconciled.** See the dedicated section above. Four genuine, non-contradictory figures exist across different periods (~17,000 year 1; ~23,000 service-year 2; ~20,000 calendar 2024; ~18,000 service-year 3, a confirmed 22% decrease). The ~24,000 figure in press coverage is confirmed as an unsupported misstatement and should never be cited. No longer an open gap.
- **No confirmed UK case of a bank, DVLA, HMRC, or insurer explicitly refusing a VRS/interpreter-relayed call — now confirmed as a genuine absence via a dedicated search pass, not an unresearched gap.** See the "Third-Party Call Refusal" section above for the full search record: two independent searches (6 Sept 2026) covering FOS decisions, EHRC, UK tribunal databases and news found no UK match for the Kalinova v. Permanent TSB (Ireland, 2022) fact pattern. The closest UK outcome, McKendry v. Danske Bank (NI, 2019, £2,000 settlement), involves a family relay, not a professional interpreter — a meaningfully different mechanism, and under separate NI legislation. **Do not present this as "UK banks refuse third-party BSL calls" — cite Kalinova and the Irish DPC case only as comparative/persuasive evidence from the same DPA family, explicitly labelled as Irish, not UK, and cite McKendry only with both its caveats (family relay; Northern Ireland law) stated.**
- **The ICO's own informal 2018 GDPR guidance has not been independently read as a primary document.** Its existence, date (2 Oct 2018) and substance are corroborated via a WhatDoTheyKnow FOI trail and Ofcom's paraphrase of it on the text-relay-guide page, but the original letter to (a body writing on behalf of) the UK Council on Deafness has not been opened and quoted first-hand — flagged 🟡 Lead-only for exact wording, though the substance (GDPR does not block interpreter/relay calls) is corroborated independently by the Irish DPC's near-identical formal position.
- **Whether a bare "requires a BSL interpreter" flag is itself Article 9 special-category data, distinct from a "Deaf" diagnosis label, has no ICO guidance or UK case law resolving it.** NHS AIS guidance treats the *need* as the thing to record instead of the disability, but this doesn't settle the legal classification question outright — a need-flag can still be special category if it is used to infer or treat someone differently on the basis of health status. Genuine open legal question, not merely unresearched.
- **NHS's own two flagship data-sharing standards use two different consent models and this has not been reconciled anywhere in published guidance.** AIS implementation guidance says shared/integrated records should carry AIS data "with consent"; the RADF (DAPB4019) action checklist says the national flag is shared under "implied consent." No NHS legal note resolving this tension was found — worth flagging as an internal NHS inconsistency, not a dossier gap.
- **Resolved 6 Sept 2026: workplace IT/firewall blocking of VRS apps is a confirmed absence of UK incident reports, not merely an unresearched gap.** See the "Workplace Firewalls & Travel Failure" section above — two independent search passes found no UK employment survey, tribunal case, or news report of an employer firewall blocking a VRS app, despite both 999 BSL and SignVideo publishing detailed IT allow-list guidance (implying the risk is real enough to document). Do not confuse this with the separate, real, confirmed problem of AtW VRS-minute cuts or employers blocking meeting-platform captions — those are documented but are a different fact pattern.
- **Resolved 6 Sept 2026: VRS/999 BSL failure while travelling is a confirmed absence of UK incident reports, not merely an unresearched gap.** Ofcom's own emergency-VRS FAQ addresses both cross-border calls (connects only to UK 999 control rooms) and hotel/train Wi-Fi registration ("we cannot control how these kinds of private businesses manage their networks," but emergency VRS is zero-rated on mobile data as a fallback) — real, published edge cases — but no UK incident of an actual travel-related VRS failure was found despite a dedicated search.
- **Resolved 6 Sept 2026: the enabling clause is Communications Act 2003 s.51(2)(c)**, already used by Ofcom for emergency VRS (GC C5.11–C5.12) — see the dedicated "Enabling Legislation" section above. No longer an open gap; remaining open sub-question is only whether any published source frames extending VRS as a GC-modification ask rather than a new-legislation ask (Locked Out itself does not specify the instrument).
- **Resolved 6 Sept 2026: D A Languages and Sign Solutions are sister companies under a common holding company (Valorem Group Ltd), confirmed directly against Companies House.** See the dedicated section above. No longer an open gap. One narrow sub-claim (Sign Solutions' own 19 Dec 2022 acquisition announcement, quoted by Grok) could not be independently re-fetched (blocked by robots.txt) and remains 🟡 Lead-only, though independent secondary corroboration (Slator trade press) supports the same holding-group structure.

- US comparator only, for scale: FCC's interstate TRS Fund draws $1,479,568,862 (FY2025–26) via a per-carrier levy on interstate/international revenues — illustrating what a centrally-funded universal relay model costs at national scale in a comparable economy, without claiming this figure translates directly to a UK-scale estimate.
