RNID
Manage Closely
National Founded 1911 18m+ deaf & HoH in UK rnid.org.uk
Organisation
The UK's largest charity for deaf people and those with hearing loss, supporting over 18 million people. Operates across research, policy, campaigns, and community services. Led the landmark campaign that secured official recognition of BSL as a language of England, Scotland and Wales under the BSL Act 2022. A primary architect of national NHS accessibility policy for deaf people.
Still Ignored — April 2025 (co-authored with SignHealth)
The most significant deaf healthcare access report published in the UK. Based on responses from over 1,100 deaf and hard of hearing people. Core findings reframed as a legal and patient safety issue, not just a quality concern.
70% never asked about comms needs
67% of BSL users denied interpretation
56% don't fully understand appointments
Fewer than 1 in 5 BSL users trust the NHS
1 in 10 avoid A&E or ambulance
1 in 4 avoid seeking help for new health concerns
RNID Director of Health Crystal Rolfe: "Imagine not being able to understand a cancer diagnosis, or having to rely on a family member to tell you that you're seriously ill or even dying."
Follow-up FOI — 27 January 2026
RNID's freedom of information request to NHS trusts found 92% of NHS staff in England have not completed AIS training — the clearest evidence yet that the June 2025 AIS update has not translated into frontline practice. RNID has stated publicly that the AIS update "does too little" on enforcement. No formal DHSC or NHS England point-by-point response to Still Ignored has been published; government engagement has come via ministers meeting SignHealth and via the AIS/Single Patient Record announcements rather than a direct reply.
92% of NHS staff untrained on AIS (Jan 2026 FOI)
BSL User Population — Corrected Figures
RNID's BSL prevalence page (updated June 2026) confirms there is no single agreed number — treat any single figure with caution. The working set now used in official and advisory contexts: 22,000 use BSL as main language (Census 2021, England & Wales) · ~25,000 UK-wide main-language estimate · 87,000 deaf BSL users · 151,000 total BSL users of any kind (BDA figure, used throughout the November 2025 Locked Out report). The previously-used 73,000 figure should be considered superseded.

BSL Advisory Board — Locked Out Report
Manage Closely — leading current policy document
National · DHSC-appointed statutory board Health and Social Care Subgroup Published 27 November 2025 Board refreshed December 2025
Locked Out: Exclusion of deaf and deafblind BSL users from health and social care in the UK
Authored by Dr Joanna Atkinson for the BSL Advisory Board's Health and Social Care Subgroup, published 27 November 2025. This is the most significant national Deaf and DeafBlind healthcare policy document since Still Ignored — and the first major report to give deafblind BSL users equal billing rather than treating them as a footnote. Cites the corrected population figures used throughout this matrix: ~151,000 BSL users UK-wide (87,000 deaf), 25,000 main-language users, and 450,000+ deafblind people. Dr Atkinson is now co-chair of the refreshed Board (December 2025).
12 Priorities
User-controlled interpreter booking · full enforcement of AIS including statutory s.95 powers · a national 24/7 Video Relay Service for England and Wales · digital accessibility · mandatory recording of BSL-as-primary-language in NHS data systems · a national BSL complaints service · mandatory staff training (building directly on RNID's 92% untrained finding) · statutory interpreter guidelines via NRCPD registration (explicitly: no children used as interpreters) · structured commissioner engagement with the BSL community · reinstatement of specialist sensory impairment teams (directly relevant to the Deaf Action Edinburgh case study) · early BSL exposure for deaf children · and specialist BSL-aware care for older Deaf people.
14 Urgent Improvements for DeafBlind People — Separate Annex
The report includes a dedicated annex (18 action areas for providers generally, 14 specifically for deafblind people) — the first time a national health policy document has separated deafblind needs from generic Deaf/BSL provision at this level of detail. This annex should be treated as the primary source for any DeafBlind-specific commissioning case put to SWL ICB, given the near-total absence of published SWL-local deafblind pathway data (see Bright DeafBlind and NHS DeafBlind Pathway panels).

The UK's leading children's deaf charity with very high institutional reach. Primary focus is children and families — EHCP (Education Health and Care Plan) processes, SEND systems, school audiological support, and family healthcare navigation. Most directly relevant to any work touching young deaf people in SWL schools, GP-to-EHCP referral pathways, or healthcare information designed for families.

Population — CRIDE 2025 Update
The Consortium for Research into Deaf Education (CRIDE) 2025 UK-wide summary reports 53,225 deaf children and young people — a 2% decrease from 54,321 the previous year. Of these, 45,902 are in England. This should replace any earlier "2024 CRIDE" citation.
Kingdon Review — Children's Hearing Services, November 2025
The Kingdon Review of children's hearing services published its final report on 10 November 2025 (updated 4 December 2025). It found nearly 300 children harmed by failures in newborn hearing screening pathways. As part of the wider NHS England-into-DHSC restructure, commissioning of the Newborn Hearing Screening Programme (NHSP) is due to move toward ICBs. This directly affects how SWL boroughs will identify and refer deaf children going forward, and is a significant addition to the safeguarding and quality case for investing in deaf children's health pathways in SWL.

A separate, longer-standing workforce risk: NDCS has previously reported that 48% of Teachers of the Deaf are due to retire within 10–15 years — no newer figure has superseded this, but it remains a live risk to specialist education-health support for deaf children.

Healthwatch South West London
Keep Satisfied
SWL ICB · 6 boroughs Croydon · Kingston · Merton · Richmond · Sutton · Wandsworth swlhealthwatch.org.uk
Organisation & Role
The coordinated patient voice network for all six South West London ICB boroughs. Holds formal accountability powers over NHS commissioners and is the primary route for patient experience into SWL ICB decision-making. The Healthwatch organisations in Sutton, Merton, Wandsworth, Richmond, Kingston and Croydon operate jointly on cross-borough NHS issues — deaf healthcare access being a recent example.
DA Languages BSL Rollout — October 2024
The most significant recent deaf healthcare access improvement in SWL. Following the Healthwatch SWL AIS review, the SWL ICB commissioned a new BSL interpreter provider for all SWL GP practices. DA Languages replaced Language Line on 1 October 2024, introducing:
  • On-demand video BSL interpretation — a new feature in GP services
  • In-person BSL interpretation available
  • Translation for over 500 spoken languages
  • Training materials for all GP staff across the six boroughs
Prior to October 2024, patients reported Language Line took too long to book, was unreliable, and raised privacy concerns. Healthwatch SWL surveyed BSL users and GP staff between April and June 2025 (6–8 months after DAL went live).
Survey Results — Published, Report in Circulation 2026
The follow-up report exists and is a critical local evidence source. Headline finding: "continued frustrations" despite the provider change — the DAL rollout has not resolved the underlying access problem, only changed the supplier. Specific issues raised:
  • Appointment cancellations with little notice
  • DAL contacts the individual booker directly rather than the practice inbox — creating single points of failure
  • Patients want an online booking and confirmation platform — this aligns directly with Locked Out's national Priority 1 (user-controlled interpreter booking)
ICB data confirms 878 BSL interpreter requests were logged October 2024–June 2025 (not unique patients): Wandsworth 466, Croydon 185, Sutton 72, Merton 68, Kingston 61, Richmond 26. The Wandsworth concentration is explained by Achieving Together's Deaf care homes (Harding House, Huguenot Place) and Oak Lodge School's presence in the borough. Report recommendations cover AIS delivery, reliability monitoring, and patient-controlled booking. Source: Healthwatch Croydon / SWL BSL Report to ICB.

Croydon Hearing Resource Centre
Keep Informed
SWL ICB · Croydon Croydon Council Sensory Impairment Service RAD caseworker partnership
Organisation & Relevance
Operated by Croydon Council's Sensory Impairment Service in formal partnership with RAD, the Croydon Hearing Resource Centre is a specialist assessment and support service for deaf and hard of hearing residents. It provides: hearing aid maintenance and support, specialist equipment assessment, home alert devices for safety and independence, and liaison with NHS audiology services.

Healthcare access dimension: The Centre operates at the community-level delivery end of the AIS — it is where residents actually receive the practical equipment and support that the Accessible Information Standard is supposed to guarantee them. Croydon Council explicitly notes it provides equipment to alert deaf residents to sounds in their home environment for safety and independence. This is also the most concrete existing example in SWL of a council-NHS-charity partnership delivering on AIS obligations in practice.

Care Act Duties — Correction: No 2024 National Replacement Exists
The 2014 DHSC guidance (Deafblind people: guidance for local authorities, published 18 December 2014) remains the live national policy document — do not cite a "2024 DHSC Deafblind Code," as no such replacement has been published. The statutory basis is s.78 Care Act 2014 (adults) and s.7 LASSA 1970 (children), with the Care and Support (Assessment) Regulations 2014 (reg. 6) requiring deafblind assessments to be carried out by someone with specific training and expertise. Local authorities must also designate a Director-level senior officer with overall responsibility for deafblind services — this is a named-person requirement, equivalent to the AIS executive lead requirement described elsewhere in this matrix. Locked Out (Nov 2025) treats the 2014 duties as still current and calls for a consistent UK-wide legal definition of deafblindness.

The Funding Crisis — Verified Cost Data
Employer NICs rose to 15% and the secondary threshold fell to £5,000 from 6 April 2025 — NCVO estimates this cost the charity sector £1.4 billion in 2025/26 (the Employment Allowance rise to £10,500 helps only the smallest employers). A CFG survey (November 2025) found 77% of charity leaders reported finances worse than a year earlier; 35% had made redundancies; 51% left vacancies unfilled. VODG (disability providers) estimated £266m in extra costs for its members alone — around 6.7% of income against typical margins under 2% — and at least one deaf-service provider told VODG it would hand back contracts as unviable.

Deaf Action (Edinburgh) — full sequence: Deaf Social Worker service cut September 2024; specialist equipment service (including alerting systems) cut March 2025; Community Care Assistant Service cut November 2025; redundancies confirmed April 2026 (one deaf social worker, two equipment posts, one CCA role). Income £3.6m against spend £3.9m to 31 March 2025. CEO Philip Gerrard MBE: "Those services have been dismantled, one after another, and the result is that deaf people are now being pushed into generic systems that are not designed for BSL users." No UK Government or NHS England national response has followed — this was treated as a local Scottish integration joint board decision, which is itself instructive: there is no national backstop protecting specialist Deaf services from local budget decisions.

1. Provider Selection Regime (PSR) — with real examples
The PSR (distinct from, and sitting alongside, the wider Procurement Act 2023 which came into force 24 February 2025) is the specific regime for ICB/NHS/LA procurement of healthcare services. It allows direct award, "most suitable provider," or competitive process — integration, health inequalities, and social value are all scored criteria, which is the lawful route to justify awarding a Deaf-led DDPO an AIS/BSL support contract without full open tender.

This is not theoretical — it is already happening elsewhere: SignHealth was awarded the Bury Council Independent Mental Health Advocacy (IMHA) contract for Deaf people in 2025 (~£61,500). Cumberland Council tendered a d/Deaf and dual-sensory advice, equipment, and Care Act assessment service from 1 September 2025, specifically reserved as SME/VCSE-suitable. Norfolk Deaf Association was until 2025 an incumbent community audiology/microsuction provider (lost the 2025 re-procurement — a cautionary example that PSR wins are not permanent). West Yorkshire ICB has a published "Keep it Local" PSR social-value approach designed specifically to make VCSE awards easier — worth requesting as a template for SWL ICB.

2. Section 256 / 257, NHS Act 2006 — and the NHS model grant agreement
Still on the statute book. s.256 lets NHS England or an ICB make payments toward community services expenditure by a local authority; s.257 specifically allows those payments to be made to a voluntary organisation instead of or alongside the local authority. This is typically used for pooled or Better Care Fund-style transfers rather than as a standing Deaf grant programme — but the power exists and is lawful. NHS England also publishes a standing NHS model grant agreement (v5, 18 November 2024) specifically for ICB grants to voluntary organisations — this is a ready-made legal document, not something that needs drafting from scratch.
3. Cabinet Office VCSE Procurement Target — PPN 001
PPN 001 (13 February 2025) requires central government departments, executive agencies and NDPBs to set a three-year SME direct-spend target from 1 April 2025 and a two-year VCSE direct-spend target from 1 April 2026, achieved by the financial year ending March 2028, with annual published results. This binds central government and DHSC directly; it does not automatically bind ICBs — but it is the strongest available political benchmark to cite when asking SWL ICB to set an equivalent local VCSE spend target.
4. The SWL Reality Check — DAL, Not a DDPO, Holds the Contract
No published example of SWL ICB directly commissioning a Deaf charity or DDPO for AIS delivery was found. 5. VCSE Health and Wellbeing Alliance & ICP
The Alliance is DHSC/NHSE's national strategic partnership with VCSE infrastructure bodies on inclusion health — no evidence found that a Deaf-led organisation currently holds core membership for 2025–26; access is via a lead partner or funded project, not an open application. At place level, the SWL Integrated Care Partnership brings together the ICB, local authorities, Healthwatch, and VCSE organisations — the correct governance body to raise Deaf and DeafBlind commissioning directly. Healthwatch SWL is an ICP member and already drove the DA Languages rollout, which is a working precedent for VCSE-initiated NHS commissioning change, even if the outcome (a commercial contract, not a DDPO one) wasn't ideal.
National Grants — What Does and Doesn't Exist
No Deaf-earmarked NHS England transformation fund was found for 2025–26 (ICB allocations for 2026/27–2028/29, published 17 November 2025, are general). No dedicated DHSC Deaf health grant scheme exists. The closest new money is a £15,000 government fund (June 2026) to assess specialist deafblind interpreter capacity — workforce planning, not service delivery, and tiny relative to need. Do not build a funding strategy around a national Deaf-specific grant pot — none currently exists. The realistic routes are ICB-level PSR contracts, s.256/257 payments, and the Better Care Fund / model grant agreement mechanism.
